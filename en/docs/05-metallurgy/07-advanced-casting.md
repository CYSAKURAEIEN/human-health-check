# Advanced Casting

> **Forging can only make simple shapes, but casting can make pots, wheels, gear blanks, valves and machine-tool beds in one go — however complex the shape, if you can make a pattern, you can cast it**

## ⚠️ Safety first

**The biggest danger in casting is molten metal meeting water and exploding, and it usually happens just when people think things are "dry enough".**

- 💥 Damp moulding sand, moulds, crucibles, tools or ground all cause a steam explosion when molten metal touches them, throwing molten metal everywhere
- 💥 **Never pour on a concrete floor**: the moisture in concrete expands when heated and makes the floor burst; work over a thick layer of dry sand
- 🔥 Molten metal spilt on a foot causes extremely severe burns: wear high-topped leather boots with your trouser legs over them (do not tuck your trousers into your boots)
- 🔥 Synthetic clothing melts at high temperature and sticks to the skin: wear cotton, wool or leather
- ☠️ Brass melted too hot gives off white zinc fumes (metal fume fever); molten lead is poisonous
- ⚠️ Crucible tongs that do not grip securely and cracked crucibles are the most common causes of accidents: check them before every use

## 1. Casting basics

### 1.1 Melting and pouring temperatures of common metals

| Metal | Melting point | Approximate pouring temperature | Shrinkage (approx.) | Difficulty |
|------|------|-------------|-----------|------|
| Tin | 232°C | About 300°C | Very small | ⭐ |
| Lead | 327°C | About 350-400°C | About 1% | ⭐ (poisonous ☠️) |
| Zinc | About 420°C | About 450-480°C | About 1% | ⭐⭐ |
| Aluminium | About 660°C | About 700-750°C | About 1.3% | ⭐⭐ |
| Brass | About 900-940°C | About 1000-1100°C | About 1.5-2% | ⭐⭐⭐ |
| Bronze (10% tin) | About 1000°C | About 1100-1200°C | About 1.5% | ⭐⭐⭐ |
| Copper | 1085°C | About 1150-1200°C | About 2% | ⭐⭐⭐ |
| Grey cast iron | About 1150-1200°C | About 1300-1400°C | About 1% | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ |
| Steel | About 1450-1530°C | About 1550-1650°C | About 2% | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (practically impossible with improvised methods) |

```
Shrinkage: the proportion by which metal shrinks as it cools from liquid to room temperature
→ Make the pattern slightly larger than the finished part, by the shrinkage
  Example: an aluminium casting must be 100 mm long, so make the pattern about 101.3 mm
```

### 1.2 The basic casting process

```
1. Make a pattern (wood, wax, foam, an old part)
2. Use the pattern to make a cavity in moulding sand (moulding); put in a sand core where it must be hollow
3. Make the gating system (sprue, riser, vents)
4. Melt the metal
5. Pour
6. Once cool, open the sand mould and take out the casting
7. Cut off the sprue and riser, clean up and grind, and heat-treat if needed
```

## 2. Furnaces and crucibles

### 2.1 Crucible furnace

```
Construction:
- Shell: an iron drum, or brick-built
- Lining: refractory, about 5-8 cm thick
- Chamber diameter: about 10 cm larger than the crucible (about 5 cm all round, so the flame flows round the crucible)
- Air inlet: low in the side of the chamber, blowing in along the tangent, so the flame swirls round and wraps the crucible
- Furnace floor: a firebrick as a crucible stand, so the crucible sits above the air inlet
- Lid: made of refractory, with a vent in the middle (you can look in and add metal through it)
- Fuel: charcoal (for melting aluminium and copper), coke (better for melting copper and cast iron)
- Air supply: bellows, a skin bag, a hand-cranked or electric blower

If it is not hot enough: blow harder, put the lid on to hold the heat, use evenly sized fuel (walnut- to egg-sized),
  thicken the lining
```

For preparing refractories, see [Iron Smelting – Preparing refractories](01-iron-smelting.md#22-making-refractory-material); for air supply and controlling furnace temperature, see [Metallurgy Basics – Temperature control](00-metallurgy-basics.md#4-temperature-control).

### 2.2 Crucibles

| Crucible | Good for melting | Notes |
|------|---------|------|
| Steel crucible (thick-walled steel pipe with a bottom) | Aluminium, zinc, lead, tin | Simple and durable; not suitable for copper and copper alloys (steel oxidises badly at high temperature, and iron gets into the copper) |
| Clay-graphite crucible | Copper, brass, bronze, aluminium | The best; salvage them from scrap wherever possible |
| Home-made clay crucible | Copper, bronze | Fireclay + grog (crushed old firebrick, broken porcelain), about half each |

```
Home-made clay crucible:
1. Mix fireclay and grog, add a little water and knead into a clay; knead it thoroughly to drive out air bubbles
2. Shape it into a thick-walled cup (walls about 2-3 cm thick, the bottom thicker), with a small pouring lip at the rim
3. Dry it in the shade for at least one or two weeks, until thoroughly dry
4. Fire it slowly (first warm it on a low fire for a few hours, then raise the heat gradually until it is fired through)
5. Preheat before every use and check for cracks; never use a cracked crucible

Crucible tongs and pouring fork:
- Shape them to fit the crucible; once gripped it must not turn or slip out
- A pouring fork (a long-handled iron ring carried by two people) suits larger crucibles
- Try them empty before use: grip, move, tip
```

### 2.3 Iron-melting furnace (cupola, for awareness)

```
Melting cast iron needs about 1300-1400°C, which usually means a cupola:
- An upright cylindrical furnace with a refractory lining
- Charged from the top in layers: coke, iron, a little limestone (to form slag), layer after layer
- Strong air blast from tuyeres at the bottom
- Molten iron runs out of a tap hole at the bottom
It needs: coke, a strong blast, a fairly large scale and several people working together
→ Master casting aluminium and copper first, then think about cast iron
```

## 3. Patterns

```
Materials: wood (the most common), wax, plaster, rigid foam plastic, old parts (used directly as patterns)

Key points:
✓ Shrinkage allowance: enlarge by the metal's shrinkage (see 1.1)
✓ Draft: give vertical sides a taper of about 1-3°, so the pattern comes out of the sand without breaking the mould
✓ Machining allowance: leave a few extra mm on faces that will later be turned or filed flat
✓ Fillets: round inside corners, so the casting does not crack at sharp corners
✓ Keep wall thickness as even as possible: where thick and thin differ greatly, they cool at different rates and shrinkage holes and cracks form easily
✓ Make the pattern surface smooth, painted or waxed, so it lifts out easily
✓ Make complex patterns in two halves (a split pattern), divided along the parting line
```

## 4. Moulding sand and moulding

### 4.1 Green sand

```
Recipe (approximate):
- Clean fine sand (river sand or quartz sand, sieved)
- Clay about 5-10% (as binder)
- Water about 3-5%

Test (squeeze test):
- Squeeze a handful hard; when you let go the lump keeps the shape of your fingers and has a smooth surface → right
- Will not form a lump, falls apart as soon as you let go → not enough clay or water
- Sticks to your hand, shows wet marks → too much water (⚠️ too much water is the most dangerous; it explodes easily when you pour)
- When you break the squeezed lump, the break is clean, neither crumbling nor sticky → very good
✓ Used moulding sand can be sieved, re-wetted, mixed and used again and again
✓ Use finer "facing sand" for the layer against the pattern, for a smoother casting surface
```

### 4.2 Flask moulding (two-part moulding)

```
Flasks: two wooden or iron frames with no top or bottom (cope and drag), which can be lined up exactly (with pins or marks)

Steps:
1. Put the drag on a flat board, with one half of the pattern in the middle (flat face down)
2. Dust on a layer of parting powder (dry fine sand, talc or charcoal powder) so the sand does not stick to the pattern
3. Fill with moulding sand and ram it firm in layers with a wooden mallet (ram the corners tight, but not too hard, or gas cannot escape)
4. Strike it off level and turn it over
5. Put on the other half of the pattern (if split) and the cope, and dust with parting powder
6. Set the sprue pin and riser pin in place, fill with sand, ram firm and strike off
7. Pierce some vents in the sand with fine wire (not into the pattern)
8. Pull out the sprue pin and riser pin, separate cope and drag, and carefully lift out the pattern
9. Repair any damaged places, and cut a channel joining the sprue to the cavity
10. Blow out loose sand, close the mould, and put weights on top or clamp it (so the molten metal does not lift the cope during pouring)
```

### 4.3 Sand cores

```
When a casting must be hollow (such as a pipe or a hub with a hole), put a sand core in the cavity
- Make the core from sand plus a binder (binders: molasses, flour paste, linseed oil and so on),
  formed in a core box and baked hard
- The pattern must have "core prints" to leave seats in the mould for the core to rest on
- The core must let gas out: pierce a vent through the middle
```

### 4.4 Other kinds of mould

| Method | Description | Good for |
|------|------|------|
| Floor moulding | Moulding directly in a sand pit in the ground | Large, simple castings |
| Lost-wax casting | See [Copper Smelting – Lost-wax casting](02-copper-smelting.md#42-lost-wax-casting-precision-casting) | Fine, complex small parts |
| Lost-foam casting | Make the pattern from foam plastic (salvaged packaging foam), bury it in dry sand and pour straight in; the molten metal burns the foam away | Aluminium, copper; no need to remove a pattern; ⚠️ gives off fumes, work outdoors |
| Permanent mould | A mould made of steel or cast iron, used again and again | Ingots and simple parts of aluminium, zinc, lead, tin; preheat and smoke with soot before use |
| Stone mould | See [Copper Smelting – Stone-mould casting](02-copper-smelting.md#43-stone-mould-casting-durable) | Small parts |

## 5. The gating system

```
          Pouring basin (funnel)
                 │
       Sprue (wider at top, narrower below)
                 │
          ┌──────┴──────────┐
        Runner            Riser (above the thickest part of the casting)
          │                 │
        Ingate ─→  Cavity (casting)

Pouring basin and sprue: let the molten metal flow in smoothly; the sprue is wider at the top and narrower below, with a small well at the bottom to absorb the impact
Runner and ingate: lead the molten metal to the cavity, entering smoothly from the side at a fairly low point, not jetting straight at the sand
Riser: a column of stored metal thicker than the casting; as the casting cools and shrinks, the riser feeds it molten metal
  → Place it over the thickest part of the casting, the part that solidifies last
  → The riser must be thicker than the part it feeds, so it solidifies last
Vents: let out the air in the cavity and the gas given off by the moulding sand
```

## 6. Melting and pouring

### 6.1 Melting

```
✓ Preheat and dry the crucible and tools; the charge must be dry (see Scrap Metal Recycling and Aluminium Casting)
✓ Put in a little metal first; once it melts, add more preheated metal one piece at a time
✓ Aluminium: skim once molten; do not overheat, do not stir too much
✓ Copper and bronze: cover the surface with a layer of charcoal powder to reduce oxidation; add the tin only after the copper is fully molten
  (for proportions and steps, see Copper Smelting – Melting bronze)
✓ Brass: do not let it get too hot when adding zinc, to reduce zinc loss and zinc fumes
✓ Skim the dross off the surface before pouring
```

For melting bronze, see [Copper Smelting – Melting bronze](02-copper-smelting.md#33-melting-bronze).

### 6.2 Pouring

```
1. Stand the mould on dry sandy ground, with weights on the cope
2. When two people work together, agree on calls and movements beforehand
3. Bring the crucible lip as close to the pouring basin as possible
4. Pour continuously, quickly and steadily, keeping the pouring basin full all the time; do not stop part-way
5. Pour until molten metal appears in the riser
6. Pour leftover metal into a dry iron mould or sand pit prepared for it; do not tip it out anywhere
7. Wait for it to set: small parts about ten-odd minutes, large parts longer; let iron castings cool until dark before knocking out
```

### 6.3 Knocking out and cleaning

```
- Once cool, open the flask and knock off the moulding sand
- Cut off the sprue and riser with a saw or chisel
- File and grind off fins and burnt-on sand
- Iron castings: slow cooling or annealing lowers the hardness and makes them easier to machine
```

## 7. Cast iron

Cast iron is cheap, wear-resistant and damps vibration, and it can be cast into complex shapes. It is an important material for pots, stoves, pipe fittings, machine-tool beds and gears.

```
Grey cast iron: about 3% carbon and a fair amount of silicon; when it cools fairly slowly, the carbon separates out as graphite flakes
  → Grey fracture, can be machined, wear-resistant and vibration-damping; but brittle and cannot be forged
White cast iron: forms when it cools too fast or has too little silicon
  → Bright white fracture, extremely hard and brittle, almost impossible to machine
  → Thin walls and surfaces against a cold mould easily turn white

To get grey cast iron that can be machined:
✓ Do not make walls too thin, and do not let the mould be too cold
✓ Remelt salvaged grey-iron parts (brake drums, brake discs, engine blocks, old iron pots); their composition is about right

⚠️ Cast-iron parts cannot be hardened for use as cutting tools, and cannot be forged
⚠️ Cast-iron pots and iron stoves crack with sudden cooling or heating (pouring cold water on them when red-hot)
```

## 8. Casting defects and their causes

| Defect | What you see | Common causes | Remedy |
|------|------|---------|------|
| Gas holes | Smooth round holes inside or on the surface of the casting | Moulding sand too wet or not permeable; overheated metal absorbing gas | Less water, more vents, control the temperature |
| Shrinkage cavities | Irregular holes inside thick parts | No riser, or riser too small | Add a riser over thick parts; make wall thickness more even |
| Misruns, cold shuts | A piece of the casting is missing, or there are seams that have not fused | Metal too cold, poured too slowly, walls too thin | Raise the pouring temperature; pour fast and without stopping |
| Sand holes, sand inclusions | Grains of sand trapped in the casting | Sand not rammed firm, pouring washed away the mould, loose sand not cleaned out | Ram firm, aim the ingate away from sand walls, blow clean before closing |
| Shift | The two halves are offset | Mould not lined up when closed | Use locating pins or alignment marks |
| Burnt-on sand | A layer of sand stuck to the casting surface | Too hot, sand too coarse | Use fine facing sand, brush on a mould wash (graphite powder or talc mixed with water) |
| Cracks | The casting cracks | Uneven wall thickness, sharp corners, cooling restrained | Fillets, even walls, knock out promptly to loosen the sand |

## 9. Checklist

### Before pouring

- [ ] Sand mould neither too wet nor too dry, vents pierced, cope weighted down
- [ ] Pouring area is dry sandy ground, not concrete
- [ ] Crucible has no cracks; crucible tongs tried
- [ ] All tools and any metal to be added preheated and dried
- [ ] Protection: face shield, leather gloves, leather apron, high-topped leather boots, cotton clothing
- [ ] Dry sand ready for spills (⚠️ never water)
- [ ] Pouring route clear, bystanders well back

### After pouring

- [ ] Leftover molten metal dealt with safely
- [ ] Casting fully cooled before knocking out
- [ ] Record: metal, temperature judgement, sand condition, result and defects, to do better next time
