# Bellows and Forges

> **The heat of a fire is decided not by the fuel but by the air. An open charcoal fire reaches only seven or eight hundred degrees Celsius; with forced air it can melt copper and smelt iron — the bellows are the heart of metallurgy**

## ⚠️ Safety first

- ☠️ **Carbon monoxide**: when a furnace runs in a shed or indoors, carbon monoxide builds up and causes headache, unconsciousness and even death → ventilation is essential; best work under a half-open shelter
- 🔥 Forced air blows sparks and bits of charcoal a long way; clear away anything flammable and keep sand and water ready for putting out fires
- 🔥 The end of the blast pipe (tuyere) nearest the fire gets extremely hot and an iron pipe soon burns out; keep your hands away from the blast pipe
- ⚙️ Water-powered and machine-driven blowers have moving parts that can catch clothing and hands

For carbon monoxide poisoning, see [Home and Road Accidents – Carbon monoxide poisoning](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/07-home-and-traffic-accidents.md#carbon-monoxide-poisoning).

## 1. Why forced air matters so much

```
How fast fuel burns depends on how much oxygen it gets
- Charcoal burning naturally in air: about 700-800°C, not enough to melt copper
- Steady forced air: about 1100-1300°C, enough to smelt copper and make bloomery iron
- Strong blast + a well-insulated furnace: about 1300-1400°C or more, enough to produce molten iron

If the furnace will not get hot, nine times out of ten there is not enough air:
- Too little air
- Air not continuous (stopping and starting)
- Air not reaching where the fuel is deepest and most concentrated
- Leaks in the air pipe
```

For judging furnace temperature, see [Metallurgy Basics – Temperature control](00-metallurgy-basics.md#4-temperature-control).

## 2. Comparing blowing equipment

| Equipment | Difficulty | Air volume | Continuous? | Notes |
|------|------|------|---------|------|
| Blowpipe (blown by mouth) | ⭐ | Very small | No | Only enough to melt a tiny amount of metal or solder small parts |
| Skin bag | ⭐⭐ | Medium | Nearly, with two used in turn | The oldest way of blowing air |
| Pot bellows | ⭐⭐ | Medium | Nearly, with two used in turn | Pottery or wooden bowls covered with skin; simple to make |
| Wooden fan bellows | ⭐⭐ | Medium to large | Two used in turn | Traditional in China; hinged wooden boards opening and closing to blow air |
| Double-acting piston bellows | ⭐⭐⭐ | Large | **Continuous** | China's traditional box bellows; blows on both push and pull; the most practical |
| Water-powered blowing (*shuipai*, water-driven bellows) | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Large | Continuous | Needs no human power and can work for long periods |
| Centrifugal blower | ⭐ (salvaged) | Large | Continuous | Salvaged electric blowers, car heater fan motors |

## 3. How to make each kind of bellows

### 3.1 Skin bag

```
Material: a whole sheepskin (or other animal hide), as intact as possible
1. When skinning, make as few cuts as possible at the neck and legs, so the hide comes off as a bag
2. Tie a pipe (wood, bamboo or pottery) into the opening of one leg as the air outlet
3. Nail a wooden strip along each side of the neck (or back) opening; gripping the strips lets you open or close it
4. Tie off and sew up all other openings tightly

Use:
- Open the mouth and lift → the bag fills with air
- Close the mouth (grip the strips tight) and press down → air is squeezed out through the pipe
- Work two bags in turn and the air hardly stops
```

### 3.2 Pot bellows

```
1. Two pottery or wooden bowls (about 30-40 cm across), each with a hole low in the side for an air pipe
2. Cover the mouth of each bowl with soft leather or thick cloth, tied tight all round so it does not leak
3. Fix an upright wooden stick (or a cord) to the centre of the cover as a handle
4. Make a small hole in the cover as the air inlet (cover it with a finger when pulling up, or make a simple leather flap)

Use: lift the handle to draw in air, press down to blow; work the two bowls in turn, one in each hand
```

### 3.3 Double-acting piston bellows (most recommended)

China's traditional wooden box bellows: they blow on both the push and the pull, the air is steady and even, and one person can work them for a long time.

```
Construction (side view, schematic):

  Inlet valve          Inlet valve
   ↓                         ↓
 ┌─┬─────────────────────────┬─┐
 │ │    Piston  ═══════════════════ Rod ←→ operator
 └─┴─────────────────────────┴─┘
   └──→ Air channel along side ┘
               ↓
     Outlet valve → tuyere

1. Box: a rectangular wooden box, about 60-100 cm long, inside cross-section about 25 × 30 cm, inner walls planed smooth
2. Piston: a board the same size as the inside cross-section, edged all round with soft leather, felt or chicken feathers (feathers pointing outwards),
   so it slides but does not leak
3. Rod: passes through a hole in one end of the box and is fixed to the centre of the piston
4. Inlet valves: a square hole in each end board, with a leather flap or board nailed on the inside that can swing inwards
   → When the piston moves away from that end, the flap is sucked open and air comes in; when the piston presses towards that end, the flap is pressed shut
5. Air channel: run a channel along the side (or bottom) of the box, each end opening into the inside of one end of the box
6. Outlet valve: where the middle of the channel leads out to the tuyere, fit a small board that can swing left and right (or two one-way leather flaps);
   whichever side the air comes from pushes it to the other side, blocking backflow

How it works:
- Push the rod: air in front of the piston is compressed and blown out of the tuyere via the channel; fresh air is drawn in behind the piston
- Pull it back: the reverse; air on the other side is blown out
→ Every stroke blows, so the air is continuous

Key points:
✓ The seal round the piston matters most: too tight and it will not move, too loose and it leaks
✓ The box and channel must not leak: seal the joints with glue or clay
✓ Fit a leather sleeve at the rod hole to reduce leakage
```

### 3.4 Water-powered blowing (*shuipai*)

```
As early as the Eastern Han dynasty (1st century AD), China used water power to drive bellows for ironmaking; this was called the "shuipai"

Principle:
The waterwheel (vertical or horizontal) turns → a crank and connecting rod turn rotation into back-and-forth motion → pushing and pulling the bellows
→ As long as water flows, it can blow day and night without stopping, saving a great deal of labour

Key points:
- Needs a stream with a certain head and flow (for estimating power, see Physics – Water power)
- The connecting rod between bellows and waterwheel must be strong, and the speed right
- The furnace has to be built by the water; watch out for the flood season
```

For estimating water power, see [Physics – Water power: waterwheels and watermills](../../../docs/08-基础科学知识/02-物理.md#33-水力-水车和水磨).

### 3.5 Salvaged electric blowers

```
- Car heater (air-conditioning) blower motors: 12 V, can be run from a car battery or a small solar system
- All kinds of centrifugal fans, cooker-hood fans, hair dryers (on the cold setting only)
- Fit a damper with an adjustable opening at the air intake to control the air volume

⚠️ Keep fans with plastic housings well away from the furnace; carry the air to the tuyere in a metal pipe
⚠️ If an electric fan suddenly loses power, hot gas from the furnace may flow back up the air pipe → do not use plastic for the air pipe or tuyere
```

## 4. Tuyeres and air pipes

```
Tuyere (air nozzle): the part where the air enters the furnace, directly facing the heat
- A pottery pipe fired from fireclay is best (withstands high heat and does not burn out)
- An iron pipe soon burns through; use it only further from the fire
- Bore about 2-3 cm (small forge) to 5 cm or more (large furnace)

Position:
- Furnace: tuyere in the lower part of the furnace, angled slightly downwards, aimed at the centre of the chamber
- Forge: tuyere in the side or bottom of the hearth, aimed just below where the work sits

Preventing backflow:
With traditional skin bags and pot bellows, leave a small gap between the end of the air pipe and the tuyere (do not join them solidly),
  so that drawing in air does not suck hot gas from the furnace back into the bellows
```

## 5. The forge

### 5.1 How a forge is built

```
                Hood (optional)
                   │
       ┌───────────────────────────┐
       │  Hearth (brick or stone)  │
       │      ┌─────┐              │← Height about 70-80 cm
       │      │ Fire│← Tuyere      │  (comfortable for working standing up)
       │      │ pot │              │
       │      └─────┘              │
       └───────────────────────────┘

Firepot: a hollow in the middle of the hearth, about 20-30 cm across and about 15 cm deep
Tuyere: side blast (air in from the side; the simplest) or bottom blast (air in through the bottom of the firepot)
A bottom-blast firepot needs an ash gate in the bottom that can be opened, to clear out clinker easily
Keep alongside: the quenching trough, the fuel heap, the tool rack
```

For the sizes of a simple forge, see [Forging Simple Tools – Essential tools](03-forging-simple-tools.md#13-essential-tools).

### 5.2 Fuel

```
Charcoal:
✓ Clean, almost no sulphur, good for forge welding
✗ Burns fast, is bulky, and a lot is needed

Coal:
✓ High heat value, concentrated heat
✗ Its sulphur makes iron brittle and forge welding difficult; the clinker it forms must be cleared often
→ Best turned into coke before use (coke is also the fuel needed to melt cast iron)

Size of fuel pieces: walnut-sized, even
```

### 5.3 Managing the fire

```
✓ Keep the fire deep: bury the work in the middle of the fire, with fuel above and below
  - Near the tuyere is the oxidising zone (plenty of oxygen); work placed there scales badly on the surface
  - A little higher is the reducing zone (hottest, little oxygen); the best place to heat the work
✓ Keep the hearth clean: clear clinker regularly (clinker blocks the tuyere)
✓ When not in use, reduce the air and bank the fire with damp fuel round the outside, to keep it alight
✓ Turn the work often while heating, so it heats evenly
✓ Do not let the work "burn" in the fire (lots of sparks flying out means the steel is burning)
```

### 5.4 The anvil

```
✓ Ideal anvil: a large steel anvil, tens of times heavier than your heaviest hammer
✓ A length of rail: stand it on end (cut face upwards); it rebounds far better than lying flat
✓ Large steel blocks, thick steel parts taken from large machinery
✓ A big hard stone (in the early days)
Mounting: fix it firmly on a thick wooden stump or a brick base, with the anvil face at about the height of the operator's knuckles with arms hanging down
```

For the anvil, hammers, tongs and other tools, see [Forging Simple Tools](03-forging-simple-tools.md); for anvil material in the ruins, see [Scrap Metal Recycling and Aluminium Casting – Steel and iron](05-scrap-metal-recycling-and-aluminium-casting.md#31-steel-and-iron).

## 6. Furnace insulation and saving fuel

```
✓ Thicken the furnace walls, line them with refractory, and wrap the outside in a layer of insulation (straw-and-mud, earth mixed with ash)
✓ Put a lid on the furnace mouth to reduce heat loss
✓ Use the hot exhaust gas to preheat the air being blown in (run the air pipe along the outside of the furnace or the flue)
  → Hot blast clearly raises the furnace temperature and saves fuel (historically the hot-blast stove was a major advance in ironmaking)
✓ Use one fire for several jobs: melt, then forge, then temper on the same fire
✓ Stop work on rainy days; damp linings and fuel both keep the temperature down
```

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] Furnace in a well-ventilated place, with nothing flammable nearby
- [ ] Bellows and air pipes do not leak; valves open and close freely
- [ ] Tuyere intact, not burnt through
- [ ] Fuel dry and evenly sized
- [ ] Quenching trough and fire-fighting sand ready
- [ ] When two people work together, agree calls for blowing and for taking work out
