# Sheet and Wire

> **Buckets, chimneys and pot lids need sheet iron; fences, springs and nails need iron wire; generators and telegraphs need copper wire. Turning metal into thin sheet and fine wire is the step from "beating out tools" to "making utensils and machines"**

## ⚠️ Safety first

- 🩸 The edges of metal sheet and the ends of wire are very sharp: wear leather gloves, and clear up offcuts promptly
- 💥 Wire that snaps suddenly during drawing whips back violently: do not stand in line with the wire, and keep everyone clear of the winch when drawing with one
- ☠️ The fluxes and acids used for tinning and galvanising are corrosive, and molten tin and zinc cause burns; heating galvanised parts gives off zinc fume
- 🔥 Workpieces are very hot after annealing; even when they do not look red they may be several hundred degrees Celsius

## 1. Annealing: the key to cold working

```
Hammering, bending and stretching metal cold makes it harder and more brittle
(work hardening). If you keep working it, it cracks and breaks. At that point anneal it
to make it soft again:

Copper, brass: heat to dark red; let it cool in air, or quench it straight into water
  (unlike steel, copper does not harden when quenched, and quenching conveniently
  knocks off the scale)
Iron, mild steel: heat to cherry red (about 780-850°C), bury in furnace ash and let it cool slowly
Aluminium: needs a much lower temperature (about 300-400°C) and must not be heated to red
  (it would melt);
  a rough-and-ready method: first rub soap on the aluminium or draw on it with a marker pen,
  then heat until the soap turns black or the mark disappears

✓ The rule: work it a few times → anneal → work it a few more times → anneal again
✓ After annealing, the scale can be removed by soaking in dilute acid or vinegar
  and brushing it off (pickling)
```

For the theory of work hardening, see [Engineering mechanics and materials – Why metal can be forged](../../../docs/09-进阶科学与工程/01-工程力学与材料.md#101-金属为什么能锻打).

## 2. Sheet

### 2.1 Getting metal sheet

```
Most realistic: salvage it from ruins
- Car bodies, appliance casings (mild steel sheet)
- Sheet-iron roofs, sheet-iron drums (often galvanised)
- Aluminium sheet, aluminium sections, copper sheet

Forging into sheet:
- Heat an iron bar or block and hammer it out repeatedly in all directions,
  so it gradually gets thinner and wider
- Very hard work, and difficult to get an even thickness; only suits small, fairly thick sheets

Rolling:
- Two hardened steel rolls, linked by gears so they turn in opposite directions;
  metal squeezed between them is pressed thinner
- Thin it only a little each pass, gradually closing the gap between the rolls;
  cold-rolled copper and aluminium need annealing many times
- A small hand-cranked rolling mill can be built from salvaged shafts, gears and bearings
```

### 2.2 Cutting and bending

```
Cutting: cut thin sheet with tin snips (hand shears); cut thick sheet with a cold chisel
  over the edge of the anvil, or saw it
Bending:
- Clamp the sheet between two straight pieces of hardwood or angle iron and tap it over
  along the edge with a mallet → a straight fold
- Hammer it over a round bar, a round log or the horn of the anvil → bent into a cylinder or curve
- Hammering sheet iron with a wooden mallet or rawhide mallet is less likely to leave hammer marks
Wired or rolled edge: roll the edge of the sheet over once; this both stiffens it
  and stops it cutting hands
```

### 2.3 Patterns: draw the shape on the flat sheet first

```
Cylinder (bucket body, chimney pipe):
  The pattern is a rectangle: length = π × diameter (plus allowance for the seam), width = height

Cone (funnel, bucket body wider at the bottom):
  Slant height L = √(r² + h²)  (r is the base radius, h is the height of the cone)
  The pattern is a sector of radius L, with angle = 360° × r ÷ L

Example: a funnel (the cone part) 20 cm across the mouth (r = 10 cm) and 12 cm high
  L = √(10² + 12²) ≈ 15.6 cm
  Angle = 360° × 10 ÷ 15.6 ≈ 231°
  → On the sheet draw a circle of radius 15.6 cm, cut out a 231° sector, roll it up and join the seam
```

For the mathematics, see [Mathematics – Geometry](../../../docs/08-基础科学知识/01-数学.md#四几何).

### 2.4 Seams

```
- Folded (grooved) seam: bend the edges of two sheets so they hook into each other,
  then flatten (see Welding and joining – folded seams in sheet iron)
- Riveting: for places that take heavier loads
- Soft soldering: after folding or lapping, seal the seam with tin so it does not leak
```

For how to make seams, see [Welding and joining](../../../docs/05-冶金技术/06-焊接与连接.md).

### 2.5 Raising: hammering flat sheet into bowls and basins

```
1. Lay an annealed round copper or iron disc over a hollow (a pit carved in a wooden
   block, or a sandbag)
2. With a round-faced hammer, hammer round and round from the outer edge towards the centre;
   the disc gradually dishes down
3. When it hardens, anneal it and carry on
4. Finally put it over a round-headed iron stake and lightly hammer the surface smooth
   from the inside outwards (planishing)
→ Copper basins, copper kettles and iron woks can all be made this way;
  copper is easiest, then mild steel
```

## 3. Tinplate

Iron sheet with a coat of tin does not rust easily. It can be made into kettles, lunch boxes and lampshades, and is easy to solder.

```
1. Clean: scour the iron sheet bright with sand or sandpaper, or soak it in dilute acid
   (vinegar will do) to remove rust and scale; rinse in clean water
2. Apply flux: zinc chloride solution (see Welding and joining – soft soldering)
3. Dip in tin: melt tin in an iron pot (a little above 232°C), sprinkle a little rosin
   on the surface or cover it with a layer of oil, dip the iron sheet in,
   hold it there briefly, and take it out
4. While it is hot, wipe off the excess tin evenly with sacking or a soft cloth
5. Wash off the remaining flux with clean water

Small parts can also be done this way: heat the iron part, apply flux, press a stick of tin
on the surface to melt it, and wipe it even with a cloth
⚠️ For anything used for food or drink, use pure tin only, never lead-containing solder
```

For where to get tin, see [Other metals – Tin](10-other-metals.md#2-tin).

## 4. Wire

### 4.1 How wire drawing works

```
Sharpen one end of a metal rod, push it through a hole slightly smaller than the rod,
and pull it hard through from the other side: it becomes thinner and longer.
Pull it again and again through a series of holes, from large to small,
and you get wire as fine as you need.

Each pass reduces the cross-sectional area by about 10-20% (more than that and it snaps)
After a few passes the wire hardens; anneal it before carrying on
```

### 4.2 The draw plate

```
Material: high-carbon steel (salvaged files and leaf springs are best), about 1-2 cm thick

Making it:
1. Anneal the steel plate first to soften it
2. Drill a row of holes from large to small
3. Open each hole out from the back into a cone (wide at the entry, narrow at the exit);
   the diameter at the exit is the diameter of wire from that hole
   Do not make the difference between neighbouring holes too large
   (cross-sectional area reduced by about 10-20%, i.e. diameter reduced by about 5-10%)
4. Smooth the walls of the holes with a tapered file or grinding rod
5. Harden and temper so the hole walls are hard and wear-resistant

The smoother the hole walls, the cleaner the wire and the less likely it is to break
```

For heat treatment, see [Metalworking](../../../docs/05-冶金技术/04-金属加工技术.md).

### 4.3 Drawing steps

```
1. Prepare the wire blank: forge or cut a round rod of even thickness, and anneal it
2. File or hammer one end to a point that will go through the entry of the hole
3. Apply lubricant: soap, wax, beef or mutton fat
4. Fix the draw plate in a bench vice or to a sturdy wooden post
5. Grip the point that comes through with pliers and pull hard; for thick wire,
   pull with a winch or lever
6. Move to the next smaller hole and repeat
7. After a few passes the wire becomes hard and brittle; anneal it and carry on
8. Iron wire forms scale after annealing; pickle it off before drawing again

Copper wire is easiest to draw, aluminium next, and iron wire hardest
```

### 4.4 Electrical wire

```
Generators, motors, telegraphs and electric lights all need copper wire:
✓ Keep salvaged copper wire wherever possible (insulated enamelled wire is extremely
  valuable); do not melt it down
✓ Copper wire you draw yourself must be insulated:
  - Wind cotton or silk thread tightly round it, then dip it in varnish or wax
    (the historical method)
  - For bare overhead wires, use ceramic or dry wood as insulators
✓ Choose wire size according to the current (see Electrical engineering and electronics –
  wiring and protection)
```

For choosing wire, see [Electrical engineering and electronics – Wiring and protection](../../../docs/09-进阶科学与工程/02-电气工程与电子学.md#62-导线和保护).

### 4.5 Uses of wire

```
- Iron wire: binding, fences, wire netting, nails, rivet blanks
- Springs: wind spring-steel wire round a round bar, then harden and temper it
- Copper wire: conductors, coils
- Weaving: sieves, filter mesh, baskets
- Chain: bend iron wire into rings and forge-weld or rivet them shut
  (see Forging simple tools – iron rings and chain)
```

For making chain, see [Forging simple tools – Iron rings and chain](../../../docs/05-冶金技术/03-简易工具锻造.md#62-铁环链条).

## 5. Tube

```
- Most realistic: salvage water pipes, steel tube, copper tube
- Rolled tube: roll iron or copper sheet into a tube and seal the seam by brazing or forge welding
- Drilling: short, thick tubes can be made by drilling out a solid bar
- Bending tube: fill the tube with dry sand, plug both ends, heat it and then bend it;
  the tube is less likely to kink
```

---

## 6. Checklist

- [ ] Anneal before working, and anneal again promptly when it hardens during work
- [ ] Wear leather gloves to handle sheet and wire ends
- [ ] Do not stand in line with the wire when drawing
- [ ] Use pure tin for tinning, and wash off all flux residue
- [ ] Keep salvaged copper wire and enamelled wire separately; do not melt it down casually
