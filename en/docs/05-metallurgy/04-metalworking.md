# Metalworking

> **From adequate to excellent - heat treatment and finishing**

## 📖 Introduction

**The same iron, treated differently, performs completely differently!**

This chapter teaches you how to multiply the performance of metal goods through heat treatment, surface treatment and similar techniques. Master them and your tools will far outclass crude, roughly made products.

**Core techniques:**
- 🔥 Quenching - hardening
- 🌡️ Tempering - toughening
- ♨️ Annealing - softening
- 🔄 Normalising - making the structure uniform
- 🛡️ Case hardening (carburising) - surface hardening

This chapter covers only heat treatment and surface treatment. For forging to shape see [Forging Simple Tools](03-forging-simple-tools.md); for filing, drilling and grinding see [Bench Work and Machining](09-bench-work-and-machining.md); for annealing copper, aluminium and other non-ferrous metals see [Sheet and Wire - Annealing](11-sheet-and-wire.md#1-annealing-the-key-to-cold-working); for why quenching makes steel hard (the phase-change principle) see [Engineering Mechanics and Materials - Principles of heat treating steel](../../../docs/09-进阶科学与工程/01-工程力学与材料.md#103-钢的热处理原理).

## ⚠️ Safety first

- 🔥 **Quenching oil fires**: when red-hot work goes into oil, the oil surface flares up. Keep the oil in a metal drum with a lid, with the lid and dry sand beside it; if it catches fire, put the lid on to smother it. **Never throw water on it**
- 🔥 **Burns and steam**: during quenching the water boils violently and throws out steam and hot water; wear long sleeves, a leather apron and leather gloves, and do not put your face directly over the quench tank. Iron that already looks "not red" is still several hundred degrees; always handle it with tongs
- ☠️ **Carbon monoxide**: carburising boxes and charcoal fires indoors build up carbon monoxide; work under a ventilated shelter. When you open a carburising box, flames shoot out; stand to the side
- 👁️ **Grinding sparks**: wear goggles for wheel grinding and spark testing, and stand to the side of the wheel
- ☠️ **Galvanised parts** (wire, water pipes and screws with a white coating): do not heat and quench them directly; zinc fumes cause metal fume fever. First soak them in vinegar for a day or two to dissolve the coating, or grind it off outdoors, upwind
- ☠️ **Do not use**: molten lead baths, cyanide salt baths (used in factories for "liquid carburising" and "salt baths"; highly toxic), nitrate baths (burn and explode on contact with charcoal or oil), asbestos products

For treating burns see [Wound and Injury Care - Scalds and burns](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/02-wounds-and-injuries.md#scalds-and-burns); for carbon monoxide poisoning see [Home and Traffic Accidents - Carbon monoxide poisoning](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/07-home-and-traffic-accidents.md#carbon-monoxide-poisoning).

## 1. Heat treatment basics

### 1.1 What is heat treatment?

**Heat treatment = heating + holding (soaking) + cooling**

**Purpose:** change the internal structure of the metal to get the properties you need

**Key factors:**
- Heating temperature
- Holding time
- Cooling rate

### 1.2 Phase changes in steel (simplified)

```
Room temperature: ferrite (soft) + cementite (hard)
  ↓ heat to the critical temperature (about 727°C)
High temperature: austenite (plastic)
  ↓
  ├→ fast cooling (quenching)
  │   → martensite (extremely hard, brittle)
  │
  ├→ slow cooling (annealing)
  │   → ferrite + pearlite (soft, tough)
  │
  └→ medium cooling (normalising)
      → ferrite + pearlite (finer, good strength)
```

**Key points:**
- **The cooling rate decides the properties!**
- Fast cooling → hard
- Slow cooling → soft

### 1.3 Judging temperature (without a thermometer)

There are two sets of colours for judging temperature; do not mix them up:

- **Fire colours**: the colour steel glows by itself above 500°C; used for quenching, normalising, annealing and forging
- **Temper colours**: the colour of the oxide film on a polished steel surface at 200-350°C (the steel itself does not glow); used only for tempering

**Fire colour table (observe in the shade):**

| Temperature (approx.) | Fire colour | Use |
|------|------|------|
| 500-550°C | Dark red (just visible in the dark) | Too low; not suitable for anything |
| 650-700°C | Blood red | Stress-relief annealing |
| About 770°C | Dull cherry red; **a magnet just stops sticking** | Lower limit of quenching temperature |
| 780-850°C | Cherry red | **Quenching, normalising and annealing temperature** |
| 850-950°C | Bright red (bright cherry red) | Forging, carburising |
| 1000-1100°C | Orange-yellow | Upper limit for forging; steel starts to overheat |
| Above 1200°C | Yellow to yellow-white, throwing sparks | High-carbon steel is burnt and ruined; only wrought iron and low-carbon steel are heavily forged and forge-welded here |

**Temper colour table (for the procedure see [3. Tempering](#3-tempering)):**

| Temperature (approx.) | Temper colour | Typical tools |
|------|------|------|
| 200-220°C | Pale straw | Files, scrapers, razors |
| 230-240°C | Dark straw/gold | Kitchen knives, plane irons, drill bits |
| 250-260°C | Brown | Axes, chisels |
| 270-280°C | Purple | Scissors, punches |
| 290-300°C | Dark blue | Saw blades, screwdrivers |
| 310-350°C | Light blue-grey | Springs |

```
Key points for reading fire colours:
1. Look in the shade or with your back to the light. In sunlight the colours look darker,
   and you will unconsciously overheat the steel
2. Look in the same place and the same light every time; that is how you build experience
3. Check with a magnet: steel loses its magnetism above about 770°C.
   Touch the work with a magnet (you can tie it to a wire); when it just stops sticking,
   heat a moment longer (the colour a little brighter), and that is the quenching temperature
4. The magnet works only for steel; copper and aluminium never attract a magnet anyway
5. People perceive colour differently; the temperatures in the table are only approximate.
   Calibrate your eyes by quenching test pieces and testing hardness with a file
```

**Rhymes to remember:**
- Quenching: cherry red into water
- Tempering: straw yellow, cool in air

## 2. Quenching

### 2.1 What is quenching?

**Quenching = heating to red + rapid cooling (water or oil)**

**Purpose:** harden the metal surface

**Suitable for:** steel (not for pure iron or wrought iron!)

### 2.2 Quenching procedure

**Steps:**

**1. Heating:**
```
Heat to quenching temperature:
- Carbon steel: 780-850°C (cherry red)
- Judging the colour: more accurate when observed in a dark room
- Magnet check: heat a little more after it just stops sticking

Heating points:
✓ Heat slowly and evenly; do not let the thin edge go red while the thick back is still dark;
  you can move the edge to the outer edge of the fire so the thick part heats first
✓ Do not press the work directly in front of the tuyere (hottest there, with the most oxygen)
✗ Do not leave it soaking in the fire: the longer it stays at high temperature, the more carbon burns out of the surface (decarburisation),
  and after quenching the surface layer is soft; you must grind off 0.2-0.5 mm to reach hard steel
✗ Do not heat it above bright red: the grain coarsens, and after quenching it is brittle and cracks easily
```

**2. Holding:**
```
Keep it at red heat:
- Small pieces: 30 seconds - 2 minutes
- Large pieces: 5-10 minutes
- Purpose: heat evenly right through
```

**3. Rapid cooling:**
```
Method A: water quench (fastest)
  - Plunge straight down into the water
  - Stir quickly (even cooling)
  - Cool until it no longer glows

Method B: oil quench (slower)
  - Cools slowly, less likely to crack
  - For complex shapes and large pieces

Method C: two-liquid quench (water then oil)
  - Into water first, cool quickly for a few seconds
  - When the edge no longer glows but is not yet cold through, move it into oil at once
  - Let it cool completely in the oil
  - Gives hardness while reducing the risk of cracking

Amount and temperature of quenchant:
- At least 10 times the weight of the work; quenching a kitchen knife takes about 10 L of water or 4-5 L of oil;
  the container must be deep enough for the whole piece to go in vertically
- Water: use water at room temperature (about 20°C), not ice-cold freshly drawn water;
  adding 5-10% salt to make brine cools faster and more evenly (salt reduces the steam film on the surface of the work),
  but it is also more likely to crack the work; use it only for simple shapes
- Oil: first preheat the oil to about 50°C with a piece of red-hot scrap iron (warm enough to dip a finger in briefly);
  cold oil is thick and actually cools more slowly, so the work easily fails to harden

Going into the liquid:
1. Grip the thick end firmly with tongs, edge down, and plunge it in vertically, quickly and completely
2. In the liquid, move it up and down along the line of the edge (like a cutting stroke); do not swing it side to side;
   swinging side to side cools the two faces differently and bends the blade
3. In a water quench, after about 3-5 seconds, when it no longer boils violently and no longer glows,
   thin pieces can be lifted out; thick pieces stay in the liquid until cooled below about 100°C
4. Take it out when it is just cool enough to hold in your hand (about 50-80°C) and temper at once;
   do not let it cool completely and leave it overnight (internal stress can make it crack by itself)
```

**4. Checking:**
```
Signs of a successful quench:
- The surface is bluish-grey
- Very hard (a file skids)
- A clear ringing sound when struck

Signs of a failed quench:
- A new file bites in → not hardened (not hot enough, cooled too slowly, or not steel)
- The surface layer files, but the file skids once a layer is removed → surface decarburised; just grind it off
- Hair-fine lines (wet it and wipe dry; water seeps out along the cracks as lines) → quench cracks;
  a tool that takes load can no longer be used
```

For the detailed file hardness test see [8.1 Hardness testing](#81-hardness-testing). A diagram of the whole process from heating and quenching to tempering is at the end of [3.2 Tempering procedure](#32-tempering-procedure).

### 2.3 Choosing a quenchant

| Quenchant | Cooling rate | Advantages | Disadvantages | Use |
|--------|----------|------|------|------|
| **Cold water** | Very fast | Simple and easy to get, high hardness | Cracks easily | Simple shapes, small pieces |
| **Brine** | Fastest | Highest hardness | Most likely to crack | Special needs |
| **Warm water (30-60°C)** | Fast | Fewer cracks than cold water | Water must be heated | Medium pieces |
| **Engine oil/vegetable oil** | Slow | Less likely to crack | Smoke, fire risk | Complex shapes |
| **Animal fat** | Slow | Commonly used in ancient times | Smells | Substitute for oil |

```
Choosing the quenchant by steel (for sources of salvaged steel see the chapter on scrap metal recycling):
- Medium-carbon steel with about 0.3-0.6% carbon (car axle shafts, some farm tools): water or brine
- About 0.6-0.8% carbon (rails, leaf springs, spring steel): try oil first; if it does not harden, use water
- High-carbon steel above about 0.8% carbon, and alloy steels (files, bearings):
  use oil; these steels crack very easily in water
- Low-carbon steel, rebar, wrought iron (below about 0.3% carbon): will not harden however you quench them; carburise first

If you do not know what steel it is: cut off a small test piece, oil-quench it first and test with a file;
if the file bites, try a water quench. Break the test piece and look at the fracture (see 8.1)
✓ Used engine oil also works, but it is smoky and toxic; work outdoors in a ventilated place
✗ Do not use petrol, diesel, alcohol or other low-flash-point liquids; they catch fire the moment they touch red-hot iron
```

For the types and uses of salvaged steel see [Scrap Metal Recycling and Aluminium Casting](05-scrap-metal-recycling-and-aluminium-casting.md).

### 2.4 Selective quenching (partial quenching)

**Why?**
- The edge must be hard (to stay sharp)
- The back must be tough (so it does not break)

**Methods:**

**Method A: clay masking**
```
1. Coat the part you do not want hardened (the back of the blade) with clay
2. Thickness 5-10 mm
3. Heat the whole piece
4. Quench in water
5. The clay-protected part cools slowly and does not harden
```

**Method B: local heating**
```
1. Heat only the edge to red heat
2. Keep the back dark
3. Into the water, edge first
4. Only the edge hardens
```

**Method C: wiping with water**
```
1. Heat the whole piece
2. Wipe the edge quickly with a wet cloth
3. The edge cools fast and hardens
4. The back cools slowly and stays tough
```

### 2.5 Common quenching problems

**Problem 1: cracks after quenching**

**Causes:**
- Cooled too fast
- Too hot
- Internal stresses or existing cracks

**Prevention:**
- Quench in oil instead of water
- Control the temperature accurately
- Use good-quality material

**Problem 2: does not harden**

**Causes:**
- The material is not steel (it is wrought iron)
- Not hot enough
- Cooled too slowly

**Solutions:**
- Make sure it is steel
- Raise the temperature to cherry red
- Cool quickly

**Problem 3: warps after quenching**

**Causes:**
- Uneven cooling
- Asymmetrical shape

**Solutions:**
- Straight down, not at an angle
- Stir quickly
- Hold it in a clamp or jig

## 3. Tempering

### 3.1 Why must you temper?

**Steel after quenching:**
- ✓ Very hard
- ✗ Very brittle
- ✗ Shatters if dropped!

**Purpose of tempering:**
- Reduce brittleness
- Keep a suitable hardness
- Get a state that is both hard and tough

**Rule: always temper after quenching! Otherwise the tool will shatter easily!**

### 3.2 Tempering procedure

**Steps:**

**1. Cleaning the surface:**
- Polish the quenched piece with sandpaper or a whetstone
- Remove the scale
- Expose a bright metal surface (important! for watching the colours)

**2. Heating:**
```
Method A: tempering in the forge
  - Heat over a low fire
  - Watch the surface colour change

Method B: open-flame tempering
  - Charcoal or gas flame
  - Keep moving it, heat evenly

Method C: hot-plate/hot-sand tempering (even; recommended for small pieces)
  - Heat a thick iron plate or a tray of dry sand over the fire
  - Lay the work on it, bright face up, turning it constantly
  - The colours change slowly and are easy to control
  - ✗ Do not use a molten lead bath (lead vapour and lead dust are toxic)

Method D: running the colours (knives, chisels and other long tools)
  - Use a red-hot thick iron bar or a flame to heat only the back of the blade or the tail of the chisel
  - Heat travels towards the edge, and the colours "run" from the back to the edge like ripples:
    yellow → brown → purple → blue, one after another
  - When the target colour appears at the edge, dip the edge in water at once to stop it
    (by then the back is already blue and tougher; the edge is hard)
  - If the colours run too fast, move the heat source away to slow them down

Method E: stove/oven tempering (most accurate; suits batches)
  - A household oven or a small brick-built oven, with a thermometer that reads to 300°C
  - 200-230°C, hold for 1 hour, take out and cool in air, then repeat once
  - The long time makes inside and outside the same; more reliable than reading colours
```

**3. Watching the colours (key!):**
```
Oxide colours appear on the surface of the quenched piece:

Pale straw (200-220°C) → files, scrapers, razors
Dark straw/gold (230-240°C) → kitchen knives, plane irons, drill bits
Brown (250-260°C) → axes, chisels
Purple (270-280°C) → scissors, punches
Dark blue (290-300°C) → saw blades, screwdrivers
Light blue-grey (310-350°C) → springs
```

**4. Cooling:**
- Remove from the heat as soon as the target colour is reached
- Usually cool in air or oil
- When running the colours, you can dip in water to stop the colour; the tempering temperature is far below the quenching temperature,
  so going into water now will not harden it again, it only stops the tempering
- Colour gone too far (you wanted yellow and got blue): the only fix is to quench and temper again; you cannot "go back"

![Quenching and tempering: heat to about 780-850°C (cherry red), a little hotter after the magnet just stops sticking → grip the thick end, edge down, into the liquid vertically, move up and down → take out when just cool enough to hold and temper at once; choosing a quenchant; temper colours from pale straw 200-220°C to light blue-grey 310-350°C and typical tools; running the colours](../../images/quench-temper.svg)

*Figure: Quenching steps are in 2.2, quenchants in 2.3. Temper colours and typical tools follow the temper colour table in [1.3 Judging temperature](#13-judging-temperature-without-a-thermometer); all temperatures are approximate*

### 3.3 Tempering temperatures for different tools

| Tool | Tempering temperature | Temper colour | Required properties |
|------|----------|----------|----------|
| Files, scrapers, razors | 200-220°C | Pale straw | Very hard, slightly brittle |
| Kitchen knives, plane irons, drill bits | 230-240°C | Dark straw/gold | Hard, some toughness |
| Axes, chisels | 250-260°C | Brown | Both hard and tough |
| Scissors, punches | 270-280°C | Purple | Both hard and tough, impact-resistant |
| Saw blades, screwdrivers | 290-300°C | Dark blue | Tough, can bend and spring back |
| Springs | 310-350°C | Light blue-grey | Very tough, moderate hardness |

```
Tempering time and number of times:
- Tempering by colour: stop when the colour arrives; the time is short and only the region near the surface is tempered;
  in thick pieces (over 10 mm) the inside may not have reached temperature when the colour arrives → heat slowly,
  and after the colour arrives leave it in hot sand for another 10-15 minutes, or temper twice
- Oven tempering: hold about 1 hour per 25 mm of thickness, at least 1 hour, and do it twice
- Temper colours are accurate only on a freshly polished bright surface; oil and scale distort the colours

Judging after tempering:
✓ A file just starts to catch slightly at the edge (a little softer than after quenching)
✓ The edge chops hardwood and whittles bone without chipping or rolling → right
✗ Chipping, broken teeth → not tempered enough; temper again to the next colour up
✗ Rolled edge, blunts quickly → over-tempered; quench and temper again
✓ Different parts need different properties (hard edge, tough back): run the colours to do it in one go
```

## 4. Annealing

### 4.1 What is annealing?

**Annealing = heating + extremely slow cooling**

**Purpose:**
- Soften the metal
- Relieve internal stresses
- Make it easier to work

### 4.2 Annealing procedure

**Steps:**

**1. Heating:**
- Heat to 700-800°C (blood red to cherry red)
- Hold for 30 minutes - 2 hours
- For full softening (for filing, drilling, sawing), heat to cherry red, until a magnet no longer sticks
- Holding time by thickness: about 15-20 minutes per 10 mm; thin pieces just need heating through

**2. Slow cooling:**
```
Method A: furnace cooling
  - Leave it in the furnace after the fire is out
  - Let it cool naturally with the furnace
  - 12-24 hours

Method B: burying in ash/sand
  - Take it out and bury it in dry ash/dry sand
  - Insulated, slow cooling

Method C: wrapping in insulating material
  - Plant ash, dry furnace slag, vermiculite or perlite all work
  - Dry the ash beforehand and preheat it (keep it by the forge); cold, damp ash makes the work cool too fast
  - Bury it at least 10 cm deep and cover the top
  - ✗ Do not use old asbestos cloth or asbestos board (inhaled asbestos fibres cause cancer)

Checking it cools slowly enough: dig it out after 1 hour; it should still be too hot to touch;
if you can already pick it up by hand, it cooled too fast and is not fully softened
```

**3. Cooling to room temperature:**
- Full cooling takes 24 hours+

### 4.3 Uses of annealing

**1. Annealing before forging:**
- The metal is too hard to forge easily
- Annealing softens it so it is easier to work

**2. Stress-relief annealing:**
- Welding and working leave internal stresses
- Annealing removes them and prevents warping and cracking

**3. Correcting quenching mistakes:**
- Not happy with the quench
- Anneal and start again

**4. Reworking salvaged steel:**
- Files, bearings and leaf springs are all hardened to begin with, and are very hard to saw, drill or forge directly
- Anneal first, then work them, and quench and temper again when finished

```
Temperatures for each kind of annealing (carbon steel):
- Full annealing (completely soft): cherry red (about 780-850°C), furnace cooling or burying in ash
- Stress-relief annealing (after welding, cold bending, heavy forging): below blood red (about 550-650°C),
  hold, then cool in air or bury in ash; does not change hardness, only relieves stress to prevent warping and cracking
- Annealing partway through cold forging: heat to red and bury in ash

Judging successful annealing:
✓ A new file bites in easily and the filings curl
✓ A hacksaw cuts it and a drill goes in
✗ The file still skids → not hot enough or cooled too fast; do it again

Copper, brass and aluminium are annealed completely differently from steel (copper can be plunged straight into water);
do not apply this section to them; see the chapter on sheet and wire
```

For annealing non-ferrous metals see [Sheet and Wire - Annealing](11-sheet-and-wire.md#1-annealing-the-key-to-cold-working); for treatment after welding see [Welding and Joining](06-welding-and-joining.md).

## 5. Normalising

### 5.1 What is normalising?

**Normalising = heating + cooling in air**

**Difference from annealing:**
- Annealing: slow cooling (furnace cooling)
- Normalising: cooling in air (natural cooling)

**Purpose:**
- Refine the grain
- Make the structure uniform
- Improve strength and toughness

### 5.2 Normalising procedure

**Steps:**

1. **Heating:** 780-850°C (cherry red)
2. **Holding:** 10-30 minutes
3. **Take out and cool in air:** let it cool naturally in air

**Uses:**
- Normalising after forging (improves the structure)
- Normalising after rough machining (makes finishing easier)

```
The blacksmith's usual "three normalising heats" (after forging to shape, before quenching):
1. First: heat to bright red (about 850-900°C), take out and cool in air in a dry place until it no longer glows
2. Second: heat to cherry red (about 800°C), cool in air until it no longer glows
3. Third: heat until it just loses its magnetism (about 770°C), cool in air
   Each time the temperature is lower, and each time the grain is finer

Requirements for air cooling:
✓ Put it on dry bricks or an iron plate, or hang it up, with air moving freely round it
✗ Do not put it on damp ground or a concrete floor, and do not put it in a draught (cools too fast and unevenly)
✗ Do not lay thin pieces flat on a cold anvil (one side cools fast and it bends)

When to normalise:
✓ The forging temperature went up and down, or it was reheated too many times, and the structure is coarse
✓ Before quenching, so the quench is more even and less likely to crack or warp
✓ When you do not know how the steel was treated before (salvaged stock)
✓ A bright, coarse-grained fracture (a sign of overheating) → normalising can rescue it
```

For how to read a fracture see [8.1 Hardness testing](#81-hardness-testing).

## 6. Case hardening

### 6.1 What is case hardening?

**Case hardening (carburising) = making low-carbon iron absorb carbon so its surface turns into steel**

**Principle:**
- At high temperature, carbon atoms diffuse into the surface of the iron
- The carbon content of the surface rises
- This forms a "soft core, hard shell" structure

**Advantages:**
- Saves steel (wrought iron inside)
- Good performance (hard outside, tough inside)
- Often used for ancient swords!

### 6.2 Pack carburising (practical)

**Materials:**
```
Carburising compound recipe:
- Charcoal powder: 70-80%
- Plant ash: 15-20%
- Salt: 5-10%

Mix evenly and set aside
```

**Steps:**

**1. Preparation:**
- Polish the wrought iron work smooth
- Prepare a sealed iron box or clay pot

```
Preparation points:
- Forge the work to its final shape first and remove all scale and rust (it should not be heavily forged after carburising)
- Carburising compound: break the charcoal into pieces the size of rice grains to soybeans (too fine and gas cannot get through; too coarse and contact is poor),
  and mix well with the plant ash and salt; the charcoal must be dry
- Container: a sheet-iron box, a thick iron pipe sealed at both ends, or a refractory clay pot;
  leave at least 2-3 cm between pieces and between the pieces and the walls, and fill it and pack it tight
- Parts that do not need carburising (such as the eye for a handle) can be coated with clay to keep them separate
- Put in 1-2 small test bars (about 8 mm thick) of the same material, with one end where it is easy to pull out;
  use them to judge the case depth, so you do not have to damage the work
```

**2. Packing:**
```
[Cross-section]
┌────────────┐
│ compound (3 cm) │← top
├────────────┤
│  ┌──────┐  │
│  │ work │  │← packed all round with compound
│  └──────┘  │
├────────────┤
│ compound (3 cm) │← bottom
└────────────┘
Seal with clay
```

**3. Heating:**
```
Temperature: 900-950°C (bright red)
Time:
  - Thin pieces (blades 2-3 mm): 4-6 hours
  - Medium pieces (axes 5 mm): 8-12 hours
  - Thick pieces (10 mm): 16-24 hours

Keep the temperature steady!

Timing and temperature control:
- Start timing only when the whole box is bright red (red right through to the centre of the box);
  a large box takes 1-2 hours to heat through
- Bury the box in a charcoal fire, burn slowly with a gentle blast, keep it bright red; do not let it reach orange-yellow
  (too high a temperature makes the work's grain coarse, or even melts it locally)
- Partway through you can pull out a test bar, quench it in water and break it to see the thickness of the hard case; stop when it is enough
- When you open the box, carbon monoxide flames shoot out; stand to the side and work outdoors
```

**4. Cooling:**
- Cool slowly with the furnace
- Or take out and cool in air

**5. Inspection:**
- The surface should be dark grey
- The carburised layer can be seen

**6. Quenching:**
- Reheat to cherry red
- Quench to harden the carburised layer
- The long time at high temperature during carburising coarsens the grain, so it is best to normalise 1-2 times before quenching;
  use a lower quenching temperature (a little hotter than where it just loses magnetism); the high-carbon steel case cracks easily in a water quench;
  thin pieces can use brine or warm water, complex pieces oil

**7. Tempering:**
- Temper to reduce brittleness

### 6.3 Liquid carburising (quick)

**Method:**
```
1. Heat the work to cherry red
2. Push it quickly into charcoal powder
3. Charcoal sticks to the surface
4. Reheat
5. Carbon diffuses into the surface
6. Repeat 5-10 times
```

**Advantage:** fast (1-2 hours)
**Disadvantage:** shallow case (0.1-0.3 mm)

```
Note: this method is really "simple surface carburising by repeatedly coating with charcoal";
the case is very thin and suits only small items (an awl made from a nail, the edge of a small knife);
a single sharpening may grind right through it, so it does not suit knives that need repeated sharpening.

Real factory "liquid carburising" uses a molten cyanide salt bath, which is highly toxic;
breathing it in or getting it in a wound can be fatal. Never attempt it.
```

### 6.4 Case depth

The table below gives conservative rule-of-thumb values for improvised pack carburising (for reference only); the actual depth varies widely with the quality of the charcoal, the size of the box and the furnace temperature. Go by the test bar.

| Time | Temperature | Case depth |
|------|------|----------|
| 4 hours | 900°C | 0.3-0.5 mm |
| 8 hours | 900°C | 0.5-1 mm |
| 16 hours | 900°C | 1-1.5 mm |
| 24 hours | 900°C | 1.5-2 mm |

**Adequate depth:** 0.5-1 mm (knives)

```
The table gives approximate values for pack carburising; in practice temperature, compound and how well it is sealed make a big difference.
Go by the test bar:
1. Quench the test bar together with the work
2. File a nick in the test bar, clamp it in a vice and knock it off
3. Fracture: an outer ring of fine-grained, greyish-white material is the carburised, hardened case;
   the coarser, brighter, bendable centre is wrought iron the carbon did not reach
4. Measure the thickness of the outer ring. Knives 0.5-1 mm; farm tools such as hoes and picks 1-1.5 mm

The case on a knife must not be too thin: sharpening grinds away the hard layer at the edge
and exposes soft iron → the edge blunts quickly. For knives that need repeated sharpening,
it is better to use solid steel or "sandwiched steel" (a steel edge strip sandwiched in wrought iron)
```

For forging sandwiched and inserted steel edges see [Forging Simple Tools - Inserted steel edges](03-forging-simple-tools.md#112-inserted-steel-edges).

## 7. Other surface treatments

### 7.1 Nitriding (optional)

**Principle:** diffuse nitrogen into the surface to form an extremely hard nitrided layer

```
For information only; improvised attempts are not recommended:
- Factory nitriding holds parts for a long time in ammonia gas in a sealed furnace, or uses cyanide-containing salt baths;
  it needs special equipment, and the salt baths are highly toxic
- The widely circulated method of "burying it in urea or nitrate and heating" is unreliable and dangerous:
  urea decomposes when heated into ammonia and toxic products; nitrates are strong oxidisers
  and burn violently or even explode on contact with charcoal, grease or sawdust
- For a small community, carburising + quenching + tempering already meets the needs of almost all tools
```

### 7.2 Bluing/blacking (rust prevention)

**Principle:** form a dense oxide film

**Method:**
```
1. Remove rust and grease from the surface of the work
2. Heat to about 300°C (dark blue; heated further it turns light blue, then grey)
3. Plunge into oil at once
4. A blue-black oxide film forms on the surface

Or:
1. Immerse in an alkaline oxidising solution (complex recipe)
2. Heat to 135-155°C
3. 30-90 minutes
4. The surface turns black
```

**Effect:** prevents rust, looks good

```
The practical method is "oil-baked blacking" (the same idea as seasoning a new iron wok or a cast-iron pan):
1. Polish the work bright, wash off grease with hot alkaline water or lye from plant ash, and do not touch it with your hands after that
2. Heat until oil wiped on it smokes immediately (about 250-350°C)
3. Wipe on a thin coat of vegetable oil (linseed oil, tung oil, rapeseed oil) or beeswax;
   the oil smokes and carbonises, forming a hard black film
4. Wipe off excess oil, heat again, coat again; repeat 3-5 times, and the colour gets blacker and more even
5. When cool, wipe on another coat of oil

⚠️ Do not bake a knife that has already been quenched and tempered like this all over: heated above 300°C,
   the edge will be tempered soft. Use it only on parts that do not need high hardness
   (iron ferrules on handles, hinges, nails, pots, the non-cutting parts of farm tools),
   or blacken first and then quench and temper
⚠️ "Alkaline oxidising solution blacking" uses strong alkali solution close to boiling; a splash in the eye can blind you;
   it is not recommended as an improvised procedure
✓ The black film is very thin and is not enough by itself to stop rust; you must oil it often;
  its job is to make the oil cling better
```

### 7.3 Plating (advanced)

**Tin plating:**
- Dipping in molten tin
- Prevents rust (tinplate)

**Copper plating:**
- Electroplating or chemical plating
- Prevents rust, conducts electricity

```
What the various platings really do:
- Tin plating: suits sheet iron, cooking utensils and drinking vessels; once the tin layer is damaged,
  the iron rusts faster than before (iron is more reactive than tin), so keep it intact.
  For the method see the chapter on sheet and wire; for food and drink vessels use only pure tin, never lead-containing solder
- Hot-dip galvanising: dip iron parts into molten zinc; when the zinc layer is damaged, the zinc corrodes first
  and still protects the iron; suits outdoor iron parts, wire and buckets;
  ☠️ molten zinc gives off white smoke (zinc oxide); breathing it in causes "metal fume fever"
  (fever, shivering, cough, like flu); it must be done outdoors, upwind
- Putting an iron part into copper sulphate solution "plates itself with copper",
  but the copper layer is loose and adheres poorly; copper is less reactive than iron, so where it is damaged the iron rusts faster;
  use it only for decoration or identification marks, not for rust prevention
- Real electroplating needs a steady direct-current supply and properly made-up plating solution; it belongs to the electrochemical industry
```

For making tinplate see [Sheet and Wire - Tinplate](11-sheet-and-wire.md#3-tinplate); for the principle of galvanising see [Other Metals - Zinc](10-other-metals.md#4-zinc) and [Advanced Chemistry - Corrosion and protection](../../../docs/09-进阶科学与工程/05-化学进阶.md#54-腐蚀和防护).

### 7.4 Coatings and everyday rust prevention

| Method | How | Rust protection (approx.) | Suitable for |
|------|------|------|------|
| Thin oil | Wipe clean, then wipe on a coat of vegetable oil, engine oil or animal fat | Indoors, weeks to months | Tools in regular use |
| Thick grease/tallow | Apply a thick coat, wrap in oiled paper or cloth | Over 1 year | Tools and spare parts in long-term storage |
| Beeswax | Warm the work slightly, rub on beeswax, spread evenly | Several months | Knives, small items, iron fittings on wooden handles |
| Boiled tung oil/linseed oil | Apply thinly, let it dry to a hard film, 2-3 coats | About 1 year | Parts that are not rubbed often, farm tools |
| Bitumen/pine tar | Melt by heating and brush on | Many years | Iron parts buried or under water (post feet, iron hoops) |
| Paint | Remove rust first, then primer, then topcoat | Many years | Large items, doors and windows, sheet-iron roofs |

```
The key to rust prevention is "dry + air shut out":
✓ After use, first wipe off water, sweat and dirt, then oil. Sweat and salt cause rust fastest
✓ Store away from sea wind, latrines and animal pens (ammonia and salt spray speed up rusting)
✓ Store tools in a sealed box with charcoal or quicklime to absorb moisture
  (quicklime heats up and swells when it absorbs water; keep it in a cloth bag and do not fill the box)
✓ Surface rust already: scrub it off with sandpaper or a wire brush, or soak in vinegar and brush; wash, dry and oil at once
✗ Do not use oil containing salt (such as fat from curing meat) or animal fat containing water; the more you apply, the more it rusts
✗ Do not paint or tar cutting edges; use oil or wax on knives
✗ Food utensils (pots, knives) only with cooking oil, never engine oil or bitumen
```

For everyday tool care see [Forging Simple Tools - Preventing rust](03-forging-simple-tools.md#101-preventing-rust) and [Woodworking and Tool Maintenance - Tool care](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/09-木工与工具维护.md#工具保养).

## 8. Quality control

### 8.1 Hardness testing

**Method A: file test**
```
Push a file across the work:
- The file skids with a hissing sound → high hardness ✓
- The file bites → not hard enough ✗
```

How to test hardness with a file:
```
1. Use a sharp new file (or an old file kept only for testing hardness, not used for work),
   using a corner or edge of the file
2. Choose an unimportant spot: the tang, the back near the edge, the side of a chisel;
   first grind off the surface scale and decarburised layer to expose bright metal
3. Use only very light pressure: push once and stop; do not file back and forth (it damages the edge and the file)
4. Try it at the same time on material of known hardness (such as a piece of old file, or annealed steel) and compare the feel
```

| How the file feels | Approximate state | Notes |
|------|------|------|
| Skids completely, like sliding on glass, hissing | Quenched, not tempered (hardest) | Hardness needed for files and scrapers |
| Skids, with a slight drag | Quenched, tempered low (pale straw - gold) | Right for knives and drill bits |
| Catches slightly, leaves a faint bright mark | Tempered to brown - light blue | Right for axes, chisels, saw blades, springs |
| Bites in, makes filings | Not hardened, or over-tempered | Not acceptable for knives |
| Files very easily, filings curl | Annealed, or low-carbon steel or wrought iron | Easy to work |

```
✓ The file test only shows the surface layer; if a file will not bite a carburised piece, that only shows the surface is hard;
  for case depth look at the fracture of the test bar
✗ Do not use a file to test things harder than it is (high-carbon steel quenched and not tempered);
  that spot on the file is ruined; test once and stop
```

**Method B: scratch test**
```
Scratch with a knife point:
- Will not scratch, or only a faint mark → hard ✓
- A groove is easily scratched → soft ✗
```

**Method C: spark test (advanced)**
```
Grind on a grinding wheel and watch the sparks:
- Pig iron: short sparks, dark red to orange, a few small forks at the ends
- Wrought iron: long sparks, few forks
- Steel: medium sparks, many forks
- Quenched steel: fine, dense, bright sparks
```

Detailed spark-test judgements:
```
Preparation:
- A grinding wheel (hand-cranked or electric), fitted with a guard; wear goggles and stand to the side of the wheel
- Do it in the shade, against a dark background, so the sparks are easy to see
- Prepare "reference samples" of known steels: an old file (high carbon), rail or leaf spring (medium-high carbon),
  rebar or iron wire (low carbon), fragments of a cast-iron pot (cast iron); compare with the samples every time
- Each time, press the work against the wheel with the same pressure at the same place, so the sparks shoot out horizontally

What to look for:
1. The length and colour of the spark streams
2. How many "bursts" (forks that explode like small fireworks) there are along the streams
3. Whether the forks fork once, or fork again from the forks (second and third bursts)
```

| Material | Streams | Bursts | Colour |
|------|------|------|------|
| Wrought iron, low-carbon steel (below about 0.3% carbon) | Long, straight, thick | Very few, an occasional single small fork | Straw yellow |
| Medium-carbon steel (about 0.3-0.6%) | Medium length | Fairly many, with second forks | Yellow, fairly bright |
| High-carbon steel (above about 0.6%; files and bearings about 1%) | Short and dense | Very many, very dense, repeated forking, like clusters of fireworks | Bright white-yellow |
| Cast iron, pig iron | Short and thin, few in number | Small and few, with fine little bursts at the ends | Dark red-orange |
| Some alloy steels (such as those containing tungsten or chromium) | Streams dull, reddish | Fewer bursts | Dark red, may have arrow-shaped ends |

```
Rules for judging:
✓ The more carbon, the more, denser and more branched the bursts
✓ Use it only to compare "which piece has more carbon, which has less"; it cannot measure carbon content precisely
✓ Whether it has been quenched makes little difference to the sparks; for hardness you still need the file
✗ Do not use it to test copper, aluminium or stainless steel:
  copper and aluminium give almost no sparks, and they clog the wheel
```

**Method D: fracture check (test piece)**
```
1. Cut a small test piece of the same material (or a carburising test bar) and quench it together with the work
2. File or grind a nick on one side, clamp it in a vice and break it with a hammer
   (wear goggles; fragments fly)
3. Look at the fracture:
   - Very fine grain, greyish-white, like fine porcelain or velvet → correct temperature, hardened through ✓
   - Coarse, sparkling grain, like white sugar → quenching temperature too high (overheated) ✗
     → normalise, then quench again at a lower temperature
   - Bends when struck instead of breaking → not hardened (not steel, or not hot enough)
   - Fine on the outside, coarse in the centre → normal for a carburised piece, or a large piece not hardened through
```

For safety when using a grinding wheel see [Bench Work and Machining - Grinding](09-bench-work-and-machining.md#8-grinding).

### 8.2 Toughness testing

**Bend test:**
```
Bend the blade outwards:
- Does not break at 90° → good toughness ✓
- Breaks at <45° → brittle, needs tempering ✗
```

**Drop test:**
```
Drop from 1 m onto a concrete floor:
- Undamaged → good toughness ✓
- Chipped/broken → brittle ✗
```

```
⚠️ Bend and drop tests are destructive. The first time you treat a particular steel,
   test a sample of the same material treated in the same batch; do not risk a newly finished piece
✓ A good knife: springs back to shape after a gentle bend; after chopping hardwood and whittling bone
  the edge neither chips nor rolls; it can still be sharpened on a whetstone (one that is too hard will not sharpen)
✓ Axes, chisels and hammers must take blows: after a dozen or more chops, splits or strikes, check the edge and striking face;
  no chips, no rolled edge, no "mushroom head" on the striking face
✓ Springs: after being compressed to working position several dozen times, they return to their original length when released
```

## 9. Troubleshooting

### Problem 1: too brittle after quenching; chips as soon as it is used

**Cause:** not tempered, or not tempered enough

**Solution:**
- Temper again to 250-280°C
- Watch the colour change

### Problem 2: the knife is not sharp and blunts easily

**Causes:**
- Quenching temperature not high enough
- Cooled too slowly

**Solution:**
- Quench again
- Heat to cherry red
- Cool quickly in water

### Problem 3: uneven carburising

**Causes:**
- Compound not packed tightly
- Unsteady temperature

**Solution:**
- Pack the compound tight
- Keep the temperature constant

### Problem 4: badly warped work

**Causes:**
- Uneven cooling
- Asymmetrical shape

**Solution:**
- Use a clamp or jig
- Enter the water vertically
- Or switch to an oil quench

## 10. Case studies

### Case 1: making a high-quality kitchen knife

**Steps:**
```
1. Material: wrought iron 500 g
2. Forge into a knife shape
3. Carburise: the edge area, 4-6 hours (thin blade)
4. Quench: cherry red, in brine or warm water (edge first; a carburised case cracks easily in plain water)
5. Temper: 240°C (gold)
6. Sharpen
7. Fit the handle

Result: hard outside, tough inside; sharp and durable!
```

### Case 2: repairing a tool cracked in quenching

**Problem:** a chisel cracked during quenching

**Repair:**
```
1. Anneal (soften)
2. Heat to bright red
3. Line up the crack
4. Hammer-weld it closed
5. Grind it clean again
6. Quench again (in oil this time)
7. Temper

Success rate: low
```

```
Note: the inside surfaces of a quench crack are already oxidised, so it is very hard to truly forge-weld it shut;
it may look closed, but there is usually still ash and scale trapped inside, and under load it will crack again in the same place.
✓ Crack at a corner or edge where there is no load: grind off or cut off the cracked part and make a smaller tool
✓ Crack right through or where the load falls (the root of a chisel edge, beside a hammer eye): do not repair it;
  anneal it and forge it into something else (a punch, nails, a small knife)
✗ On tools that take blows - hammers, axes, chisels - a repaired crack can burst apart and fly off in use;
  do not risk continuing to use them
```

## 11. Checklist

### Before quenching:
- [ ] Confirmed it is steel (not wrought iron)
- [ ] No cracks in the work
- [ ] Quenchant ready (water/oil)
- [ ] Clamps and tongs all to hand
- [ ] Colours are more accurate observed in a dark room
- [ ] Oil quench: metal drum with lid and dry sand to hand, oil preheated
- [ ] Magnet and test piece of the same material ready
- [ ] Goggles, leather gloves, long sleeves

### After quenching:
- [ ] Temper immediately (no delay!)
- [ ] Hardness test (file skids)
- [ ] No cracks
- [ ] Warping within acceptable limits

### Tempering check:
- [ ] Surface polished bright
- [ ] Colour reached the target (gold/brown)
- [ ] Toughness test passed
