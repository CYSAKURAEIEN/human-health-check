## Pigs

### Why keep pigs?

```
Advantages:
✓ Grow fast (ready for slaughter in 5-6 months)
✓ Breed prolifically
✓ High meat yield
✓ Omnivorous (but must not be fed swill; see Disease prevention and treatment)
✓ Pig manure is an excellent fertiliser

Consider:
- Larger investment (feed, buildings)
- Need some skill
- Fairly strong smell
- Disease risk (especially African swine fever; China has no approved vaccine yet)
```

**After a disaster**: pigs "compete with people for grain" — a pig eats about 3 kg of grain for every 1 kg it gains. When grain is short, keep only a few pigs and feed them mainly on things people do not eat: vegetable leaves, sweet-potato vines, bran, okara (soybean pulp). Sheep, goats, rabbits and cattle eat grass and do not compete with people for grain, so they suit times of food shortage better.

### Choosing a breed

**Local pigs**:
- Adaptable, good disease resistance
- Good meat quality
- Grow more slowly
- Tolerate coarse feed: can eat more green and roughage feed (bran, vegetable leaves, sweet-potato vines) and cope better than imported breeds when concentrate feed is short
- Sows mature early, are good mothers and have large litters (Taihu pigs, for example, often have more than ten piglets in a litter)

**Imported breeds** (such as Duroc, Large White, Landrace):
- Grow fast
- High lean-meat percentage
- Good feed conversion
- But need complete feed and a better pigsty; on coarse feed they grow slowly and fall ill more easily

**Crossbred pigs**:
- Combine the advantages
- Recommended for beginners
- If you keep crossbred pigs for breeding, their offspring will segregate and be uneven. If you want to breed your own pigs in the long term, keep local sows or purebred sows (for the principles of selection see [Seed Saving and Breeding](../../../docs/04-动植物培育/08-留种与育种.md))

### Building a pigsty

**Choosing a site**:
```
✓ High, dry ground
✓ Facing the sun, sheltered from the wind
✓ Good drainage
✓ Away from houses (more than 200 m)
✓ Water close at hand
✓ At least 30 m from wells and water sources, and downhill of them (so manure water does not seep in)
✓ Downwind: downwind of the prevailing summer wind, so the smell does not blow towards houses
```

**Pigsty layout**:
```
Floor area:
- Finishing pigs: 1.5-2 m² per head
- Sows: 3-4 m² per head

Design:
✓ Warm and ventilated (warm in winter, cool in summer)
✓ Dry and clean
✓ Plenty of light
✓ Strong and durable

Fittings:
✓ Feeding trough (concrete or iron)
✓ Water trough or automatic drinker
✓ Slatted floor (easy to clean)
✓ Or concrete floor (sloped for drainage)

Areas:
- Lying area (dry)
- Dunging area (easy to clean)
- Feeding area
```

**How to build it**:
```
Floor and drainage:
- Floor slope about 2%-3% (2-3 cm fall per metre), sloping towards the dunging area and drain
- Make the lying area a little higher than the dunging area and cover it with dry straw or
  boards; do not let pigs sleep on wet concrete
- Pigs are clean animals: if the pen is big enough and the areas are clear, they dung in
  the corner furthest from the trough and the sleeping place
  → Put the dunging area at the low end, near the door and the drain; clean it every day

Walls and fences:
- Fences about 1 m high (for adult pigs) and strong: pigs root and barge. Bury the bottom
  edge of wooden fences in the ground or weigh it down with stones
- Make the pen gate open outwards, so manure is easy to clear

Ventilation and damp:
- In winter close the windows to keep warm, but leave vents in the roof or high up so damp
  and smells can escape
- Stinging eyes and a sharp smell of ammonia when you go in = not enough ventilation;
  there will be more respiratory disease
- Water droplets on the walls, damp or mouldy bedding = too wet; ventilate more and change
  bedding more often
- Avoid draughts: in winter, cold air blowing straight onto pigs through gaps in walls and
  doors is the commonest cause of colds and diarrhoea; put vents up high

Heat:
- Pigs hardly sweat; above about 28-30°C they suffer heat stress (panting with mouth open,
  lying still, not eating)
- Give shade, provide a mud wallow in the pen or yard, and splash cool water on the floor
  and on the pigs
- Give plenty of drinking water in summer
```

### Buying piglets

**Choosing healthy piglets**:
```
✓ Lively
✓ Bright eyes
✓ Glossy coat
✓ Good appetite
✓ Normal droppings (no diarrhoea)
✓ Normal breathing (no coughing)
✓ Reasonable weight (about 7-10 kg at weaning)
✓ Has vaccination records

Buying:
- 15-30 kg piglets (weaned and through the nursery stage; easier to keep alive)
- From a local farm with a good reputation
- Check the vaccination records
```

```
❌ Do not buy: piglets with runny droppings stuck to the tail and rump, a long, rough,
   dull coat, a hunched back, red or purple blotches on the skin, coughing or wheezing,
   or a limp
❌ Do not bring in pigs from areas that have had African swine fever or other epidemics;
   do not buy cheap pigs of unknown origin
✓ Keep newly bought pigs in a pen of their own and watch them for about 30 days; mix them
  with your existing pigs only when you are sure they are healthy
  (see Disease prevention and vaccination principles)
✓ After bringing them home, give a little clean water first, then a little feed a few
  hours later; switch gradually to your own feed over the first week
```

### Pig feed and rations

**Roughly how much one pig eats per day** (air-dry feed, i.e. grain, bran, soybean cake and the like, not counting the water in green feed):

| Stage | Body weight | Feed per day | Water per day |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| Nursery piglet | 10-30 kg | about 0.5-1.5 kg | about 2-5 L |
| Grower | 30-60 kg | about 1.5-2.2 kg | about 5-8 L |
| Finisher | 60-110 kg | about 2.2-3 kg | about 8-12 L |
| Pregnant sow | — | about 2-2.5 kg (about 2.5-3 kg in the last month) | about 15-20 L |
| Lactating sow | — | about 2 kg + 0.4-0.5 kg per piglet (often 5-7 kg) | about 25-40 L |
| Breeding boar | — | about 2.5-3 kg | about 15-20 L |

```
Rules of thumb:
- Water intake is about 2.5-3 times the dry feed; more in hot weather and during lactation
  → Too little water does far more harm than a missed meal: pigs eat less, grow slowly,
    and in severe cases get salt poisoning
- When feeding freely, a little left in the trough after they are full is about right;
  a lot left in the bottom of the trough half an hour after feeding = overfed, feed they
  do not like, or a sick pig

Green and roughage feed (parts people do not eat; saves a lot of grain):
- Vegetable leaves, sweet-potato vines, pumpkin, radish, lucerne (alfalfa), Chinese milk
  vetch, wild greens (do not feed any you do not know)
- Green feed can make up a good share of a local pig's ration; imported breeds and young
  piglets cannot digest much coarse fibre
- Green feed must be fresh, washed and chopped. Do not leave it piled up to heat (heaped,
  stewing vegetable leaves produce nitrite, which poisons pigs: skin and mucous membranes
  turn purple, breathing becomes difficult and death comes quickly)
- Bran, okara, distillers' grains (in small amounts) and soybean cake are all good feeds
  (for sources see the links below)
- Do not feed raw soybeans: they contain anti-nutritional factors that upset digestion;
  boil or roast them first

Salt and minerals:
- Salt: about 0.3-0.5% of the feed, mixed in well. Pigs are sensitive to salt; feeding
  leftovers or pickled vegetables, or too little water, easily causes salt poisoning
  (thirst, fits, walking in circles, death)
- Calcium and phosphorus: without a premix, use bone meal at about 1-2% (bones burnt or
  boiled thoroughly, then crushed and ground), and shell meal or limestone powder at about 1%
- Vitamins: usually not short if there is plenty of green feed

❌ Do not feed mouldy maize, peanut cake or grain: aflatoxin causes liver damage in pigs
   and abortion in sows
❌ Do not feed swill or any leftovers containing meat (African swine fever; see Disease
   prevention and treatment)
```

Related chapters: for sources of bran and soybean cake see [Grain and Oil Processing](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/14-粮油加工.md); for salt for livestock see [Salt Making](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/15-制盐.md); for mouldy grain see [Food Processing and Preservation – Aflatoxin](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/01-食物加工与保存.md#黄曲霉毒素).

### Feeding and management

#### Weaner stage (weaning to 30 kg)

```
Feed:
- Piglet feed (high protein, easy to digest)
- Free feeding
- Clean drinking water

Management:
✓ Keep warm (18-22°C)
✓ Clean and dry
✓ Prevent diarrhoea
✓ Keep in groups (10-15 per group)
✓ Castrate (males, at 1 month old)
✓ Vaccinate
```

```
Further notes:
- Diarrhoea is most likely in the first week or two after weaning: change feed gradually
  and feed little and often (4-5 times a day); do not let them gorge
- Judge warmth by the pigs: huddled in a heap and shivering = cold; spread out and
  panting = hot; lying stretched out on their sides, touching but not piled up = right
- Castration: male pigs not kept for breeding should be castrated, or their meat will
  have a strong boar taint. Have an experienced person or a vet do it; disinfect the
  instruments and wound, keep the pen clean and dry afterwards, and watch for a few days
  for pus
- Mixing groups: pigs from different litters fight when put together. Mix them in the
  evening, and sprinkle them all with the same smell (such as dilute vinegar water)
```

#### Finishing stage (30 kg to slaughter)

```
Feed:
- Finishing feed
- Or mix your own:
  * Maize 60-70%
  * Soybean meal/soybean cake 15-20%
  * Wheat bran 5-10%
  * Premix 3-5%
  * Green feed (vegetable leaves, grass) as available

Feeding:
- 2-3 times a day
- Fixed times and amounts
- Roughage before concentrates, dry before wet
- Plenty of clean drinking water

Management:
✓ Suitable temperature (15-25°C)
✓ Good ventilation (no draughts)
✓ Dry and clean (clear manure every day)
✓ Some exercise (take care with penned pigs)
✓ Keep quiet
✓ Watch their health

Feed used:
- Total feed for the finishing stage about 300-350 kg
- Feed-to-gain ratio: about 3:1 (3 kg of feed for 1 kg of live weight)
```

```
Without soybean meal and premix (common after a disaster):
- Protein: boiled soybeans, soybean cake, okara, broad beans and peas, lucerne and other
  legume forage; fish offal must be boiled thoroughly (never use any pork products)
- Minerals: bone meal, shell meal and salt, amounts as in the section above
- Maize and bran only: pigs put on fat but not frame and grow slowly; a pig may take a
  year to finish
- Local pigs on rough feeding often take 8-12 months to reach slaughter weight. That is
  normal; do not feed mouldy grain to fatten them faster

Are they growing well?
- Weigh them every month (or estimate weight from heart girth with a tape measure); in the
  growing stage about 0.5-0.8 kg a day is normal (less for local pigs on rough feeding)
- Glossy coat, pink skin, formed droppings neither hard nor loose, sleeping after eating
  = normal
- Long rough coat, thinness, rooting at walls and eating soil, biting each other's tails =
  poor nutrition (short of salt, minerals or protein) or overcrowding
```

#### Sending to slaughter

```
When:
- 5-6 months old
- Weight 90-120 kg

Signs:
- Well-filled body
- Straight, level back
- Appetite begins to fall (has peaked)

Slaughter on time (feed conversion falls if you keep them longer)
```

### Sow breeding and farrowing

**Selection and mating**:
```
Choosing a sow:
- From a sow with large litters and good mothering
- At least 6 pairs of teats, evenly spaced, no blind teats (inverted teats that give no milk)
- Strong legs, walks normally, vulva of normal size

Age at first mating:
- Local sows about 6-8 months; imported/crossbred sows about 8 months and over about 120 kg
- Mating too early: the sow has not finished growing herself; small litters and poor
  condition later

Recognising heat (oestrous cycle about 21 days, each heat lasting about 2-3 days):
- Red, swollen vulva with mucus discharge
- Restless, calling, off her feed, mounting other pigs
- Standing reflex (most important): press hard on the sow's back with your hands; she
  stands still with her ears pricked
  → The standing reflex shows the best time to mate

Mating:
- Mate first about 12-24 hours after the standing reflex appears, then again about
  12 hours later; this gives a higher conception rate
- With no boar of your own, take the sow to a nearby household that keeps a breeding boar
  (take disease precautions when moving pigs)
- If she does not come back into heat about 21 days after mating, she is very likely
  pregnant; if she does come into heat again, mate her again
```

**Pregnancy lasts about 114 days** (traditionally "three months, three weeks, three days").

```
Care during pregnancy:
- Do not overfeed in early pregnancy: overfat sows have smaller litters, more difficult
  births and crush more piglets
- The foetuses grow fastest in the last month; increase feed (see the feed table)
- Avoid frightening, chasing, crowding and slipping; do not let her fight other pigs
- About 1 week before farrowing, move the sow into a cleaned and disinfected farrowing pen
  so she gets used to it
```

**Farrowing pen and preventing crushing**:
```
- Farrowing pen about 2 m × 2.5 m. Fit a guard rail (round poles or steel pipe) all along
  the walls, about 20-25 cm off the floor and about 20-25 cm from the wall
  → When the sow lies down against the wall, the piglets can escape under the rail and are
    not crushed (crushing is one of the main causes of piglet deaths)
- Board off one corner as a warm creep area (only piglets can get in), bed it with dry
  straw, and hang a heat lamp or put in a heated box
- Temperature: the sow is comfortable at about 18-22°C, but newborn piglets need about
  30-34°C, so keep them apart: the sow's area cooler, the piglets' creep warmer
```

**Signs of farrowing and helping at the birth**:
```
Signs that farrowing is near:
- Udder swells and reddens 1-3 days before
- Carries straw to make a nest, restless, urinates often
- Milk can be squeezed from the teats: from the front teats → farrowing within about
  1 day; from the rear teats too → within a few hours
- Blood-stained fluid runs from the vulva

Preparation:
- Clean towels or soft cloths, scissors, thin thread (all boiled), iodine tincture,
  heated box/straw nest, torch, notebook
- The helper cuts their nails short, washes their hands and wears disposable gloves

Steps:
1. The sow usually farrows lying on her side, normally one piglet every 10-30 minutes or
   so; the whole litter takes about 2-5 hours
2. As soon as a piglet is born, wipe the mucus from its mouth and nose with a clean cloth,
   then dry its whole body (especially important in winter)
3. Umbilical cord: tie it tightly with thread about 4 cm from the belly, cut on the far
   side of the thread, and paint the stump with iodine tincture
   (if the cord has broken by itself and is not bleeding, just apply iodine)
4. Once dry, put it straight onto a teat to drink colostrum; put small, weak piglets on
   the front teats (more milk)
5. The afterbirth (placenta) comes last, usually within a few hours of the last piglet.
   Remove it promptly and bury it deep together with any stillborn piglets; do not let the
   sow eat it

Apparently dead piglets (not breathing but the heart is still beating):
- Clear mucus from the mouth and nose, hold it upside down by the hind legs and pat the
  chest; or support the head and neck with one hand and the rump with the other and bend
  and stretch it to help it breathe

Call an experienced person or a vet:
⚠️ Straining hard for more than about 30-60 minutes with no piglet born
⚠️ A long pause (more than about 1 hour) after a few piglets, with the sow still straining
   or exhausted
⚠️ Foul-smelling, dark fluid from the vulva, or the sow has a high fever and will not eat
   or drink
⚠️ Do not pull hard, and do not reach in with dirty hands or instruments (this tears the
   birth canal and causes infection)
```

For a diagram of birth assistance and newborn care see [Lambing and lamb care](#lambing-and-lamb-care) (pigs and sheep compared).

**Piglet care**:
```
✓ Colostrum: piglets must get it as soon as possible after birth; the first few hours
  matter most. The antibodies in colostrum are the piglet's only immunity
✓ Fixing teats: for the first few days help weak piglets to keep to the front teats, so the
  whole litter grows evenly
✓ Warmth: about 30-34°C in week 1, then about 2°C lower each week; piglets piled in a
  heap = too cold
✓ Iron: sow's milk contains very little iron. Housed piglets need iron at 2-3 days old (use
  an iron product as its instructions say, or have a vet inject it); otherwise anaemia
  starts at about 2-3 weeks (pale skin, diarrhoea, poor growth)
  → Without an iron product: every day put a shovelful of clean, fresh soil in the piglets'
     area (dug from a place where pigs have never been kept; deeper soil is cleaner) and
     let them root in it for iron
✓ Creep feeding: from about 7 days old put a little roasted, ground grain or piglet feed in
  the creep area to tempt them to eat
✓ Weaning: commercial farms usually wean at 3-4 weeks (21-28 days); at home you can wait
  until 5-8 weeks. At weaning take the sow away and leave the piglets in their own pen
✓ The sow comes back into heat about 3-7 days after weaning and can be mated for the next
  litter
```

### Disease prevention and treatment

**Prevention first**:
```
✓ Cleanliness (clean every day)
✓ Disinfection (regularly, and when the pen is empty)
✓ Ventilated and dry
✓ Balanced nutrition
✓ Vaccination (classical swine fever, foot-and-mouth disease and others)
✓ Worming (regularly)
✓ Isolate sick pigs
✓ Watch for anything unusual (appetite, droppings, behaviour)
```

```
Checks at feeding time (the easiest health check):
- Is any pig not coming to feed, or lying alone away from the group?
- Droppings: normally formed; diarrhoea, blood, or hard dry pellets like goat droppings
  are abnormal
- Breathing: is the belly heaving fast at rest? Any coughing?
- Skin: any red or purple blotches on the ears, belly or legs?
- Temperature: take the rectal temperature with a veterinary (or separate, dedicated)
  thermometer. Normal for a pig is about 38-39.5°C; over 40°C is fever

Worming:
- Pig roundworm is the commonest: worm piglets once before finishing, sows about 2 weeks
  before farrowing, breeding pigs 2-3 times a year
- Dose wormers by body weight as the label says; for a few days afterwards clear the
  droppings promptly and compost them (they contain huge numbers of worm eggs)

Disinfecting an empty pen:
- After a batch of pigs has gone, remove all manure and bedding → wash with clean water →
  paint the walls and floor with quicklime water (milk of lime)
  → leave empty to dry for about 1-2 weeks before bringing in new pigs
- Quicklime gives off great heat when wet. If it splashes in the eyes, brush it away first,
  then rinse with plenty of water
```

For safe use of lime see [Lime and Building Materials](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/16-石灰与建筑材料.md).

**Common diseases**:
```
African swine fever (the biggest threat):
- China currently has no approved vaccine on the market and no specific treatment; the
  acute form kills close to 100%
- Feeding swill (kitchen and food waste) to pigs is forbidden: it is banned by national law
  and is a main route of spread
- Do not buy pigs from affected areas; disinfect outside people and vehicles before they
  come onto the farm
- If pigs suddenly have high fever or die in numbers, report at once to the local animal
  husbandry and veterinary department

Classical swine fever:
- Prevent it with vaccine!
- High fever, not eating, constipation followed by diarrhoea, purple blotches on the skin
- Isolate at once when it appears and report to a vet

Diarrhoea:
- Common in piglets
- Keep warm, clean drinking water
- Treat with antibacterials
- Give fluids to prevent dehydration

Respiratory disease:
- Coughing, wheezing
- Ventilate, keep warm
- Treat promptly

⚠️ For serious disease consult a vet!
```

**African swine fever: more key points**:
```
How it spreads:
- Sick pigs, infected pork and pork products (the virus can survive for months in
  sausage, cured pork and frozen meat)
- Pork in swill and leftovers
- Contaminated people, clothing, shoes, vehicles, feed and tools; ticks; wild boar

Signs:
- High fever (40-42°C), not eating, huddling, skin (ears, belly, legs) turning red or purple
- Vomiting, bloody droppings, difficult breathing; often death within a few days. Sometimes
  the first sign is one or two pigs dying suddenly

Do:
✓ Feed only your own grain, bran and green feed; never feed pigs any leftovers containing meat
✓ Change into dedicated shoes and outer clothes before entering the pigsty; keep outsiders
  (especially other pig keepers and pig dealers) out of the pigsty
✓ Keep pork and meat products you have bought away from the pigsty; do not let hands and
  knives that have cut raw meat touch pig feed
✓ Keep wild boar away from domestic pigs (fencing); control ticks
✓ If you suspect it: shut all pigs in their pens at once; do not sell, slaughter or move
  them; report to the local animal husbandry and veterinary department. Do not skin, eat or
  throw dead pigs into rivers; bury them deep as required or hand them over for proper
  disposal (see the link below)
⚠️ Do not use so-called "African swine fever vaccines" of unknown origin — as of 2026 China
   has only candidate vaccines in clinical trials and no approved product on the market;
   illegal vaccines may actually spread new strains

African swine fever does not infect people, but it can kill almost every pig in an area.
```

For disposal of dead pigs see [Sanitation – Disposing of dead animals](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/03-卫生设施.md#病死动物处理).

```
Others:

Foot-and-mouth disease:
- Pigs, cattle, sheep and goats all get it. Blisters on the mouth, tongue, coronary band
  and between the claws; once they burst the animal goes lame, drools and stops eating
- Spreads extremely fast; vaccinate as national rules require. If you suspect it, report
  at once; do not treat or sell the animals yourself

Fluids for piglet diarrhoea:
- The greatest danger in scouring piglets is dehydration: skin loses its elasticity, eyes
  sink, the piglet cannot stand
- Keep the piglets suckling, and also give clean warm water or oral rehydration salts
  (ORS) solution to drink freely
- Yellow or white scour is most common from the first few days to 3 weeks of age, and is
  often linked to chilling, a wet pen or dirty teats
  → Keeping them warm, bedding with dry straw and washing the sow's teats and udder clean
     often clears it up
- Whole litters scouring, blood in the droppings, many piglets dying, or vomiting as well
  (possibly transmissible gastroenteritis or porcine epidemic diarrhoea) → call a vet

Parasites:
- Roundworm: thinness, poor growth, coughing; worm them (see above)
- Mange mites: intense itching, rubbing against walls, crusted thickened skin; isolate,
  treat as the label says, and clean the pen at the same time

Streptococcus suis infection:
- Sudden high fever, swollen joints, staggering, walking in circles, or sudden death
- Infects people! Wear gloves when handling sick or dead pigs, do not touch them if you
  have a cut on your hand, and never eat pigs that were sick or died
  (see Diseases passed from animals to people)
```

## Sheep and goats

### Why keep sheep and goats?

```
Advantages:
✓ Live mainly on grass (low cost)
✓ Adaptable
✓ Disease-resistant
✓ Breed well
✓ Meat, wool, milk

Suitable when you have:
- Grassland or hill land
- Extensive (low-input) management
- Small-scale keeping
```

```
Differences between goats and sheep:
- Goats: like tree leaves and tender shrub shoots, can climb steep slopes, tolerate coarse
  feed; produce meat, milk and cashmere
  → They gnaw bark and destroy young trees; graze them well away from new plantations
    and orchards
- Sheep: eat mainly grass, prefer gentle grassland, docile and flock together; produce
  wool, meat and milk
  → Dislike heat and damp; in low, wet places they easily get foot rot and parasites
```

### Breeds

**Meat breeds**:
- Boer goat, Dorper sheep (imported)
- Small-tailed Han sheep (local, very prolific)

**Dairy breeds**:
- Saanen dairy goat

**Wool breeds**:
- Merino sheep

**Local breeds**:
- Adaptable; recommended

### Building a sheep house

```
Site:
✓ High, dry ground
✓ Facing the sun, sheltered from the wind
✓ Close to pasture

Sheep house:
Area: 1.5-2 m² per animal (indoors)
Height: 2.5-3 m
Floor: dry, slightly sloped
Ventilation: good
Areas: adults, lambs, lambing pens

Exercise yard:
- Outside, 2-3 times the floor area
- With hay racks, water troughs and a sun shade
```

```
More:
- Sheep and goats fear damp, not cold: "sheep lie where it is dry". A damp floor harms them
  more than winter cold (foot rot, parasites, pneumonia)
- Keeping damp out: raise and ram the floor and cover it with dry straw or slats; or build a
  slatted wooden floor about 0.5-1 m off the ground
  (slats about 3-5 cm wide with gaps of about 1.5-2 cm: droppings fall through, hooves do
  not get stuck)
- Area in detail: adult ewe about 1.5-2 m², ewe with lambs about 2-2.5 m², breeding ram about
  2-3 m² (in a separate pen), weaned lambs about 0.5-0.8 m²
- Hay racks: let the animals pull hay through the bars, so they do not trample it
  (they will not eat soiled hay, and a lot is wasted)
- Predators: shut them in at night in a house with strong fences and doors; wolves and dogs
  are the main threat
- Lambing pens: separate small pens, about 1.5 m × 1.5 m per ewe (a "mother-and-lamb pen"),
  sheltered from wind and warm in winter, bedded with clean dry straw
```

### Sheep feed and rations

**Roughly how much one animal eats per day** (example: an adult of about 50 kg):

| Feed | Per day, roughly | Notes |
|------|---------|------|
| Fresh green grass | 5-7 kg | Grazed by the animal itself; about 10%-15% of body weight |
| Or hay | 1.5-2 kg | About 3%-4% of body weight |
| Concentrates (maintenance) | None or 0.1-0.2 kg | Not needed when grass is good |
| Concentrates (late pregnancy, lactation) | 0.3-0.5 kg | Upper end for ewes carrying twins |
| Salt | 5-10 g | Salt lick to take freely, or mixed into feed |
| Water | 3-6 L | More during lactation, in hot weather and on hay |

```
Storing fodder for winter:
- In the north the housed period is about 4-5 months; prepare about 200-300 kg of hay per
  animal (or an equivalent amount of straw plus silage)
- Hay: cut grass at early flowering and dry it until it snaps when bent and has no damp
  left before stacking; raise the base and put a roof on top against rain
  → Hay stacked before it is dry heats up and goes mouldy; mouldy hay causes abortion and
    poisoning
- Straw (maize stalks, bean straw, peanut haulm): chop to about 2-3 cm before feeding; bean
  straw and peanut haulm are more nutritious than cereal straw

Silage (if you can manage it):
- Chop green maize stalks and the like to about 1-2 cm, pack into a pit or plastic bags,
  tread down layer by layer to drive out the air, and seal completely
- Ready after about 40-60 days; it should smell pleasantly sour. Do not feed silage that is
  black, foul-smelling or mouldy

Changing feed: when moving from grazing to housing, or from green grass to hay, change over
               gradually across 7-10 days; a sudden change easily causes diarrhoea and bloat
```

### Feeding and management

**Feed**:
```
Mainly grass:
✓ Grazing (best, low cost)
✓ Hay (winter)
✓ Silage
✓ Crop straw

Supplementary concentrates:
- Maize, wheat bran, soybean meal
- In late pregnancy and lactation, and as creep feed for lambs

Minerals:
- Salt, bone meal
- Salt lick to take freely
```

**Feeding**:
```
Grazing:
- Out early, back late
- In summer avoid the midday heat
- In winter graze around midday
- Drive them slowly
- Do not let them eat poisonous plants or dew-wet grass (causes bloat)

Housed:
- Feed hay morning and evening
- Extra feed at night
- Roughage before concentrates
- Little and often
- Plenty of water
```

```
More on grazing:
- Rotational grazing: divide the pasture into several plots and graze them in turn, moving
  on after a few days so the grass can regrow (come back after about 3-6 weeks). This also
  breaks the parasite cycle (eggs drop onto the grass in droppings, and the larvae take a
  while to become infective)
- Do not graze low, waterlogged places with lots of small snails: they easily pass on liver
  fluke
- When legume forage (lucerne, clover) is young and wet with dew or rain, give some hay
  first before turning them out; do not let hungry animals gorge
  → Prevents rumen bloat
- Poisonous plants: know the common local ones (such as oleander, rhododendron, castor
  seeds, sprouting or frost-bitten sorghum and maize seedlings); watch carefully on new
  pasture. Hungry animals are the most likely to eat poisonous plants
- Do not let them eat plastic bags or bits of string: these build up in the stomach and
  block it
```

**Breeding**:
```
Mating:
- Ewes/does at 8-10 months
- Rams/bucks at 1-1.5 years
- Natural mating or artificial insemination

Pregnancy:
- 150 days (5 months)

Lambing:
- Mostly single lambs; some breeds have twins or more
- Prepare for the birth (lambing pen, warmth)

Suckling:
- Get colostrum into them early (ideally within 1 hour of birth, no later than 2 hours)
- Mother's milk or milk replacer
- Wean at 2-3 months
```

```
Recognising heat:
- Sheep have an oestrous cycle of about 17 days, goats about 21 days; each heat lasts about
  1-2 days
- Signs: tail wagging, red swollen vulva with mucus, calling, off feed, seeking out the male,
  accepting mounting (obvious in goats, less so in sheep, which are often detected with a
  "teaser" ram)
- Mate by weight: wait until the ewe reaches about 70% of adult weight; mating her too small
  leads to more difficult births

Mating:
- Mate within about 12-24 hours after heat begins, and again about 12 hours later
- One ram can serve about 20-30 ewes by natural mating (give him extra concentrates in the
  breeding season)
- Change breeding rams every few years; do not mate a ram with his own daughters
  (inbreeding gives weak, deformed offspring)

Planning the breeding season:
- Aim for lambs born in spring (early spring), when it is warming up, grass is growing and
  ewes give more milk
- Pregnancy is about 150 days, so count back: for lambing in early March, mate around
  October the year before
```

**Daily management**:
```
✓ Clean the house regularly
✓ Ventilated and dry
✓ Trim hooves (regularly)
✓ Shear (spring)
✓ Worm (once in spring, once in autumn)
✓ Vaccinate (foot-and-mouth disease, sheep pox and others)
✓ Watch their health
```

### Lambing and lamb care

```
Late pregnancy (last 6 weeks):
- The foetuses grow fastest; give extra concentrates (see the feed table)
- Underfed ewes carrying twins easily get "pregnancy toxaemia": dull, leaving the flock,
  unsteady, grinding teeth, blundering about as if blind, usually 1-3 weeks before lambing
  → Prevent it by feeding enough; if it happens, call a vet
- Avoid crowding, hurried driving and jumping ditches

Signs that lambing is near:
- Udder swollen, colostrum can be squeezed out; vulva slack and swollen; belly drops,
  hollows appear in the flanks
- Leaves the flock, paws the ground, gets up and lies down restlessly, keeps looking round
  at her belly
- Move ewes showing signs of lambing into a lambing pen

Normal birth:
- First a water bag appears, then two front feet (soles down) with the nose resting on the
  front legs — like a diver's position. This is a normal presentation
- After the waters break the lamb usually comes within about 30 minutes to 1 hour; a second
  twin often follows about 10-30 minutes later
- If the presentation is normal, do not interfere; let the ewe lamb by herself

Helping at the birth:
1. As soon as the lamb is born, clear the mucus from its mouth and nose. If it is not
   breathing, hold it upside down by the hind legs and pat its chest, or tickle a nostril
   with a straw to make it sneeze
2. Let the ewe lick the lamb dry (this helps them bond); in cold weather help by drying it
   with straw or a cloth
3. The cord usually breaks by itself; paint the stump with iodine tincture. If it is too
   long, tie thread about 4-5 cm from the belly and cut it
4. Help the lamb find a teat and drink colostrum as early as possible, ideally within
   1 hour, no later than 2 hours; on the first day it should drink about 10%-20% of its body
   weight in colostrum in total, in several feeds
5. The afterbirth usually comes within a few hours; take it away and bury it deep. If it
   has not come after about 12 hours, call a vet

When help is needed (call an experienced person or a vet):
⚠️ Straining hard for more than about 30-60 minutes with no lamb
⚠️ Only one front foot showing, the head showing without front feet, or the tail or hind
   feet coming first (malpresentation)
⚠️ The lamb is stuck and the ewe is exhausted
When helping: cut your nails, wash and disinfect your hands, wear long gloves, use a
             lubricant (vegetable oil or soapy water), and pull gently downwards and
             backwards along the birth canal while the ewe strains. Correcting the
             presentation needs experience; do not pull by force
⚠️ Always wear gloves at lambing: the afterbirth and birth fluids may carry Brucella
   (see Diseases passed from animals to people)
⚠️ If a ewe aborts (especially several in a flock one after another): do not touch the
   foetus or afterbirth with bare hands; bag them, bury deep and cover with lime; isolate
   the ewe and report to a vet for brucellosis testing

Lamb care:
- Chilled lambs: lamb is cold and cannot stand → warm it first (put it in water at about
  38-40°C keeping the head out, or hold it against your body); once warm, dry it and then
  give colostrum. If it is too weak to suck, feed it slowly from a bottle; do not let milk
  go down the windpipe
- Ewe rejects the lamb or has too little milk: drape the skin of a dead lamb over the orphan
  so another ewe accepts it, or hand-rear it
  (hand-rearing: colostrum first, then goat's or cow's milk warmed to about 38°C, little and
  often; wash and scald the feeding utensils every time)
- From about 7-10 days old let lambs nibble tender grass and a little concentrate so they
  learn to eat
- Wean at 2-3 months; at weaning take the ewe away and leave the lambs in their familiar pen
- Male lambs not kept for breeding: have an experienced person castrate them at about
  1-2 weeks old; disinfect and guard against tetanus
```

### Shearing and hoof trimming

**Shearing** (sheep, usually once a year):
```
When:
- Late spring once the weather has settled and warmed (in the north about April-June).
  Too early and they get cold; too late and the sheep suffer from heat and the wool starts
  to shed by itself
- Do not shear in rain or when the wool is wet (wet wool goes mouldy in storage); stop
  feeding about half a day before shearing, as an empty sheep is easier to handle

How:
1. Sweep the area and lay down a sheet; finish one sheep before starting the next
2. Sit the sheep up with its back against your legs (a sheep sitting up hardly struggles).
   Shear the belly first, then the legs, then from one side over the back to the other side
3. Push the shears flat along the skin, leaving about 0.5-1 cm of stubble. Try to cut right
   through in one pass and do not make second cuts (second cuts are short and useless)
4. Where the skin is wrinkled (neck, back of the legs) stretch it flat before cutting, so
   you do not nick it
5. Paint any cuts with iodine tincture; watch sheep with many cuts for fly strike
6. Spread out each whole fleece, remove dung lumps and soiled wool, roll it up, bag it and
   keep it in a dry place

Afterwards:
- For about 1 week after shearing keep the sheep out of rain and cold
- About 2 weeks after shearing you can dip them or treat external parasites (the wool is
  short then, so treatment works well)
- A local sheep gives about 1-3 kg of wool a year; fine-wool sheep give more

Goat cashmere: combed out with a fine-toothed comb in spring, not sheared
```

For washing and spinning wool see [Textiles, Mending and Tanning – Spinning](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/07-纺织缝补与鞣皮.md#纺线).

**Hoof trimming**:
```
Why: in housed animals, or those on soft ground, the hoof wall keeps growing and curls
     inwards, trapping mud and dung → lameness, foot rot
How often: check and trim housed animals about every 2-3 months; animals grazing on stony
           ground wear their hooves down and need less trimming

How:
1. Hooves are softer and easier to trim after rain or after standing on wet grass
2. Restrain the animal (sitting up or lying on its side), lift the foot, and clean the mud
   and dung out of the sole with a small knife or pick
3. With hoof shears or a sharp knife, cut away the overgrown edge of the hoof wall and trim
   the sole flat, parallel to the coronary band
4. Trim a little at a time; stop when the colour turns pink (any further will bleed). If it
   bleeds, press to stop it and apply iodine tincture

Foot rot:
- Red, swollen, foul-smelling, pus-filled skin between the claws; the animal limps or grazes
  on its knees
- Trim away the dead horn, wash, and move the animal to dry ground.
  Stand the feet in about 10% zinc sulphate solution (first choice, less toxic) or about
  5%-10% copper sulphate solution
  (make a shallow trough, let the animal stand in it for about 10-20 minutes, then stand on
  dry ground to dry off; do not let it drink the solution).
  Call a vet for severe cases
- The real cure is still a dry house and yard
```

### Disease prevention and treatment

```
Prevention:
✓ Cleanliness
✓ Ventilated and dry
✓ Vaccination
✓ Regular worming
✓ Balanced nutrition

Common diseases:
- Parasites: worm regularly
- Diarrhoea: clean drinking water, hygiene
- Foot-and-mouth disease: prevent with vaccine
- Sheep pox: vaccine, isolate sick animals
```

```
Health checks:
- A healthy animal: stays with the flock, bright eyes, chews the cud normally (lies chewing
  after grazing, several hours a day), droppings are firm small pellets
- Temperature: normal about 38.5-40°C (goats slightly higher)
- Eyelid check (for anaemia from parasites): turn down the lower eyelid; normally it is pink.
  Pale or even white = severe anaemia, usually from barber's pole worm (Haemonchus, a
  blood-sucking stomach worm); worm at once
- Swelling under the jaw ("bottle jaw") and thinness: also common with severe parasite burden

Worming:
- Usually once in spring and once in autumn, with an extra dose in the grazing season when
  worms are heavy; dose by label and body weight, and compost the droppings for a few days
  afterwards
- Using the same wormer over and over makes worms resistant → rotate between different
  classes of wormer, and combine with rotational grazing
- External parasites (lice, mites, ticks): dip after shearing, or apply treatment as the
  label says

Foot-and-mouth disease: see Disease prevention and treatment under pigs; cattle, sheep and
                        goats are equally susceptible

Sheep pox:
- Temperature rises, discharge from eyes and nose, then on areas with little wool (around
  the eyes, lips, udder, belly, under the tail) red patches → bumps → blisters → scabs
- Highly contagious; vaccinate on schedule. Isolate sick animals at once, report to a vet,
  thoroughly disinfect pens and equipment they used; bury dead animals deep
- People handling sick animals occasionally get pox sores on their hands (similar to
  "orf"); wear gloves when handling sick animals

Peste des petits ruminants ("goat plague"):
- High fever, mouth ulcers, runny eyes and nose, severe diarrhoea; high death rate in goats
- A disease under compulsory national vaccination. If you suspect it, report at once,
  quarantine, and do not sell animals

Clostridial diseases (braxy, enterotoxaemia and others):
- Often sudden death without visible signs, especially at the change of seasons or after
  suddenly eating a lot of concentrates or lush young grass
- Prevent with a multivalent clostridial vaccine; limit concentrates and change feed
  gradually

Rumen bloat (emergency):
- After eating a lot of dew-wet legume forage or mouldy feed, the left flank (the hollow at
  the back of the left side of the belly) swells up quickly and sounds like a drum when
  tapped; the animal is restless and breathes with its mouth open; severe cases soon
  suffocate and die
- Stop feeding at once; stand the animal on a slope, front end higher, head up;
  tie a stick crosswise in its mouth (ends tied with string behind the head) to make it
  belch; massage the left side gently and walk it slowly
- If it does not improve or is severe: call a vet at once (releasing gas with a needle must
  be done by a professional; in the wrong place it damages internal organs)
- Prevention: see the grazing points

Grain overload (acidosis):
- If an animal steals a lot of grain, within a few hours to a day it stops eating, has
  diarrhoea and is unsteady → call a vet
- Keep grain store and feed room doors shut

Hydatid disease: see Diseases passed from animals to people (linked to dogs; infects people)
```

## Rabbits

### Why keep rabbits?

```
Advantages:
✓ Extremely prolific (several litters a year)
✓ Grow fast (ready for slaughter in 3-4 months)
✓ Need little space
✓ Low investment
✓ Good feed conversion
✓ Mainly eat plants (low cost)
✓ Rabbit meat is nutritious
✓ Rabbit droppings are an excellent fertiliser

Very well suited to small-scale household keeping!
```

**After a disaster**: rabbits do not compete with people for grain; grass cut around the house is enough to raise them. A litter of several kits is ready to eat in two or three months, making rabbits the quickest way for a household to have a steady supply of meat. But rabbit meat is very lean. Eating only rabbit, without fat and other foods, leads to malnutrition ("rabbit starvation"); eat it with grain and fat (see [Nutrition and Deficiency Diseases](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/02-营养与营养缺乏病.md)).

### Breeds

**Meat rabbits**:
- New Zealand, Californian, Belgian rabbits
- Local meat rabbits

**Wool rabbits**:
- Angora

**Fur rabbits**:
- Rex

### Building a rabbit house

**Cages** (recommended):
```
Cage sizes:
- Adult rabbit: 60×50×40 cm (length × width × height)
- Doe: 70×60×40 cm (needs a nest box)

Materials:
- Wooden or wire-mesh cages
- Mesh floor (so droppings do not build up)

Placing:
- Indoors or under cover
- Well ventilated
- Out of direct sunlight
- 30-50 cm off the ground
```

```
Details:
- Floor: bamboo slats or galvanised wire mesh; bamboo slats about 2-2.5 cm wide with gaps of
  about 1-1.2 cm. Gaps too wide trap and break rabbits' feet; wire too thin causes sore hocks
  ("sore hocks" = hair loss and ulcers on the soles of the feet)
  → Put a board in the cage for the rabbit to rest its feet
- Rabbits gnaw wooden cages; cover the outside of wooden bars with sheet iron or wire mesh
- Put a manure channel or a sloping droppings board under the cage for easy cleaning; urine
  gives off a lot of ammonia, so clear droppings and urine every day
- Nest box: a wooden box about 40 × 25 × 20 cm with a hinged lid on top and an entrance at
  the front about 10 cm above the floor of the box (so kits do not crawl out too early);
  drill a few small holes in the bottom against damp
- Temperature: rabbits do best at about 15-25°C; above about 30°C they easily die of
  heatstroke, which is more dangerous than cold
  → In summer give shade and ventilation, sprinkle water on the floor, and put a bottle of
    frozen water in the cage for the rabbit to lean against
- Keep out rats, cats, dogs and snakes: rats kill kits and spread disease
```

**Free-range indoors or in pens**:
- Need more space
- Harder to keep clean
- Rabbits kept on the ground dig burrows and escape, and catch coccidiosis more easily (they eat droppings on the ground); bury mesh at the bottom of the fence and keep the ground dry

### Feeding and management

**Feed**:
```
Main feed:
✓ Green grass and green vegetables (staple)
✓ Hay (always available; wears down teeth)
✓ Carrots, Chinese cabbage and the like

Concentrates:
- Rabbit feed (pellets)
- Or mix your own: maize, wheat bran, soybean meal

Do not:
❌ Dew-wet grass (causes bloat)
❌ Rotten or spoiled feed
❌ Sudden changes of feed (change gradually)

Note:
- Better dry than wet (mostly hay; little watery feed)
- But always give drinking water

Saying: "Rabbits eat a hundred kinds of grass — if you go and find them"
```

```
Common plants rabbits like, and what not to feed:
✓ Good feed: wild grasses, lucerne, clover, dandelion, plantain, Indian lettuce (Ixeris),
  sweet-potato vines, peanut haulm, mulberry leaves, black locust (Robinia) leaves, elm
  leaves; in autumn and winter hay and dried tree leaves
✓ Root crops such as carrot, sweet potato and pumpkin in moderation, as juicy winter feed
❌ Poisonous or harmful: oleander, rhododendron, thorn apple (Datura), hemlock, castor
   leaves, potato sprouts and green skin, sprouted potatoes, frost-bitten vegetable leaves,
   grass and vegetables recently sprayed with pesticide
❌ Do not feed plants you do not know; do not cut grass from roadsides or places sprayed with
   herbicide

Fibre matters:
- Rabbits digest fibre in the caecum; too little hay and coarse fibre causes diarrhoea and
  bloat. Do not feed only concentrates and juicy greens

Eating droppings (caecotrophs):
- Every early morning rabbits eat a kind of soft, shiny little dropping straight from the
  anus. This is normal: it contains B vitamins and protein. Do not stop it
```

**Feeding**:
```
- Feed morning and evening
- Extra feed at night (rabbits feed at night)
- Fixed times and amounts
- Little and often
- Clean drinking water (automatic drinker)

Amount:
- Adult rabbit: about 300-500 g of fresh green grass + about 50-100 g of concentrates a day
```

```
More:
- Grass only, no concentrates: an adult rabbit needs about 0.5-1 kg of fresh grass a day
  (about 15%-25% of body weight) plus hay to eat freely; it grows more slowly, but can be raised
- Does in late pregnancy and lactation need far more: a nursing doe may eat more than twice
  her usual amount; increase concentrates to about 150-200 g
- Water: adult rabbits about 0.3-0.5 L a day, nursing does about 1 L; always give water when
  feeding hay and pellets
  → A doe short of water after kindling may eat her kits
- Spread out grass cut on rainy days and let the surface water dry before feeding
```

**Breeding**:
```
Mating:
- Does at 5-6 months
- Bucks at 6-7 months

Pregnancy: 31 days

Kindling:
- 6-10 kits a litter
- Prepare a nest box (hay and rabbit fur for warmth)
- Check that the doe is nursing

Nursing:
- Mother's milk for 30-40 days
- Gradually add feed
- Separate into cages after weaning

Productivity:
- 5-6 litters a year!
- Numbers grow very fast
```

```
Recognising heat:
- Rabbits have no fixed oestrous cycle; mating triggers ovulation (induced ovulation)
- Look at the vulva: pale and dry = not in heat; pink = just starting; bright red, moist and
  swollen = best time to mate; purplish-black = heat nearly over
  Saying: "Pink is early, purple-black is late, bright red is just right"
- The doe is restless, rubs her chin on the cage, pushes hay about, eats less

Mating:
1. Put the doe into the buck's cage (not the other way round: a buck in a strange place
   spends his time sniffing around first)
2. Normally it is over within a few minutes; after mating the buck falls to the side or
   backwards and gives a cry
3. After mating take the doe out and gently lift her hindquarters for ten seconds or so, so
   the semen does not run out
4. You can mate her again about 6-8 hours later
- One buck can serve about 8-10 does; no more than 1-2 matings a day, 2 days on and 1 day off
- Do not mate close relatives among breeding rabbits; bring in a buck from elsewhere every
  few years

Pregnancy check:
- About 10-12 days after mating, put the doe on a flat surface and gently feel both sides of
  the lower belly with one hand. A string of slippery, springy lumps the size of peanuts or
  grapes = pregnant
- Be gentle; do not squeeze hard (it can cause abortion). If you feel nothing, mate her again
```

**Daily management**:
```
✓ Clear droppings every day
✓ Keep dry and clean
✓ Ventilate (rabbits dislike damp and stuffy heat)
✓ Protect from heat in summer (shade, ventilation)
✓ Keep warm in winter (shelter from wind; no need for high temperatures, rabbits tolerate cold)
✓ Watch the droppings (normally round pellets)
✓ Watch appetite and behaviour
```

```
More:
- Picking up a rabbit: grasp the skin behind the ears at the scruff with one hand and support
  the rump with the other; never lift by the ears or hind legs alone (it injures the rabbit)
- Teeth: rabbit teeth grow all the time, so always give hay and twigs (willow, poplar, fruit
  tree) to gnaw. Front teeth that grow too long jam the mouth so the rabbit cannot eat; they
  must be clipped (by an experienced person)
- Nail trimming: in caged rabbits overlong nails curl and snag; clip them about every
  2-3 months, avoiding the blood vessel inside
- Wool rabbits: shear (or pluck) Angoras about every 2-3 months; keep them warm afterwards
```

### Kindling and kit care

```
Before kindling:
- About 27-28 days after mating put in a nest box lined with hay
- 1-2 days before kindling the doe carries hay to build a nest and pulls fur from her chest
  and belly to line it — this is normal
- She must have plenty of water
- Keep it quiet; for a few days before and after kindling keep strangers, dogs and cats away

Kindling:
- Mostly at night or early morning, usually over within about half an hour
- The doe bites through the cords, eats the afterbirths and licks the kits dry herself; she
  usually needs no help
- If a first-time doe has not pulled fur for the nest, gently pluck some of her belly fur
  and put it in the nest (this also exposes her teats)

Check after kindling (the next day):
- Lure the doe away first (give her some green grass) and rub some of the nest's hay and fur
  on your hands (to remove the human smell) before checking the nest
- Remove dead kits, wet hay and remains of afterbirth; count the kits
- Well-fed kits: round, full bellies, rosy skin, lying quietly in a heap
- Hungry kits: flat bellies, wrinkled skin, crawling about and squeaking
  → The doe may be short of milk or unwilling to nurse; put her into the box once a day to
     nurse (a doe nurses only 1-2 times a day for a few minutes each time; this is normal)

Fostering:
- If a litter is too big (more kits than the doe has teats, usually 8) or the doe is short of
  milk, move the extra kits to another litter born at the same time (within 1-2 days); leave
  them in the new nest for a while first so they take on its smell

Kit growth:
- Eyes open at about 10-12 days; they start leaving the nest to eat at about 18-20 days
- As soon as they come out of the nest, put in a little tender grass and concentrate so they
  learn to eat
- Wean at 30-40 days: ideally take the doe away first and leave the kits in their cage for a
  few days, then separate them (less stress and diarrhoea)
- Around weaning is the most dangerous time in a rabbit's life (coccidiosis, diarrhoea);
  keep feed clean and change it slowly

Does that eat their kits or will not nurse:
- Common causes: shortage of water, shortage of salt/protein, fright, strange smells in the
  nest, first-time doe
- What to do: make sure of water and feed; keep it quiet; cull a doe that eats two litters
  in a row
```

### Disease prevention and treatment

```
Prevention:
✓ Clean and dry!
✓ Ventilation
✓ Clear droppings often
✓ Disinfect feeding utensils
✓ Vaccination
✓ Isolate sick rabbits

Common diseases:
Diarrhoea:
- Dirty food, chilling, changing feed too fast
- Stop green feed, give hay, keep warm
- Oral antibacterials

Coccidiosis:
- Common in young rabbits; serious!
- Prevention first (medication, hygiene)
- Signs: diarrhoea, wasting, death

Mange:
- Skin disease, hair loss
- Isolate and treat, apply medication

⚠️ Rabbit diseases strike fast; prevention is what counts!
```

```
More:

Rabbit haemorrhagic disease (the most dangerous):
- A viral disease; often sudden death with no warning. Some rabbits convulse and scream
  before dying, with bloody froth from the nostrils
- A whole group can die within a few days; mostly affects rabbits over 2 months old
- No specific treatment; only vaccine works. Follow the vaccine instructions: usually first
  dose at about 35-40 days old, then a booster about every six months. Ask about vaccination
  before bringing in breeding rabbits
- If it breaks out: bury dead rabbits deep or burn them; do not eat, sell or skin them;
  thoroughly disinfect cages with flame or disinfectant; report to the local animal husbandry
  and veterinary department
- Wild rabbits, rats, flies, and people's shoes and clothes can all bring in the virus →
  keep outsiders out of the rabbit house

Coccidiosis:
- Commonest from around weaning to 3 months old: diarrhoea, swollen belly, wasting, high
  death rate
- The key is to stop rabbits eating their own droppings: cage floors that let droppings fall
  through, clearing droppings every day, keeping feed and water free of droppings, not
  feeding grass on the ground
- Around weaning add an anticoccidial to feed or water as the label says to prevent it;
  keep to the withdrawal period

Pasteurellosis (snuffles, pneumonia):
- Sneezing, runny nose, the rabbit wipes its nose with its front paws (fur on the inside of
  the front paws matted); severe cases die of pneumonia
- Common with poor ventilation and a strong ammonia smell; isolate sick rabbits, cull those
  that do not recover after a long time

Heatstroke:
- In hot weather the rabbit breathes fast, ears red and hot, lies flat without moving
- Move it to a cool, airy place and dab cool water on its ears; prevention matters more than
  treatment (see the rabbit house details)

Bloat:
- After eating dew-wet grass, mouldy or frozen feed, or a lot of beans: swollen belly, not
  eating, grinding teeth
- Stop juicy feed, give only hay and water, gently massage the belly, and let the rabbit
  move about

Can people catch anything?
- Rabbit mange mites and skin fungus (ringworm) can pass to people: wear gloves when handling
  sick rabbits and wash your hands
- Wild hares may carry tularaemia; take care when skinning them; see Diseases passed from
  animals to people
```

### Costs and returns

```
Household keeping (10 does + 2 bucks):

Inputs (first year):
- Breeding rabbits: 600-1000 yuan
- Cages: 500-800 yuan
- Feed: about 6000-9000 yuan/year
  (raising 300-400 meat rabbits to 2.5 kg needs about 3 t of feed; cutting your own grass
  saves some of this)
- Vaccines, medicines, other: about 500 yuan
Total: about 7600-11300 yuan

Output:
- Each doe produces 30-40 kits a year (on average 6 litters × 6 kits)
- 10 does produce 300-400 a year; at about 80% survival, 240-320 go to slaughter
- Meat rabbits at slaughter: 2-2.5 kg (live weight)
- Total live weight: about 500-800 kg
- Price: 20 yuan/kg (live rabbits; fluctuates widely)
- Income: about 10000-16000 yuan

Net profit: about 0-8000 yuan in the first year; after that you save the cost of breeding
            stock and cages

Notes:
- Returns depend heavily on survival rate, feed prices and market outlets
- Eat some, sell some
- Takes a fair amount of labour (feeding, cleaning)
```

## Cattle, donkeys and other draught animals

Ploughing, pulling carts and turning millstones need large animals. For their strength, harness, and care while working see [Vehicles, Boats and Roads – Animal power and harness](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/18-车船与道路.md#畜力与挽具) and [Vehicles, Boats and Roads – Caring for draught animals](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/18-车船与道路.md#牲畜的照料); this section covers day-to-day keeping.

```
Think first about whether to keep one:
- One head of cattle eats about 3-4 t of hay a year (or the equivalent in green grass or
  straw), and also needs housing and daily care
- A household with little farmland and no pasture often cannot afford a large animal;
  several households sharing one and taking turns to use it is better value
- For beginners: a donkey (easy to keep, tolerates coarse feed) or a docile castrated ox
  first; horses need better feed and are more delicate
```

### Keeping cattle: key points

```
How much they eat (example: a draught yellow cattle of about 400 kg):
- About 30-40 kg of fresh grass a day, or about 8-10 kg of hay/straw a day (about 2%-2.5% of
  body weight)
- On working days add about 1-2 kg of concentrates (maize, soybean cake, wheat bran); start
  adding 2-3 weeks before the busy farming season to build up condition
- Water about 30-50 L a day, more in hot weather and when working; water buffalo also need to
  soak in water or wallow in mud to cool down
- Salt about 30-60 g a day; salt lick to take freely
- Chop straw short (about 3-5 cm), ideally mixed with green grass and a little concentrate;
  on dry straw alone they get thinner and thinner

Note:
- Cattle are ruminants: after eating they need to lie down quietly and chew the cud. Do not
  work them straight after feeding
- Preventing bloat: as for sheep, do not let hungry cattle gorge on dew-wet young legumes
- No wire, nails or scraps of iron in the fodder: cattle do not chew carefully, and metal
  that pierces the stomach wall causes traumatic reticulitis (hardware disease: not eating,
  arched back, reluctant to walk downhill), and can even pierce the heart sac and kill
  → Pick metal out when chopping fodder, and store fodder away from scrap iron
- Cattle house: about 4-6 m² per head, floor dry and non-slip, with bedding;
  when tethering, leave the rope long enough for the animal to lie down and get up freely
- Hoof trimming: check housed cattle about every six months; trim if hooves are misshapen
  or the animal is lame
```

```
Breeding:
- Oestrous cycle about 21 days; heat is very short (about half a day to a day): restless,
  bellowing, mounting other cattle. Standing still when mounted by another animal is the
  most reliable sign; clear mucus runs from the vulva
- Timing of mating: the saying goes "in heat in the morning, mate in the afternoon; in heat
  in the afternoon, mate the next morning"
- Heifers first mated at about 15-18 months, when they reach about 2/3 of adult weight
- Pregnancy: yellow cattle about 280 days (about nine and a half months), water buffalo
  about 300-330 days (varies with breed)
- Calving: normal presentation as in sheep (two front feet plus the nose). If there is no
  progress about 2 hours after the waters break, or the calf is malpresented, call a vet
- Calf: colostrum within 1 hour of birth, about 10% of body weight in total on the first day;
  paint the cord with iodine tincture; keep the calf pen dry
- The cow can do light work from a dozen or so days after calving; no heavy work while she is
  nursing

Working:
- Start training cattle at about 2-3 years old, beginning with an empty cart or a light
  plough and increasing the load gradually
- For daily working and rest times see caring for draught animals in Vehicles, Boats and
  Roads; avoid midday in summer
- Bulls not kept for breeding become docile after castration and suit draught work (have an
  experienced person or a vet do it)

⚠️ Safety: bulls and cows with calves may attack suddenly; cattle kick backwards, so do not
   stand directly behind them. Lead cattle by a nose ring or halter, and never wrap the lead
   rope around your hand
```

### Keeping donkeys, horses and mules: key points

```
The biggest difference from cattle: equines do not chew the cud and have small stomachs, so
they cannot eat much at once. What they fear most is colic (abdominal pain from intestinal
blockage)

How much they eat (example: a donkey of about 200 kg):
- About 3-5 kg of hay or chopped straw a day (about 1.5%-2.5% of body weight); on working
  days add about 0.5-1 kg of concentrates
- Estimate for horses in the same way by body weight, but for heavy work they need much more
  concentrate (about 2-4 kg for a horse)
- Water about 15-30 L a day (donkey); horse about 30-50 L a day
- Salt: donkey about 20-30 g a day, horse about 30-60 g a day; salt lick to take freely

How to feed (the old sayings make good sense):
- "Chop each inch of straw three times, and even without grain they put on flesh": chop
  fodder very short (about 2-3 cm); donkeys and horses eat more and digest it better
- "A horse without night fodder never gets fat": little and often, with an extra feed of
  fodder at night; do not fill them up at one meal
- Fodder first, then concentrates; damp the concentrates with water
- After work, tie them in the shade to rest for about half an hour; water and feed them only
  once the sweat has dried and they have got their breath back
- Change feed gradually over about 1-2 weeks

Preventing colic (the commonest emergency in horses, mules and donkeys):
- Causes: sudden change of feed, fodder too coarse or long, too little water, mouldy or
  frozen feed, a full meal straight after hard work
- Signs: not eating, looking round at the belly, pawing, lying down and rolling, sweating,
  few or no droppings
- What to do: stop feeding at once; lead the animal slowly round (so it does not roll and
  twist its gut) and call a vet. Do not drench it with oil or medicine (if it goes down the
  windpipe it can kill), and do not force it to work
- Prevention: chop fodder short, feed evenly, give enough water, never feed mouldy fodder

Hooves: trim about every 1-2 months; animals working on hard roads need shoeing (by an
        experienced farrier); see Vehicles, Boats and Roads
Teeth: old donkeys and horses whose teeth have worn sharp and uneven eat poorly and lose
       weight; have an experienced person rasp them level

Breeding:
- Pregnancy: donkey about 12 months (about 360 days), horse about 11 months (about 340 days);
  jennies and mares are usually first mated at about 3 years old
- Oestrous cycle about 21 days, heat lasts about 4-7 days; mate from the 2nd-3rd day of heat,
  and again 1-2 days later
- Mules are crosses between donkeys and horses and are almost all infertile; they can only
  be bred by crossing donkeys and horses
```

**Livestock zoonoses**: like sheep and goats, cattle can pass on brucellosis and anthrax, and cattle can also pass on tuberculosis (through raw milk); see [Diseases passed from animals to people](#diseases-passed-from-animals-to-people). Wounds, droppings and soil around horses, donkeys and mules often carry tetanus bacteria; people caring for these animals should pay attention to [tetanus prevention](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/02-外伤处理.md#破伤风预防) after any injury.

## Disease prevention and vaccination principles

```
Vaccination (when vaccines are available):
✓ Vaccinate according to the schedule set by the local animal husbandry and veterinary
  department. Highly pathogenic avian influenza, foot-and-mouth disease, peste des petits
  ruminants and others are under compulsory national vaccination; brucellosis is under
  compulsory vaccination in the vaccination zones each province designates; classical swine
  fever, Newcastle disease, anthrax and others are added by each province according to the
  disease situation. Follow local requirements for the exact diseases and timing
✓ Keep vaccines refrigerated (usually about 2-8°C, per the instructions); do not freeze them
  (frozen inactivated vaccines stop working) and keep them out of the sun
✓ Vaccinate only healthy animals; postpone for sick, weak or heavily pregnant animals as the
  instructions say
✓ Use a new needle for each animal (or at least each pen), and disinfect needles and
  injection sites, to prevent cross-infection
✓ Watch the animals for about half an hour after vaccination (allergic reactions occasionally
  occur); record the date, vaccine name and batch number
✓ Vaccines only reduce risk; they do not replace hygiene and isolation

Without vaccines (likely after a disaster):
- Biosecurity is then the only line of defence, and also the cheapest:
✓ Keep newly arrived animals isolated and under observation for about 30 days; mix them in
  only if all is well
✓ Keep different species in separate pens (for example, do not keep sheep and cattle together
  in one small pen; keep pigs away from other livestock)
✓ Keep outsiders out of animal houses; your own family should change into dedicated shoes and
  clothes before entering, or put a lime pit/disinfectant mat at the door
✓ Keep out rats, flies, birds and wild animals (wild boar → African swine fever, wild hares →
  rabbit haemorrhagic disease)
✓ Clean feed and water; do not share tools between pens
✓ "All in, all out": after a batch leaves, empty the pen, wash it, disinfect with lime and
  let it dry before the next batch
✓ Isolate sick animals as soon as you find them; the keeper feeds healthy animals first and
  sick ones last, then changes clothes and washes hands

Reporting outbreaks:
⚠️ Several animals falling ill or dying within a short time, high fever spreading fast,
   blisters on the mouth or feet, sudden death with blood from the mouth and nose:
   all must be reported at once to the local animal husbandry and veterinary department
   (township veterinary station).
   This is not "making trouble": stamping it out early saves the animals of every household
   around you.

General principles for veterinary medicines:
- Antibacterials work only against bacteria; they do nothing against viral diseases (African
  swine fever, foot-and-mouth disease, rabbit haemorrhagic disease)
- Follow the label dose and course; do not increase the dose or mix several drugs at random
- Keep to the withdrawal period: leave the number of days the label states between treatment
  and slaughter, milking or collecting eggs; do not eat meat, milk or eggs from that period
- Record every treatment

Dead animals: bury at least 1.5 m deep, cover with quicklime, away from water; do not eat,
              sell or throw into rivers
```

For how to dispose of dead animals see [Sanitation – Disposing of dead animals](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/03-卫生设施.md#病死动物处理).

## Diseases passed from animals to people

People who keep livestock are more likely than others to catch diseases from animals. The ones below matter most in rural China. **If you have an unexplained fever, always tell the doctor "I keep sheep/pigs" or "I have handled sick or dead animals"** — many people are misdiagnosed for a long time because they did not say so.

| Disease | Main source | How people catch it | Signs in people | Key prevention |
|----|---------|----------|---------|---------|
| Brucellosis | Sheep, goats, cattle, pigs (animals that abort are the most dangerous) | Through cuts and mucous membranes when helping at births or handling aborted foetuses and afterbirths; drinking raw milk; breathing contaminated dust | Recurrent fever (rising and falling), heavy sweating, weakness, joint and back pain, painful swollen testicles in men | Wear gloves at births; do not drink raw milk; bury aborted material deep |
| Anthrax | Cattle, sheep, goats, horses and other animals that died of disease | Skinning, butchering or eating animals that died of disease; touching hides or wool carrying spores | A painless black, scab-like ulcer on the skin with swelling around it; eating meat from diseased animals can cause severe abdominal pain and diarrhoea; the inhaled form is extremely dangerous | Do not skin, butcher or eat animals that died of disease |
| Streptococcus suis infection | Pigs that were sick or died | Through cuts on the hands when slaughtering or handling sick or dead pigs | High fever, headache, vomiting; can quickly progress to shock or meningitis | Do not slaughter or eat sick or dead pigs; wear gloves when handling them |
| Hydatid disease | Dogs that ate diseased sheep or cattle offal pass worm eggs | Touching dogs; food and water contaminated with dog droppings | Cysts grow in the liver or lungs years later | Do not feed diseased offal to dogs; worm dogs; wash hands before eating |
| Tapeworm/cysticercosis | Pigs, cattle | Eating undercooked "measly pork" or "measly beef" | Tapeworm in the gut; eggs that get into the body can form cysts in the brain and muscles, causing epilepsy | Cook meat right through; keep toilets and pigsties apart |
| Trichinellosis | Pigs, wild boar | Eating raw or undercooked pork | Fever, severe muscle pain, swollen eyelids | Cook meat right through, especially wild boar |
| Rabies | Dogs, cats and others | Bites, scratches | Once symptoms appear, almost 100% fatal | See [Wound and Injury Care – Rabies prevention](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/02-外伤处理.md#狂犬病预防关键) |

**Protecting yourself against brucellosis**:
```
✓ Wear gloves (ideally long-sleeved gloves) and a mask when helping at births, handling aborted
  foetuses and afterbirths, or reaching into the womb of a female animal
✓ Do not help at births with cuts on your hands, or dress them well before putting on gloves
✓ Bag aborted foetuses, afterbirths and bedding, bury them deep and cover with quicklime;
  disinfect the pen. Do not feed them to dogs
✓ Do not drink raw milk: always boil goat's and cow's milk before drinking, and boil milk for
  cheese and yoghurt first too
✓ If a female animal aborts for no clear reason (especially late in pregnancy, or several in a
  flock one after another), report it to a vet for testing
✓ Brucellosis in people needs a long course of medicine to cure; it must be diagnosed and
  treated in hospital. Do not take medicines on your own
```

**Anthrax: dead animals you must never touch**:
```
Suspicious signs:
- Cattle, sheep, goats or horses dying suddenly, with high fever or difficult breathing
  before death, or with no signs at all
- The carcass swells quickly; dark red blood that does not clot runs from the mouth, nose
  and anus
- The carcass does not stiffen, or stiffens only partly

Do:
❌ Never skin, open, bleed, eat or sell it
   (anthrax bacteria form spores once exposed to air, and these survive in soil for decades;
    opening the carcass spreads spores all over the ground)
✓ Do not move the carcass. Cover it with plastic sheeting to keep off flies and dogs, report
  at once to the local animal husbandry and veterinary department, and let specialists
  deal with it
✓ If you have touched a suspicious dead animal and a painless black scab-like sore appears on
  your skin, see a doctor at once and tell them about the contact
✓ In areas where anthrax is common, vaccinate livestock as local rules require
```

Before handling hides, always read the safety notes at the start of [Textiles, Mending and Tanning](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/07-纺织缝补与鞣皮.md#鞣皮): only process hides from healthy animals slaughtered for food; never skin animals that died of disease or of unknown causes.

## Slaughter hygiene and meat inspection

```
The law: in normal times in China, pigs, cattle, sheep and other animals sold on the market
         should go through designated slaughterhouses and quarantine inspection (with an
         inspection certificate); slaughtering at home for your own use must also follow
         local rules. What follows is basic hygiene for when no inspection is available;
         it does not replace official inspection.
```

**Before slaughter**:
```
✓ Slaughter only healthy animals: watch them the day before; behaviour, appetite, breathing,
  droppings and temperature should be normal
❌ Do not slaughter or eat sick animals, animals that died of disease, animals that died of
   unknown causes, or animals still within the withdrawal period after treatment
✓ Stop feeding about 12-24 hours before slaughter (give water as usual): with the gut empty
  it is less likely to be cut when opening the carcass, and the meat is cleaner
✓ Let the animal rest quietly before slaughter; do not chase or beat it (stress spoils the
  meat and makes it keep less well)
```

**Hygiene in killing and opening the carcass**:
```
1. Place: away from water (at least 30 m), on hard ground or on a sheet, with clean water;
   wash and scald knives, basins and hooks beforehand
2. Kill quickly and accurately, with as little suffering as possible; bleed thoroughly
   (meat that is not well bled spoils easily)
3. After bleeding, hang the carcass up to work on it; do not skin and open it on the bare
   ground
4. When opening, first cut round the anus and tie it off. Cut upwards with the knife tip
   pointing outwards, guarding it with your fingers, and do not cut the stomach, intestines
   or bladder (dung and stomach contents are the main source of contamination of meat).
   If you do cut them, cut away the contaminated part; do not just rinse it with water
5. Put the offal in a separate clean container, apart from the carcass
6. Workers wear gloves; anyone with cuts on their hands does not take part. If a knife
   becomes contaminated during the work, change it or scald-wash it
7. Afterwards wash the area, bury blood and waste deep (do not tip them into streams or
   ditches), and wash and dry the tools
```

**Inspecting offal and meat (if you find anything abnormal, do not eat it)**:
```
What to look at:
- Spleen: is it greatly enlarged and black, with black, tar-like blood when cut?
  → ⚠️ This is a warning sign of anthrax: stop at once, do not cut any further, report to
    a vet
- Liver: any white spots or streaks, or blister-like cysts of various sizes (hydatid, liver
  fluke)?
- Lungs: large areas of consolidation, pus, or nodules (tuberculosis)?
- Lymph nodes (the "lumps" in the neck, groin and elsewhere): enlarged, bleeding or pus-filled?
- Muscle: in pork cut into the cheek muscle, loin muscle, heart and diaphragm, and look for
  rice-grain-sized, translucent white blisters (cysticerci, "measly pork")
- Overall: yellow fat and tissue under the skin (jaundice), bleeding spots all over, dark
  meat, an odd smell, many abscesses

What to do:
- A small local abscess or the odd parasite cyst: cut it out with the surrounding tissue and
  bury it deep; cook the rest thoroughly
- Measly pork, abnormalities in many places, bleeding all over, jaundice, foul smell: do not
  eat any of the animal; bury it deep
- Bury all diseased offal deep; do not feed it raw to dogs (this is exactly how hydatid
  disease spreads)

Trichinella cannot be seen with the naked eye → always cook pork and wild boar right through
(no blood colour in the centre when cut, juices run clear)
```

**Keeping meat after slaughter**:
```
- After slaughter, hang the carcass as soon as possible in a cool, airy, fly-proof place to
  cool; do not pile it up while warm
- In hot weather, cure, dry or smoke whatever cannot be eaten the same day as soon as
  possible (see the links below)
- Keep raw and cooked apart: do not use the knife and board that cut raw meat directly for
  cooked food
```

For preserving methods see [Food Processing and Preservation – Cured pork and salt pork](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/01-食物加工与保存.md#腊肉咸肉) and [Food Processing and Preservation – Dried meat](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/01-食物加工与保存.md#肉干). For rendering lard see [Grain and Oil Processing – Rendering animal fat](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/14-粮油加工.md#熬炼动物油).

## Using manure

Livestock manure is the most important source of fertiliser for farming, but fresh manure contains parasite eggs and germs. Used directly it scorches seedlings and spreads disease, so **it must be composted until fully rotted**.

```
Rough manure output (fresh, per day):
- Finishing pig about 2-3 kg (over about 5 kg including urine)
- Sheep or goat about 1-2 kg
- Rabbit about 0.1-0.2 kg
- Cattle about 20-30 kg, donkey about 8-15 kg

Composting:
1. Mix manure with bedding straw, crop straw and fallen leaves (pure manure is too wet and
   sticky to let air in)
2. Build a heap about 1-1.5 m high; it is moist enough when a squeezed handful holds together
   and water shows between your fingers but does not drip
3. Within a few days the inside of the heap should reach about 55°C or more (too hot to keep
   your hand in). Several days at this heat kill most germs, worm eggs and weed seeds. Turn
   the heap about every 1-2 weeks so the outside is turned in and "cooked" too
4. Cover the heap with straw or soil so rain does not wash it away; keep the composting site
   at least 30 m from water
5. After about 1-3 months (longer in winter), when it is dark brown, crumbly, no longer
   smells bad and smells of earth, it is fully rotted

Use and rates:
- As base fertiliser: well-rotted manure at about 15-30 t/ha (about 1.5-3 kg/m²), mixed into
  the soil when digging
- For vegetables eaten raw (lettuce, cucumber and the like) use only fully rotted manure, and
  apply it well before harvest
- Liquid manure and urine are also good quick-acting fertilisers, but dilute and ferment them
  before use

Other uses:
- Pig and cattle manure can go into a biogas digester, and the digestate (sludge and liquid)
  used as fertiliser; biogas digesters carry risks of suffocation and explosion, see the link
  below
- Dried cattle and donkey dung cakes can be used as fuel
```

For composting principles and fertilising see [Farming and Animal Husbandry – Soil improvement](../../../docs/04-动植物培育/README.md#土壤改良); for human excreta and how long pathogens survive see [Sanitation – Using faeces and urine](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/03-卫生设施.md#粪便与尿液的利用); for biogas safety see [Lighting and Energy – Biogas](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/06-照明与能源.md#沼气); for dung cakes and other fuels see [Firewood and Woodland Management – Other fuels](../../../docs/04-动植物培育/09-薪柴与林地管理.md#其他燃料).

## 📝 Livestock keeping essentials

### Recommended for beginners

```
Best suited:
1. Rabbits (breed fast, low investment, relatively easy to manage)
2. Sheep and goats (if you have grassland; low-input, disease-resistant)

Needs experience:
- Pigs (high investment, demanding, disease risk)
```

### Choosing what to keep

```
Factors to consider:
✓ Money (rabbits < sheep/goats < pigs)
✓ Space (rabbits need little; pigs, sheep and goats need more)
✓ Feed source (sheep and goats suit those with grassland; pigs need bought feed)
✓ Skill (rabbits, sheep and goats are low-input; pigs need careful management)
✓ Purpose (own use, sale, both)
✓ Labour

Household set-up:
- 10-20 chickens (eggs and meat)
- 10-20 rabbits (meat, prolific)
- 3-5 sheep or goats (if you have grassland)

Or:
- Chickens + 1-2 pigs (a year)
```

### Keys to success

```
✓ Cleanliness and hygiene (most important of all!)
✓ Feeding properly (balanced nutrition, fixed times and amounts)
✓ Disease prevention and worming (prevention first)
✓ Careful observation (find problems early, treat early)
✓ Learning the skills (books, experienced people)
✓ Keeping records (feed, disease, returns)
✓ Sensible scale (do not overreach; grow steadily)
```

```
The record book should at least note: each animal's origin and date of birth, mating date and
expected birth date, number born and number surviving, vaccinations and treatments (with
withdrawal periods), worming dates, illnesses and deaths
→ Expected birth date: pig mating date + 114 days, sheep/goat + 150 days, rabbit + 31 days,
  yellow cattle + 280 days, donkey + about 360 days
```

### Points to note

```
✓ Know local regulations (licences for keeping livestock, environmental rules)
✓ Disease control requirements (animal disease prevention certificate)
✓ Environmental protection (manure handling)
✓ Good relations with neighbours (control smell and noise)
✓ Guard against zoonoses (brucellosis, rabies and others)
✓ Humane husbandry
✓ Market risk (price fluctuations)
```

```
Never:
❌ Feed swill or leftovers containing meat to pigs
❌ Eat, sell or skin animals that died of disease or of unknown causes
❌ Help at births or handle aborted foetuses with bare hands
```
