## First, what this chapter covers

Growing the grain is only half the job. Between the ears of rice and wheat in the field and the rice, steamed buns and cooking oil in the pot lies a whole chain of work: cutting, threshing, winnowing, drying, hulling, grinding and pressing oil. Once the machines stop, all of this has to be done by human, animal and water power. And **if a spell of continuous rain arrives at harvest time, grain that cannot be dried will sprout and rot within days**; a whole year's work can be lost.

For growing methods see [Grain Crops](../04-farming-and-animal-husbandry/02-grain-crops.md); for the basic requirements for drying and storage see [Food Processing and Preservation – Grain and pulses](01-food-processing-and-preservation.md#grain-and-beans). This chapter covers the processing from harvest to the point where the food can go into the pot.

```
⚠️ Safety:
- ☠️ Throw away the whole batch of mouldy grain, peanuts or maize: do not eat it, press oil
  from it or feed it to livestock (aflatoxin)
- ☠️ Cottonseed oil is not edible unless it has been refined; tung oil and castor oil are
  poisonous and must never be used as cooking oil
- Flour and bran dust floating in the air explodes when it meets a naked flame:
  no naked flames in mills or granaries
- Threshing machines, stone rollers, millstones and oil presses can all pull in hands,
  sleeves and hair
- When animals are turning a mill or roller, do not stand inside the circle they walk
- Slaked-lime water (used to alkali-treat maize) burns skin and eyes
```

## Harvest: when and how to cut

### Judging ripeness

| Crop | When to cut | Simple test |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| Rice | About 90% of grains yellow and ripe | Ears bend down; pressing a grain with your fingernail gives no white milk and leaves a hard mark |
| Wheat | Late dough stage to early full ripeness | The grain can be cut through with a fingernail like wax but gives no milk; any later and it starts shedding grain |
| Maize | Husks dry and yellow, kernels hard | The milk line at the top of the kernel has gone and a black layer appears at its base |
| Soya beans | Leaves dropped, pods yellow | Shake the plant and the beans rattle in their pods |
| Sorghum, foxtail millet | Grains in the lower part of the head hard | Grains are glossy; biting them gives no milk |

```
Press a grain with your fingernail; there are three stages:
1. Milk stage: white milk comes out at once → too early; cut now and the grains shrivel
   and the yield is low
2. Dough stage: soft like wax, you can press into it but there is no milk
   → start harvesting wheat
3. Full ripeness: hard, cannot be pressed in → right for rice; wheat has already started
   shedding grain

✓ Better two or three days early than late: overripe crops shed grain, lodge (fall over)
  and sprout in the ear if it rains
✓ Watch the weather: start cutting at the beginning of several dry, sunny days in a row;
  see weather observation
```

For reading the weather see [Weather Observation and Forecasting](../08-basic-science/07-weather-observation-and-forecasting.md).

### Cutting and binding

```
1. Cutting with a sickle: hold a handful of stalks in your left hand and draw the sickle
   in your right hand towards you, close to the ground
   ⚠️ Always pull the sickle in a direction away from your legs and feet; keep it sharp,
      as a blunt blade slips more easily and causes injuries
2. Stubble: for rice and wheat usually leave about 10-20 cm of stubble; the straw has other
   uses (thatching, animal bedding, papermaking, fuel)
3. Lay the cut rice or wheat in small handfuls on the stubble to dry for a day or two
   (when the field is dry)
4. Binding sheaves: pull out a small twist of straw as a binding band, tie it tightly
   round the middle of an armful of stalks, with all the ears at one end
5. Stacking: ears inwards or upwards, stalks outwards, build a stack with a pointed top
   and cover the top with straw mats against rain
   → Do not pile it in a big heap for long: damp rice or wheat packed together heats up,
     sprouts and rots
```

For sharpening sickles see [Woodworking and Tool Maintenance – Sharpening](09-woodworking-and-tool-maintenance.md#sharpening).

## Threshing

First prepare a **threshing floor**: choose a flat, sunny, well-aired piece of open ground, remove the grass roots, sprinkle it with water and roll it hard again and again with a stone roller. When dry the surface is hard and smooth and no soil gets mixed into the grain. If there is no threshing floor, spread large tarpaulins or bamboo mats.

### Beating (threshing against a tub)

```
The simplest method; all you need is a big wooden tub or box (a threshing tub):
1. Surround three sides of the tub with straw mats or cloth to catch flying grain
2. Grip the stalks of a handful of rice with both hands, raise it high and beat it hard
   against the tub wall or a wooden frame set at a slant inside the tub
3. Beat each handful three or four times, turn it over and beat again;
   the grain falls into the tub
✓ Suits rice (the grain comes off easily) and small handfuls of wheat
→ A skilled person can thresh several hundred kg of rice a day; it is very tiring,
  so take turns
```

### Flail

```
Construction: a long handle (about 1.5-2 m) with a swinging beater joined to its end
              by a cross pin (several wooden or bamboo strips bound side by side,
              about 0.5-1 m long)
Use: spread rice, wheat or bean pods on the threshing floor and swing the handle with
     both hands so the beater flips over and strikes the heads
✓ Suits wheat, soya beans, rapeseed, foxtail millet and similar
⚠️ When several people thresh together, stand in a row, keep the same rhythm and keep
   your distance so the beater does not hit anyone
```

### Rolling

```
1. Spread the heads in a ring on the threshing floor about 20-30 cm deep
2. Animals (cattle, donkeys, horses) pull a stone roller round and round the ring;
   people follow and turn the straw with wooden forks
3. Turn it after each pass, repeat several times until no grain is left in the heads
4. Fork off the straw, leaving grain and broken chaff, then winnow
✓ Suits wheat, foxtail millet, sorghum and pulses; far more efficient than beating by hand
⚠️ Muzzle the animals (otherwise they eat as they walk); do not stand in front of the roller
```

For the power of draught animals see [Thermodynamics and Power Machinery – Animal and human power](../09-advanced-science-and-engineering/03-thermodynamics-and-engines.md#83-animal-and-human-power).

### Simple pedal thresher

```
Construction:
- A wooden drum that can turn (about 40 cm in diameter, about 0.6-1 m long), its surface
  covered with thick wire bent into upside-down U shapes (wire loops)
- One end of the drum connects through a crank and connecting rod to a pedal below
- The drum is mounted on a wooden frame, screened on three sides

Use: one person works the pedal to spin the drum fast, holds a handful of rice firmly
     with both hands and turns the heads over on the drum; the wire loops comb and beat
     the grain off
→ About 2-3 times as efficient as beating

⚠️ Hold only the base of the stalks; never put your hands near the drum; tie your cuffs
   tight, wear no scarf, put long hair up
⚠️ Stop the drum completely before clearing tangled straw
```

For how cranks, gears and other transmissions work see [Physics – Gears and transmission](../08-basic-science/02-physics.md#25-gears-and-transmission).

## Winnowing and wind cleaning

Threshed grain is mixed with broken straw, chaff, shrivelled grains and dust; use the wind to blow the light material away.

### Winnowing in the wind

```
1. Wait for a steady light breeze (leaves moving all the time, thin twigs swaying gently);
   too strong a wind blows good grain away too
2. Stand side-on to the wind, scoop up grain with a wooden shovel and throw it upwards
   at a slant in an arc
3. Heavy, plump grains land close by; light shrivelled grains and chaff land a little
   further off; dust and broken leaves blow away
4. Lightly sweep the chaff that lands on the surface of the grain heap with a broom
5. It usually takes two or three passes to get it clean
```

### Winnowing basket

```
For small amounts use a winnowing basket: hold both sides and toss it up and down,
pushing slightly forwards
→ The grain gathers towards the back of the basket, chaff and bits are tossed to the
  front edge and a light shake sends them out
✓ Use the same method to remove bran after milling or pounding rice
```

### Winnowing fan (fanning mill)

On windless days, make your own wind with a hand-cranked winnowing fan.

```
Principle: grain falls at an even rate from a hopper above and passes through a
           sideways stream of air
           → heavy grains fall almost straight down, light grains are blown a little
             to the side, chaff and dust are blown out

Construction:
1. A wooden box with a hand-cranked fan at one end (4-6 wooden blades on a shaft,
   inside a round air duct)
2. A hopper on top, with an adjustable gate at its outlet to control the flow
3. Two outlets in the bottom of the box: the one near the fan delivers plump grain,
   the one a little further off delivers shrivelled grain
4. The other end of the box is open; chaff and dust blow out there

Adjusting:
- Crank fast → strong wind; good grain may be blown out too
- Crank slowly → weak wind; chaff is not blown clean
- Keep the flow thin and even; pour in too much at once and the wind cannot get through it
```

## Drying to a safe moisture content

Freshly harvested rice and wheat contain about 20-30% water, enough for both moulds and insects to thrive. Dry the grain to a safe moisture content before storing it: **rice <13%, wheat <12%, maize <14%**, soya beans below about 12%, and oilseeds such as peanuts and rapeseed lower still (about 8-10%).

### Sun-drying grain

```
1. Spread the grain only after the morning dew has dried, about 3-5 cm deep, and rake
   furrows in it with a wooden rake (more surface exposed to the heat)
2. Turn it about once an hour
3. In the evening, while the grain is still warm, rake it into heaps and cover them
   so the night dew does not dampen it again
   (but let it cool before bagging or storing it: warm grain in bags condenses moisture
   and turns damp again)
4. Do not dry rice too fast in fierce sun in one go, and do not splash water on it
   to cool it straight after drying
   → The inside and outside of the grain shrink unevenly and it cracks ("sun-cracking"),
     breaking into broken rice when milled
5. Usually dry for 2-4 sunny days in a row

Protecting from rain:
✓ Keep tarpaulins, straw mats, brooms and wooden shovels ready at the edge of the
  threshing floor; as soon as the sky changes, everyone clears the floor
✓ Rake the grain into long ridged heaps with pointed tops, cover them with cloth weighted
  with stones round the edges, and raise them off the ground to keep groundwater out
✓ In long spells of rain, spread the grain thinly indoors or under a shelter, keep it
  aired and turn it often;
  you can rig bamboo mats indoors and warm-dry the grain over a small fire underneath
  (no flame may touch it directly; beware of fire)
```

### How to tell it is dry

```
Bite test: bite a grain; it snaps cleanly with a "crack", does not stick to your teeth
           or change shape → basically dry
           Feels soft or sticky, or keeps a tooth mark → still damp
Hand test: grab a handful, squeeze it hard and let go; the grains fall apart at once
           and rustle → dry
           It stays in a lump or your palm feels damp → damp
Rattle test: shake it in a bottle or bamboo tube; a crisp sound → dry; a dull sound → damp
Salt-bottle test (more reliable):
  1. Fill a dry glass bottle about 3/4 full of grain and add 1-2 spoonfuls of dry fine salt
  2. Close it tightly, shake for 1 minute, leave for 15 minutes, then shake once more
  3. Salt sticks to the bottle wall or forms lumps → the grain is too damp
     (moisture above roughly 14%); keep drying
     Salt stays loose → ready to store
```

## Storing grain: preventing mould and insects

For containers, stores and storage times see [Grain Crops – Storage essentials](../04-farming-and-animal-husbandry/02-grain-crops.md#storage-essentials). This section adds the specific methods against mould and insects.

```
Against mould:
✓ The only reliable method is drying: below the safe moisture content moulds hardly grow
✓ Keep the store off the ground and away from walls, on boards or stones, so damp from
  the ground cannot rise
✓ Check every month: push your hand deep into the grain; if it feels warm or damp or
  smells mouldy → spread it out and dry it again at once
✓ After the rainy season, check the surface layer and the grain against the walls

Against insects:
1. Cold: stored-grain insects barely breed below about 15°C; in northern winters,
   spreading the grain outdoors to freeze for a few days kills most of the eggs;
   let it cool after freezing, then bring it back in
2. Airtight storage (oxygen starvation): fill containers that can be sealed completely
   (jars, drums, thick plastic bags) with dried grain and seal them
   → The insects inside use up the oxygen breathing and die, usually within a few weeks
   ✓ Fill them as full as possible to leave little air; do not keep opening them
3. Plant ash: mix dry plant ash into pulses and small amounts of seed (enough to cover
   the surface and fill the gaps between grains), or layer grain and ash in a jar;
   this stops insects moving and laying eggs; wash it well before eating
4. Sun: spread infested grain in fierce sun; the insects crawl out and leave; then sieve
5. Small cloth bags of Sichuan pepper, dried chillies or mugwort leaves placed in the
   grain jar repel insects to some extent

☠️ Do not fumigate grain yourself with aluminium phosphide (fumigation tablets sold as
   grain-insect killers): it releases highly toxic phosphine gas, and families die from
   it every year
```

```
☠️ What to do with mouldy grain:
- Mouldy, caked, discoloured or bitter grain, peanuts or maize → throw away the whole batch
- Do not pick out the good bits to eat, do not wash it and eat it, do not feed it to
  chickens or pigs
- Heat does not destroy aflatoxin, and it passes into people through meat, eggs and milk
```

For aflatoxin see [Food Processing and Preservation – Aflatoxin](01-food-processing-and-preservation.md#aflatoxin); for working out how long your stocks will last see [Record-keeping, Trade and Division of Labour – How long will it last?](13-record-keeping-trade-and-division-of-labour.md#working-out-how-long-it-will-last).

## Rice: hulling and milling

Paddy rice has a hard outer husk that people cannot digest. Removing the husk gives **brown rice**; milling off the outer bran layer gives **white (polished) rice**.

### Hulling: removing the husk

```
Pounding in a mortar (simplest):
1. A stone mortar or one hollowed out of hardwood, filled about 1/3 deep with paddy rice
2. Pound up and down with a wooden pestle (thick and rounded at one end); the grains rub
   against each other and the husks crack and come off
3. After pounding a while, tip it out, winnow off the husks with a winnowing basket,
   and pound again any grains still unhulled
✓ Treadle pounder: a long wooden beam pivoted on a stand in the middle, with a pestle
  head at one end; tread on the other end and release it, and the pestle head drops
  and pounds the rice → using your legs is far less effort than using your arms
  (the lever principle)

Clay huller:
- Like a small mill, with an upper and a lower stone; the shell is woven from bamboo
  strips and filled with packed clay, with bamboo or hardwood slats set in the grinding
  faces as teeth
- Paddy falls through the hole in the middle of the upper stone; turning the upper stone
  rubs the husks off between the two stones without crushing the rice (the gap between
  the stones is wider than in a flour mill)
- What comes out is husk and brown rice mixed; separate them with a winnowing fan or basket
```

For the lever principle see [Physics – Levers](../08-basic-science/02-physics.md#21-levers).

### Milling rice and yields

```
Keep pounding the brown rice in the mortar, or roll it under a stone roller; the grains
rub against each other, the bran layer is rubbed off, and the bran is winnowed away

Rough yields (by weight):
- Paddy → brown rice: about 78-80%
- Brown rice → white rice: about 88-92% (the whiter you mill it, the more you lose)
- Paddy → white rice: about 65-70%

Rice bran: highly nutritious; can be mixed into porridge and rice or made into cakes,
           and is excellent animal feed;
           it goes rancid easily, so use it within a week or two of milling
Husks: fuel, animal bedding, mixed into mud for adobe bricks, compost
```

### Brown rice and white rice: do not mill it too white

Most of the vitamin B1 in rice is in the bran layer and the germ. The whiter it is milled and the harder it is washed, the more is lost. In history, whole populations that lived mainly on polished white rice with few pulses or other grains suffered **beriberi** (swelling and numbness of the legs, heart failure); this is not "athlete's foot", the fungal infection.

```
✓ Eat brown rice, or half-milled rice milled only once or twice (the grains still slightly
  yellowish)
✓ Combine it with pulses and other grains
✓ A second benefit: one pass less gives a higher yield of rice and saves effort
✗ Brown rice keeps less well than white rice (the oil in the bran layer goes rancid);
  mill it as you need it
```

For details see [Nutrition and Deficiency Diseases – Vitamin B1](02-nutrition-and-deficiency-diseases.md#vitamin-b1-do-not-mill-rice-too-white).

## Wheat: grinding flour with a millstone

### Before grinding

```
1. Sieve out clods and weed seeds and pick out stones
2. Winnow it clean or wash it quickly in clean water, then spread it until the surface is
   dry and the inside still slightly moist (tempering)
   → The bran becomes slightly tough and breaks into large flakes that sieve out easily;
     do not soak it, as wet wheat clogs the mill
3. Black, misshapen grains (ergot) and mouldy grains must be picked out
   ☠️ Ergot is poisonous and causes gangrene of the limbs and convulsions;
      it is more common in rye
```

### How a millstone is built

```
- Two round stones, upper and lower, usually of a hard stone that does not shed grit
  (granite, sandstone)
- The lower stone (bed stone) is fixed, with a short spindle at its centre
- The upper stone (runner stone) turns on the spindle; it has one or two feed holes (eyes)
  and a push or pull bar fixed to its side
- Both facing grinding surfaces are cut with furrows:
  - The face is divided into sections (commonly 6 or 8), each cut with a set of
    parallel slanting grooves
  - The grooves on the upper and lower stones slant in opposite directions and cross
    like scissors as the stone turns
  - The centre is slightly hollowed (the eye area); grains are first cracked here, then
    pushed outwards and ground finer and finer
- Around the lower stone is a tray (a ledge to catch the flour); flour squeezes out of
  the gap between the stones and falls onto it

Sizes for reference:
- Hand mill: about 40-60 cm in diameter, can be turned by one person
- Animal-driven mill: about 80-100 cm in diameter, pulled by a donkey or ox
```

### Grinding and sifting

```
1. Put wheat into the eye, not too much at a time; let it trickle down by itself
2. Turning the mill: turn at a steady speed; a hand mill turns at a dozen-odd to
   twenty-odd turns a minute
3. The first pass gives cracked wheat and coarse meal; sift out the flour with a fine sieve
4. Put the coarse remainder left on the sieve back through the mill; grind and sift
   again and again, usually 3-5 times
5. The flaky material left at the end is bran

Flour yield (by weight):
- Wholemeal flour (bran and all ground fine): about 95% or more
- Ordinary flour: about 75-85%
- White flour (only the whitest flour of the first few passes): about 60-70%
→ When food is short, take more flour; bran contains B vitamins and fibre, do not waste it

Output (approximate): a hand mill grinds a few kg an hour; a donkey mill about a dozen-odd
  kg an hour
  A family of 4 needs about 2 kg of grain a day (about 0.5 kg per person):
  about 1 hour on a hand mill
```

### Re-dressing the millstone

After a month or two the furrows become shallow and flat: the mill grinds slowly, the flour gets hot and more bran comes through. Then the furrows must be recut.

```
1. Lift off the upper stone (two or three people, or raise it bit by bit with crowbars
   and wooden wedges, keeping feet clear)
2. Sweep both faces clean and check with a straight edge whether they are still flat;
   grind down high spots first
3. With a steel chisel and hammer, deepen the existing slanting grooves one by one,
   keeping the direction and angle of each groove unchanged
4. One side of each groove is steep, the other gentle; the edges must be sharp to shear
   the grain
5. Sweep clean when finished; first grind a little bran or broken grain to carry out the
   stone grit, and do not eat this batch

⚠️ Wear goggles: chips fly when cutting stone, and eye injuries are the most common
⚠️ Breathing stone dust from years of cutting stone causes silicosis; wet the stone
   before cutting and wear a mask
```

For forging and quenching chisels see [Bench Work and Machining](../05-metallurgy/09-bench-work-and-machining.md).

### Animal-driven mills and watermills

```
Animal-driven mill: the animal is harnessed to the mill bar and walks in a circle;
                    blindfold it (to stop it getting dizzy and stopping to steal food)
Watermill: a waterwheel drives the millstone
- Vertical waterwheel → a pair of gears turns the rotation of the horizontal shaft into
  rotation of a vertical shaft, which drives the upper stone
- Horizontal waterwheel: water strikes a horizontal wheel fixed to the bottom of a vertical
  shaft, and the shaft drives the upper stone directly; the simplest design, suited to
  mountain streams with a good drop
- A small watermill (about 1-2 kW) does the work of a dozen to twenty-odd people turning
  mills, and can serve the whole village
```

For estimating water power see [Physics – Water power: waterwheels and watermills](../08-basic-science/02-physics.md#33-water-power-waterwheels-and-watermills) and [Thermodynamics and Power Machinery – Water power](../09-advanced-science-and-engineering/03-thermodynamics-and-engines.md#81-water-power); for headrace channels see [Water Supply Engineering – Diverting water](10-water-supply.md#bringing-water-by-gravity).

## Maize

### Shelling and grinding

```
Shelling:
1. Hang the cobs up with their husks on, or dry them in an airy place (moisture <14%);
   the kernels shell well only once they are hard
2. Rub two cobs against each other, or push the kernels off along the rows with your
   thumb; for large amounts, beat cobs in a sack with a wooden stick
3. Simple sheller: drill a hole slightly wider than a cob in a board, drive a ring of nail
   heads round the hole, and push the cob hard through the hole with a twisting motion
4. Dried corncobs make good fuel

Grinding: on the millstone into grits (coarse) or cornmeal (fine), the same way as wheat
- Maize germ is oily, so the meal goes rancid easily; grind small amounts often
```

### ⚠️ If maize is your staple: cook it in alkaline water first

The niacin in maize is in a bound form that the body can hardly use, and maize protein is short of tryptophan (from which the body can make some niacin). In history, whole populations that lived mainly on maize and ate few pulses, meat or eggs suffered **pellagra**: red, peeling skin wherever the sun reaches it, diarrhoea, mental disturbance, and death in severe cases.

Native Americans ate maize for thousands of years yet rarely had this disease, because they **cooked maize in limewater**: the alkali releases the bound niacin and also softens the skin of the kernels, improving taste and nutrition.

```
Cooking maize in limewater (approximate amounts):
1. 1 kg dried maize kernels, about 2-3 L water, about 10-20 g slaked lime
   (1-2% of the weight of the maize)
   With no slaked lime, use the alkaline water drained through plant ash
2. Stir the slaked lime into the water first, then add the maize; boil for about
   30-60 minutes, until the skins can be rubbed off with your fingers
3. Take off the heat and leave to soak overnight
4. Pour off the alkaline water and rub and rinse repeatedly to remove most of the skins
5. Dry and grind into meal, or grind wet straight into dough for flatbreads

⚠️ Slaked lime and alkaline water burn skin and eyes; if any splashes into the eyes,
   rinse at once with plenty of clean water
⚠️ Use only slaked lime (calcium hydroxide); never put quicklime straight into water
   (it gets violently hot and spatters)
```

For the method and background see [Nutrition and Deficiency Diseases – Niacin](02-nutrition-and-deficiency-diseases.md#niacin-treat-maize-with-alkali-before-eating-it); for burning lime see [Chemistry – The lime cycle](../08-basic-science/03-chemistry.md#44-the-lime-cycle-the-basis-of-building).

## Pulses and minor grains

### Pulses

```
1. When the pods turn yellow and rattle when shaken, cut the whole plants and dry them
2. Beat them with flails on the threshing floor, roll them with a stone roller, or for small
   amounts put them in a sack and beat it with a stick; the beans pop out of the pods
3. Winnow off the broken pods and dry to below about 12% moisture (crisp when bitten,
   a clear rattle when shaken)
4. Pulses are the most prone to bean weevils: store them airtight once dry, or mix with
   plant ash (see above)

Splitting and skinning: for soya beans, broad beans and the like, open up the gap of the
                        millstone so it only splits them in two; winnow off the skins;
                        they cook faster
Grinding to milk: soak, then grind with water on the millstone into a slurry for soya milk
                  and tofu

⚠️ All pulses must be cooked right through (raw beans contain saponins, trypsin inhibitors
   and other harmful substances)
⚠️ Broad beans: some people (favism, an inherited disease) suffer acute haemolysis after
   eating broad beans or contact with broad-bean pollen, with fever, jaundice and urine
   the colour of strong tea; see a doctor at once. If anyone in the family has had it,
   the whole family should avoid broad beans
```

For making tofu see [Food Processing and Preservation – Tofu](01-food-processing-and-preservation.md#tofu); for bean sprouts see [Nutrition and Deficiency Diseases – Vitamin C](02-nutrition-and-deficiency-diseases.md#vitamin-c-bean-sprouts-are-the-most-reliable-winter-source).

### Minor grains

| Crop | Hulling and processing | Notes |
|------|------------------------|-------|
| Foxtail millet | Roll or pound to remove the husk, winnow clean | Small grain, needs a fine sieve; millet porridge is good food for the sick |
| Proso millet (glutinous) | As foxtail millet | Very sticky; good for rice cakes and sticky millet rice |
| Sorghum | Roll off the outer skin to make pearled sorghum, or grind to meal | Dark sorghum contains tannins, tastes astringent and reduces protein absorption; soak before cooking, or use it for brewing and feed |
| Buckwheat | Hull to make buckwheat groats, or grind to flour | A few people are severely allergic to buckwheat |
| Oats | Hulled oats are hard to dehusk; pound and winnow repeatedly | Flatten into rolled oats, which cook quickly |

```
✓ Combining minor grains and pulses with the staple grain markedly reduces deficiency diseases
✓ Minor grains are small and the husk is close in size to the grain, so they are harder to
  winnow clean than rice or wheat; crank the winnowing fan slowly and keep the flow thin
```

## Pressing oil

Fat is the most concentrated energy food (about 9 kcal per g, more than twice that of grain); it also fuels lamps, makes soap and protects tools.

### Raw materials

| Raw material | Oil content of seed (kernel) | Traditional press oil yield (by weight of raw material, approx.) | Notes |
|------|-------------|---------------------------|------|
| Peanut kernels | About 40-50% | About 35-40% | High yield, easy to press; ⚠️ never press mouldy ones |
| Sesame | About 45-55% | About 40-45% | Stone-ground sesame oil uses the water-displacement method, see below |
| Rapeseed | About 35-45% | About 30-35% | Grows in both north and south; reliable yields |
| Sunflower seeds | Kernels about 40-50% | In the shell about 25-30% | Dehull first for more oil |
| Tea-oil camellia seeds | Kernels about 40-50% | Whole seed about 20-25% | Southern hill country; keeps well |
| Soya beans | About 17-20% | About 8-12% | Low yield by traditional pressing; the press cake is good feed and fertiliser; better to eat the beans themselves |
| Pig leaf fat, back fat | — | About 70-80% | Rendering; the easiest |

```
☠️ Never press oil from mouldy or bitter peanuts, maize germ or sunflower seeds:
   aflatoxin dissolves into the oil; the oil can look perfectly clear and still be
   poisonous, and deep-frying temperatures do not break it down
☠️ Cottonseed: contains gossypol; eating traditionally pressed cottonseed oil for a long
   time causes low blood potassium, weakness throughout the body and infertility in men
   and women; only cottonseed oil that has been alkali-refined is edible, which a small
   workshop cannot do → do not eat traditionally pressed cottonseed oil
☠️ Tung oil: poisonous; swallowing it causes violent vomiting and diarrhoea; it looks and
   smells like cooking oil, and people have mistakenly eaten it again and again in history
   → Use it only as a wood finish, for waterproofing and as lamp oil; keep it in its own
     container labelled "TUNG OIL – NOT EDIBLE", stored apart from cooking oil
☠️ Castor seeds: contain ricin; the seeds must not be eaten; use castor oil only as a
   lubricant and as a raw material for laxatives, never as cooking oil
```

### Basic steps

```
1. Cleaning: sieve out soil and stones, pick out mouldy and shrivelled seeds, dry
   (oilseed moisture about 8-10%)
2. Dehulling: hull peanuts, sunflower seeds and tea-oil seeds first (the husks absorb oil);
   rapeseed and sesame need no hulling
3. Roasting: roast in an iron wok over a low fire, stirring all the time, until the seeds
   are lightly golden and smell fragrant (roast sesame until it crackles)
   → Heat breaks the cells and thins the oil, so more oil comes out; scorched seeds give
     bitter, dark oil
4. Crushing: crush with a stone roller or grind coarsely on a millstone (not too fine,
   or it clogs and the oil cannot run out when pressed)
5. Steaming: steam the meal on a steamer tray for about 10-20 minutes, until it forms a
   lump when squeezed in the hand without falling apart or releasing water
   → The meal absorbs moisture and heats up, the protein sets, and the oil flows out
     more easily
6. Making cakes: while hot, pack the meal into iron or bamboo rings, wrap it tightly in
   straw or sackcloth, tread it down firmly and form round cakes
   ✓ Work fast: once it cools it will not give up its oil
7. Pressing: put a stack of cakes in the press and increase the pressure gradually;
   the oil runs out between the cakes and is caught underneath
8. After the first pressing, the cakes can be crushed, steamed and pressed again
   for a little more oil
```

### Three kinds of press

```
Wedge press (traditional workshop):
- A large hollowed-out hardwood log (the press trough), with the cakes stacked upright inside
- Wooden wedges are driven in between the cakes and wooden blocks; people swing a suspended
  ram (ram beam) to strike the wedges again and again, driving them ever tighter
- Principle: a wedge is an inclined plane; a small advance buys a very large squeezing force

Lever press (the easiest to build yourself):
- A long, strong wooden beam (about 4-6 m), one end set into a hole in a wall or post as
  the pivot
- The cakes are placed close to the pivot, with the beam pressing down on them
- Hang stones or a basket of heavy objects on the other end and add weight gradually
- Example: cakes 0.5 m from the pivot, weight 5 m from the pivot, 200 kg hung
  → the force on the cakes equals the weight of about 2000 kg
- Press slowly; one pressing takes several hours to a whole night

Screw press:
- A thick wooden or iron screw passes through a fixed crossbeam, with a pressing plate
  at its lower end
- Turn the screw with a long bar and the plate presses down on the cakes
- Principle: a screw is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder; one turn lowers it by
  only one pitch, multiplying the force many times
- A wooden screw is carved from hardwood; an iron screw needs a lathe or files
```

For how levers, inclined planes and screws work see [Physics – Simple machines](../08-basic-science/02-physics.md#2-simple-machines).

### Stone-ground sesame oil (water-displacement method)

```
Sesame needs no press; water is used to "swap out" the oil:
1. Roast the sesame and grind it on a millstone into a very fine paste
2. Slowly add almost-boiling water to the paste, stirring hard as you add it
3. The water binds to the sesame solids, which sink, and the oil is squeezed out and
   floats on top
4. Keep shaking the pot and skimming off the top layer of oil
→ The yield is close to that of pressing, and no press is needed
```

### Rendering animal fat

```
1. Cut pig leaf fat or back fat into small pieces, add a little water to the pot and
   render slowly over a low fire
2. Take off the heat once the water has boiled off and the cracklings turn golden and
   shrink (burnt, black cracklings make the fat bitter)
3. Strain through gauze while hot into a clean, dry earthenware jar
4. Good fat sets with a smooth, white surface
✓ The cracklings are edible, or can go into fillings
⚠️ Never let water suddenly get into the pot while rendering (the fat spatters violently);
   if the fat catches fire, put the lid on; never pour water on it
```

For rendering purer fat see [Lighting and Energy – Rendering animal fat](06-lighting-and-energy.md#rendering-animal-fat); for making soap from waste fat see [Soap Making – Step 2: Prepare the fat](04-soap-making.md#step-2-preparing-the-fat).

## Clarifying and storing oil

```
Clarifying:
1. Freshly pressed crude oil is cloudy, with solids and water in it; leave it to stand in
   a jar for a few days to a few weeks so the solids and water settle
2. Carefully ladle off the clear oil on top or siphon it off with a thin tube; use the
   sediment at the bottom for other things (soap, lubrication)
3. For longer keeping: heat the clear oil in a pot over a low fire until no more tiny
   bubbles rise (the water has boiled off), cool and put into jars; there must be no
   drops of water in the pot while heating
   Heat rapeseed oil until it gives off a little blue smoke, to remove its raw green taste,
   before use (cooked oil)

Storing:
✓ Use dark glass bottles, earthenware jars or stainless-steel drums; fill them full and
  seal tightly (leave little air)
✓ Keep out of the light and cool; light, heat, air, copper and iron speed up rancidity
✓ Take a little at a time; do not open a big jar every day
✓ Traditionally pressed oil usually keeps a few months to half a year; animal fat keeps
  rather longer in winter
✗ Do not keep oil in copper vessels or badly rusted containers

How to tell it is rancid: a "rancid" smell (the sharp smell of stale oil), darker colour,
thicker texture
→ Do not eat it any more; use it for soap or as lamp oil
```

## Workshop safety

### Dust explosions

Flour, rice bran, cornmeal and sawdust all burn. In heaps they do not burn easily, but **fine dust drifting in the air ignites all at once when it meets a spark**, causing an explosion; mill and granary explosions were common throughout history.

```
✓ No naked flames in mill rooms, granaries or where flour is sifted: no oil lamps,
  no smoking, no fires inside
✓ Light with lamps kept away from the dust, or put the lamp under a glass shade and
  hang it outside the door to shine in
✓ Sift flour and tip meal gently so as to raise little dust; sweep the floor and the dust
  on the beams often
✓ Millstones and bearings that run dry can overheat and ignite settled dust too
  → grease and check them regularly
✓ Keep doors and windows open for ventilation so dust can clear
```

For the principle see [Advanced Chemistry – Reaction rates and catalysis](../09-advanced-science-and-engineering/05-advanced-chemistry.md#4-reaction-rate-and-catalysis); for what to do if fire breaks out see [Fire](../02-disaster-response/04-fire.md).

### Entanglement in machinery and draught animals

```
⚠️ Thresher drums, winnowing-fan cranks, watermill gears and oil-press screws can all catch
   sleeves, hair and scarves
   → Tie cuffs tight, put long hair up, wear no scarves or long dangling items
⚠️ Before clearing tangles or adjusting a machine, let it stop completely;
   for a watermill, close the sluice and cut off the water first
⚠️ Put wooden guards over waterwheels and gears; keep children away from the watermill's
   channel and wheel
⚠️ Animal-driven mills: do not step inside the circle the animal walks; when feeding the
   animal or adjusting its harness, stop it first and have someone hold it
⚠️ Ram-type oil presses: nobody may stand within the ram's swing
⚠️ Moving millstones and stone rollers: work as a team with crowbars and rollers;
   do not bend over and force-lift
```

## 📝 Key points of grain and oil processing

```
1. Harvest two or three days early rather than late; start cutting at the beginning of a
   sunny spell, and clear the threshing floor before rain
2. Dry to a safe moisture content before storing: rice <13%, wheat <12%, maize <14%;
   grains should snap cleanly when bitten; check with the salt-bottle test
3. Insects: freeze in the cold, store airtight, mix with plant ash; never fumigate with
   aluminium phosphide yourself
4. Throw away whole batches of mouldy grain, peanuts and maize: do not eat them, press oil
   from them or feed them to livestock
5. Do not mill rice too white (beriberi); if maize is your staple, cook it in limewater
   first (pellagra)
6. Recut the millstone furrows when they wear flat; grind and sift repeatedly to get more
   flour and lose less bran
7. Pressing oil: clean → dehull → roast → crush → steam → make cakes → press;
   the lever press is the easiest to build yourself
8. Do not eat traditionally pressed cottonseed oil; tung oil and castor oil are poisonous:
   store them in their own clearly labelled containers
9. Keep oil out of the light, filled full and tightly sealed; stop eating it once it
   smells rancid
10. No naked flames in mills and granaries (dust explosions); stop machines completely
    before clearing them, and tie your cuffs tight
```
