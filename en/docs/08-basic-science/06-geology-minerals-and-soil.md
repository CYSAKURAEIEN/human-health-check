# Geology, Minerals and Soil

> **Lime comes from limestone, bricks and tiles from clay, iron from haematite, well water from aquifers, and crops grow out of the soil – learn the rocks and soil under your feet and you will know where to look for what you need**

## ⚠️ What this chapter covers

This chapter gives the basics of recognising rocks, minerals, groundwater and soil, to help you judge in the field "what might be here".

```
⚠️ Safety:
- When quarrying, mining or digging wells, collapse is the greatest danger: slope or shore up
  the pit walls, and do not dig below a steep bank
- Abandoned mine shafts, tunnels and the bottoms of deep wells may lack oxygen or collect
  toxic gases; never enter them rashly
- Hillsides slide easily after rain and during the thaw; watch for the warning signs of geological disasters
```

For geological disasters see [Geological Disasters](../02-disaster-response/01-geological-disasters.md); for identifying specific metal ores see [Metallurgy Basics – Identifying and collecting ores](../05-metallurgy/00-metallurgy-basics.md#5-identifying-and-collecting-ore).

## 1. The three main kinds of rock

| Type | How it forms | Common rocks | Features | Uses |
|------|---------|---------|------|------|
| Igneous rock | Molten rock (magma) underground cools and solidifies | Granite, basalt | Hard, usually without layering | Building stone, millstones, paving; granite areas often have tin and tungsten ores, and weather into sandy soil and clay |
| Sedimentary rock | Mud, sand and the remains of living things settle layer by layer and are compacted | Sandstone, shale, limestone, conglomerate, coal | Often clearly layered, may contain fossils | Limestone is burnt for lime and used for cement; sandstone for whetstones and building; shale weathers into clay; coal |
| Metamorphic rock | Existing rock changed by high temperature and pressure | Marble, slate, quartzite, gneiss | Dense, often with a flaky or banded structure | Marble can be burnt for lime and used as stone; slate can be split into thin sheets for roof tiles |

```
Judging in the field:
- Layer upon layer of stripes, possibly fossils → sedimentary rock
- Grains of even size, shiny crystals, no layers → granite or another igneous rock
- Black, dense, with small gas holes → basalt
- Can be split into very thin flat plates → slate, shale
```

## 2. Recognising minerals

### 2.1 Identification methods

```
Hardness: scratch it with things of known hardness
Streak: rub it hard on unglazed white porcelain (e.g. the base of a bowl, the back of a tile)
  and look at the colour of the powder left behind
  → the streak colour is far more reliable than the colour of the mineral's surface
Lustre: metallic (like metal), glassy, greasy, earthy (dull)
Specific gravity: does it feel especially heavy in the hand?
Magnetism: test with a magnet
Cleavage: when broken, does it split along fixed directions into flat faces?
Acid: add a drop of vinegar or dilute hydrochloric acid; fizzing means it contains carbonate
  (limestone, marble)
```

### 2.2 Mohs hardness

| Hardness | Reference mineral | Field comparison |
|------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Talc | A fingernail scratches it easily |
| 2 | Gypsum | A fingernail can scratch it (fingernail about 2.5) |
| 3 | Calcite | A copper coin can scratch it (copper coin about 3.5) |
| 4 | Fluorite | A knife scratches it easily |
| 5 | Apatite | A knife can just scratch it |
| 6 | Orthoclase | Can scratch glass (glass and knife about 5.5) |
| 7 | Quartz | Can scratch a steel file (steel file about 6.5) |
| 8 | Topaz | |
| 9 | Corundum | |
| 10 | Diamond | The hardest |

```
How to use: if A can scratch B, A is harder than B
  Example: a mineral scratches glass, and a knife cannot scratch it
  → hardness above 5.5; it may be quartz
```

### 2.3 Common useful minerals

| Mineral | Appearance | Streak | Other features | Uses |
|------|------|------|---------|------|
| Quartz | Colourless or white, glassy lustre | White | Hardness 7, scratches glass; breaks with a shell-like (conchoidal) fracture | Raw material for glass, abrasive |
| Calcite | White, colourless | White | Hardness 3, fizzes with acid, breaks into rhombic blocks | Main component of limestone |
| Gypsum | White | White | Hardness 2, a fingernail can scratch it | Burnt to plaster of Paris for moulds and setting fractures |
| Magnetite | Black | Black | Strongly magnetic, very heavy | Iron ore |
| Haematite | Reddish-brown to steel grey | **Reddish-brown** | Fairly heavy | Iron ore |
| Limonite | Yellowish-brown, rust-coloured | Yellowish-brown | Often earthy | Iron ore; bog iron ore belongs to this group |
| Pyrite | Golden yellow, metallic lustre, often cubes | **Greenish-black** | Much harder than gold, gives sparks when struck | "Fool's gold"; not gold ore |
| Galena | Lead grey, strong metallic lustre | Greyish-black | Very heavy, cubes | Lead ore (☠️ toxic) |
| Malachite | Bright green | Pale green | Fizzes with acid | Copper ore |
| Cassiterite | Black, brown, strong lustre | White to light brown | Very heavy, very hard | Tin ore |
| Kaolinite (china clay) | White and earthy | White | Fine and smooth, plastic when wet | Porcelain, refractory materials |

```
⚠️ Telling gold from pyrite:
  Gold: very soft (a knife can mark it), very heavy, golden streak, deforms rather than
        shatters when hammered
  Pyrite: very hard, greenish-black streak, shatters into small pieces when hammered
```

For the various metal ores see [Iron Smelting](../05-metallurgy/01-iron-smelting.md), [Copper Smelting](../05-metallurgy/02-copper-smelting.md) and [Other Metals](../05-metallurgy/10-other-metals.md).

## 3. Where to find useful things

### 3.1 Building and household materials

| What you need | Where to look | How to recognise it |
|------|---------|------|
| Limestone | Limestone hills (often with caves, stone forests, underground rivers) | Greyish-white, fizzes with vinegar |
| Clay | Fine soil layers on river terraces, lake shores and low-lying ground; zones of weathered shale | Sticky when wet, can be rolled into a thin thread without breaking (see section 5) |
| Sand | Riverbeds, river beaches, seashores | River sand is cleaner; sea sand contains salt and must be washed before use in building |
| Building stone | Outcrops of granite, sandstone, limestone | Choose stone without cracks and not badly weathered |
| Whetstone | Fine sandstone, slate | Fine, even grains |
| Coal | Sedimentary rock areas, often in layers with shale and sandstone | Black, burns |
| Salt | Seashores, salt lakes, brine wells | Evaporate in the sun or boil down |

### 3.2 Common clues to metal deposits

```
- Placer deposits: rivers carry heavy minerals weathered from the hills (gold, cassiterite)
  and drop them in the riverbed, on the inside of bends, behind large boulders
  → pan the river sand (see Other Metals – gold panning)
- Veins: quartz veins filling cracks in the rock, sometimes with metal sulphides →
  look near quartz veins for minerals with metallic lustre, and green or rusty staining
- "Iron hat" (gossan): a large area of loose, rust-red or brown rock at the surface, left by
  the oxidation of a sulphide ore body below; copper, lead, zinc and other ores may lie beneath
- Green or blue rusty stains: copper ore may be present (malachite, azurite)
- Bog iron ore: brown iron-rich deposits at the bottom of marshes and wetlands, an important
  source of iron in ancient times
- Place names: many place names containing "copper", "iron", "silver", "tin", "alum" or
  "saltpetre" come from ancient mining
```

## 4. Groundwater

### 4.1 Where groundwater is

```
Rain soaks into the ground and is stored in the pores and cracks of rock and soil
- Aquifer: a layer that can hold and pass water, such as sand, gravel, fractured rock,
  or limestone with caves
- Impermeable layer (aquiclude): a layer that does not let water through, such as clay or dense shale
- Water table: the upper limit of fully waterlogged ground; a well must be dug below it

Places where groundwater is easy to find:
✓ Valleys, the foot of hills, alluvial plains near rivers (thick sand and gravel)
✓ Places with springs, or with especially lush plants (reeds, willows and other water-loving plants)
✓ Under dry riverbeds (channels that carry water in the rainy season often have underground flow)
✓ Where a permeable layer meets an impermeable layer; springs often flow out there

Places where it is hard to find:
✗ Hilltops, ridges
✗ Areas of dense rock (look for zones of cracks)
```

### 4.2 Water quality

```
- Groundwater in limestone areas has a lot of calcium and magnesium (hard water): it leaves
  scale when boiled and soap will not lather, but it is generally drinkable
- Well water near the sea may be salty; pumping too much lets seawater flow in
- Shallow groundwater is easily polluted by latrines, cesspits and rubbish → keep wells far
  from sources of pollution, on the uphill side
- In some areas groundwater has too much fluoride or arsenic, and drinking it for a long time
  causes disease → if the area has a history of such local diseases, take special care
```

For digging wells, drawing water and purifying it see [Water Sources and Water Supply](../07-everyday-living-skills/10-water-supply.md).

## 5. Soil

### 5.1 What soil is made of

```
Mineral particles (about half the volume) + organic matter (humus) + water + air
+ huge numbers of microbes

By particle size:
- Sand: coarse, feels gritty, drains fast, holds water and nutrients poorly
- Silt: medium, feels like flour
- Clay: extremely fine, sticky when wet and hard when dry, holds water and nutrients well
  but lets little air in

Loam: a balanced mix of the three, best for growing crops
```

### 5.2 Simple soil texture tests

```
Rolling test:
1. Take a handful of moist soil (add water and knead until it is like modelling clay)
2. Roll it into a thin thread between your palms
- Will not form a thread, falls apart at once → sandy soil
- Forms a thread, but breaks as soon as you bend it → loam
- Forms a long thin thread that can be bent into a ring without breaking → clay

Jar (settling) test (more accurate):
1. Fill a clear jar about one-third with soil, add water almost to the top, and add a pinch
   of salt or washing powder to help the particles separate
2. Shake hard, then leave to stand
3. What settles after about 1 minute is sand, after a few hours is silt, and only after
   one or two days is clay
4. Measure the thickness of each layer to find the rough proportions
```

### 5.3 Acidity and alkalinity

```
Test: mix soil with water and test with red cabbage juice or test paper (see Chemistry – pH)
  - Fizzes with vinegar → contains lime, alkaline
- Acid soils (the red and yellow soils common in southern China): improve with lime or wood ash
- Alkaline and saline-alkaline soils (parts of the north, coasts): flood with water to wash out
  the salt, dig drainage ditches, add more organic manure, grow salt-tolerant crops
- Most crops prefer soil close to neutral
```

For the red cabbage indicator see [Chemistry – pH](03-chemistry.md#61-ph).

For how much lime to use on acid soil see [Lime and Building Materials – Agricultural and other uses](../07-everyday-living-skills/16-lime-and-building-materials.md#farming-and-other-uses).

### 5.4 Soil fertility

```
The nutrients plants need most: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium
- Nitrogen: manure, urine, legumes, green manure
- Phosphorus: bone meal, wood ash, manure
- Potassium: wood ash
Organic matter: compost, farmyard manure, ploughing in straw → makes soil loose, helps it
hold water and nutrients, feeds the microbes

Judging: dark, loose soil with lots of earthworms is usually fairly fertile
```

For fertilisers and compost see [Grain Crops](../04-farming-and-animal-husbandry/02-grain-crops.md) and [Sanitation – Using faeces and urine](../07-everyday-living-skills/03-sanitation.md#using-faeces-and-urine).

### 5.5 Protecting the soil

```
Topsoil (the top ten-odd to several tens of cm) takes hundreds or thousands of years to form,
and one rainstorm can wash it away
✓ On slopes build terraces and plant along the contours (ridges along the contour lines);
  do not make ridges running down the slope
✓ Keep the ground covered with plants or straw as far as possible; do not leave it bare for long
✓ Do not clear steep slopes; keep the woods and grassland
✓ Plant trees along riverbanks and ditch edges to hold the soil
```

For contour lines see [Trip Preparation and Calling for Help – Reading maps and contour lines](../03-wilderness-survival/07-trip-preparation-and-calling-for-help.md#reading-maps-and-contour-lines).

## 6. Reading landforms and rock layers

```
- The direction and angle of tilted rock layers decide which way groundwater flows and ore
  layers extend
- River terraces (step-like platforms beside a river) are mostly ancient riverbeds; they often
  have sand and gravel layers and groundwater, and were where people of old lived and farmed
- "Drunken forest" on a hillside (trees leaning every which way), cracks, water seeping at the
  foot of the slope → the slope may be sliding slowly; do not build nearby
- A fan-shaped deposit at the mouth of a gully (alluvial fan) → flash floods and debris flows
  pass through here
```

For the warning signs of landslides and debris flows see [Geological Disasters](../02-disaster-response/01-geological-disasters.md).
