# The Periodic Table

> **118 elements arranged in one table, with their properties repeating periodically like a calendar — know where an element sits in the table and you can predict most of its properties. This is the most information-packed table in chemistry**

This chapter contains the complete periodic table and basic data for all 118 elements, and explains how to read patterns from the table. ## 1. What the periodic table is

```
Atomic number = number of protons in the nucleus = number of electrons
  (in a neutral atom)
  → the atomic number decides which element it is

Arrange the elements by atomic number from smallest to largest, and start
a new row each time the arrangement of the outermost electrons "starts
again":
- The rows are called periods: an atom in period n has n electron shells
  (7 periods in all)
- The columns are called groups: elements in the same group have the same
  number of outermost electrons (main groups) and similar chemical
  properties (18 columns in all)

Main-group elements (columns 1, 2 and 13-18): the last digit of the group
  number = the number of outermost electrons
  Example: group 1 has 1 outer electron, group 17 has 7
Transition metals (columns 3-12): their properties change more gradually;
  most are hard metals that conduct electricity
Lanthanides and actinides: really belong in column 3 of periods 6 and 7
  respectively, but are too long, so they are placed separately below the
  table
```

```
Rough regions:
- Lower left and middle: metals (the great majority)
- Upper right: non-metals
- Along a staircase line between them (boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic,
  antimony, tellurium): metalloids, with properties in between; many are
  semiconductors
- The far right column: noble gases, which hardly take part in chemical
  reactions
```

## 4. How to read the periodic table

### 4.1 From left to right (across a period)

```
- Atoms get gradually smaller (the nuclear charge rises and holds the
  electrons more tightly)
- Metallic character weakens and non-metallic character strengthens:
  sodium (a very reactive metal) → magnesium → aluminium → silicon →
  phosphorus → sulphur → chlorine (a very reactive non-metal)
- The highest positive valence rises step by step from +1 to +7
- The noble gases on the far right have a full outer shell and hardly
  react
```

### 4.2 From top to bottom (down a group)

```
- Atoms get gradually larger (more electron shells)
- Metals get more and more reactive: lithium < sodium < potassium <
  rubidium < caesium
  → lithium in water bubbles slowly, sodium darts about on the surface,
    potassium bursts into flame at once
- Non-metals get less and less reactive: fluorine > chlorine > bromine >
  iodine
  → chlorine can displace bromine and iodine from their salts
```

### 4.3 Common valences (main groups)

| Group | Outer electrons | Common valences | Examples |
|----|-----------|-----------|------|
| 1 | 1 | +1 | NaCl, KOH |
| 2 | 2 | +2 | CaO, MgCl₂ |
| 13 | 3 | +3 | Al₂O₃ |
| 14 | 4 | +4, -4 | CO₂, SiO₂, CH₄ |
| 15 | 5 | -3, +3, +5 | NH₃, P₂O₅ |
| 16 | 6 | -2 (also +4, +6) | H₂O, SO₂, H₂SO₄ |
| 17 | 7 | -1 (also +1, +5, +7) | NaCl, NaClO |
| 18 | 8 (helium 2) | 0 | Hardly form any compounds |

```
Writing formulas from valences: the positive and negative valences must
add up to zero
  Example: aluminium +3, oxygen -2 → 2 aluminium (+6) and 3 oxygen (-6)
  → Al₂O₃
```

### 4.4 Predicting properties from position: examples

```
Q: What is rubidium (Rb) like?
A: Group 1, period 5 → an alkali metal, more reactive than potassium
   → very soft, silvery white, reacts violently or even explosively with
     water, must be kept under kerosene, valence +1

Q: What properties might selenium (Se) have?
A: Group 16, below sulphur → chemically like sulphur; can form H₂Se and
   SeO₂; valences -2, +4, +6
```

## 5. How Chinese element names are built

Most Chinese element names are a single character made of "radical + sound". The radical tells you the element's state at room temperature:

| Radical | Meaning | Examples |
|------|------|------|
| 钅 (the "metal" radical) | Metal | 钠 sodium, 镁 magnesium, 铝 aluminium, 钾 potassium, 钙 calcium, 铁 iron, 铜 copper, 锌 zinc |
| 气 (the "gas" radical) | Gas at room temperature | 氢 hydrogen, 氦 helium, 氮 nitrogen, 氧 oxygen, 氟 fluorine, 氖 neon, 氯 chlorine, 氩 argon, 氪 krypton, 氙 xenon, 氡 radon |
| 氵 (the "water" radical) / 水 | Liquid at room temperature | 溴 bromine; 汞 mercury (with 水 "water" at the bottom) |
| 石 (the "stone" radical) | Solid non-metal at room temperature | 硼 boron, 碳 carbon, 硅 silicon, 磷 phosphorus, 硫 sulphur, 砷 arsenic, 硒 selenium, 碲 tellurium, 碘 iodine |

```
The other half of the character usually gives the sound:
- Many take the sound of the first syllable of the foreign name: 钠 nà
  (Natrium), 钾, 钙, 镁, 锰……
  This naming system was established in the 1870s by the Qing-dynasty
  chemist Xu Shou and others
- 金 gold, 银 silver, 铜 copper, 铁 iron, 锡 tin, 铅 lead, 汞 mercury,
  硫 sulphur and others were known in ancient times and keep their old
  names
- Exceptions: 金 (gold) is itself a metal; 汞 (mercury) is a liquid metal
```

## 7. Mendeleev's predictions: why the periodic table is science

```
In 1869 the Russian chemist Mendeleev arranged the 63 elements then known
by atomic weight and found that their properties repeated periodically.
He did two bold things:

1. Left gaps: he left places in the table for elements that "should exist
   but had not yet been found"
2. Predicted properties: from the properties of the elements around each
   gap, he predicted the properties of these unknown elements

For example, his "eka-silicon" (below silicon):
                     Mendeleev's prediction (1871)  Germanium as found (1886)
  Relative atomic mass   about 72                       72.6
  Density                about 5.5 g/cm³                5.35 g/cm³
  Oxide                  XO₂, density about 4.7 g/cm³   GeO₂, density 4.7 g/cm³
  Chloride               liquid, boiling below 100°C    GeCl₄, boils at about 86°C

His "eka-aluminium" (gallium, 1875) and "eka-boron" (scandium, 1879) were
also discovered in turn, with properties matching his predictions

→ A good theory does not just sort what is known; it predicts the unknown,
  and it can be tested
→ Even starting from nothing, once you have found this pattern you can
  search for and understand new substances with a sense of direction
```
