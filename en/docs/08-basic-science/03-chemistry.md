# Chemistry

> **Burning lime, smelting iron, making soap, brewing, disinfecting — understand how substances change, and you can turn stone, wood ash and grain into the things you need**

## ⚠️ Safety first

**This chapter explains the principles of chemistry so that you understand the chemical changes in production and daily life. It is not a guide to chemistry experiments.** Many chemicals are corrosive, toxic or flammable:

- ☠️ Strong acids and strong alkalis cause severe burns to skin and eyes; wear gloves and goggles when handling them
- ☠️ Mixing different cleaning products can produce toxic gases (see Section 9)
- ☠️ Heavy metals such as lead and mercury build up in the body and cause chronic poisoning
- 🔥 Alcohol vapour, hydrogen and biogas burn and explode on contact with a naked flame
- 🏭 Any work that gives off gas must be done outdoors or in a well-ventilated place

For actual production work see the relevant chapters in the metallurgy, pharmaceuticals and everyday living skills parts, which have more detailed safety requirements.

## 1. What substances are made of

### 1.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures

```
Element: a basic substance that cannot be broken down further by chemical
  means (e.g. iron, oxygen, carbon); 118 are known so far
Compound: two or more elements joined in fixed proportions
  (e.g. water H₂O, table salt NaCl)
  → its properties are completely different from those of its elements:
    sodium is a metal that bursts into flame in water, chlorine is a toxic
    gas, yet together they make salt that you can eat
Mixture: several substances mixed together in no fixed proportion
  (e.g. air, seawater, soil)
  → can be separated by physical methods (filtering, evaporating, distilling)
```

### 1.2 Atoms and molecules

```
Atom: the smallest unit of an element, made of a nucleus (protons and
  neutrons) and electrons
  The number of protons decides which element it is
  (hydrogen 1, carbon 6, oxygen 8, iron 26)
Molecule: several atoms joined together
  (a water molecule H₂O = 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom)
```

### 1.3 Conservation of mass

```
In a chemical reaction the total mass of the substances does not change,
and neither do the kinds or numbers of atoms
  → atoms do not appear out of nothing or vanish; they only regroup
  → burnt wood leaves only a little ash because most of it has turned into
    carbon dioxide and water vapour and gone into the air
  → rusted iron is heavier because it has combined with oxygen and water
    from the air
```

### 1.4 The mole: the unit of chemical calculation

```
1 mol = 6.02 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant)
The mass (in g) of 1 mol of a substance = its relative molecular mass
  Water H₂O: 1×2 + 16 = 18 → 1 mol of water = 18 g
  Carbon dioxide CO₂: 12 + 16×2 = 44 → 1 mol = 44 g

Use: working out from an equation how much raw material you need and how
  much product you will get
  (example in Section 3)
```

### 2.2 The reactivity series of metals

```
Potassium Calcium Sodium Magnesium Aluminium Zinc Iron Tin Lead (Hydrogen) Copper Mercury Silver Platinum Gold
K  Ca Na Mg Al Zn Fe Sn Pb (H) Cu Hg Ag Pt Au
←── more reactive                          more stable ──→

Uses:
1. The further right a metal is, the easier it is to smelt from ore
   - Gold and silver can be found as pure metal in nature; copper, tin and
     lead can be smelted with charcoal alone
   - Iron needs a higher temperature; aluminium and magnesium need
     electrolysis and could not be made in ancient times
   → This is why people entered the Bronze Age first, then the Iron Age

2. A metal further left can displace a metal further right from a solution
   of its salt
   - Put iron into copper sulphate solution and copper is deposited on its
     surface (the ancient "wet process" for copper)
   Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

3. Metals to the left of hydrogen react with dilute acids and give off
   hydrogen;
   do not keep acidic food (vinegar, fruit juice, pickles) in iron, zinc
   or aluminium containers for long

4. When two different metals are joined and kept damp, the more reactive
   one corrodes first
   → Galvanised iron sheet: the zinc corrodes first and protects the iron
   → Batteries: use the difference in reactivity between two metals to
     produce a voltage
```

## 3. Chemical equations

### 3.1 Writing and balancing

```
Reactants → products; each kind of atom must have the same number on
both sides

Example: burning hydrogen
  H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (not balanced: 2 oxygen on the left, 1 on the right)
  2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (balanced: 4 hydrogen and 2 oxygen on each side)

Common marks: ↑ gas given off; ↓ precipitate formed; △ heat;
  ⇌ reversible reaction
```

### 3.2 Calculating with equations

```
Example: how much quicklime do you get from burning 100 kg of limestone
(calcium carbonate)?
  CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂↑
  100      56     44   (relative molecular masses: 40+12+48=100;
                        40+16=56; 12+32=44)
  → 100 kg of calcium carbonate gives in theory 56 kg of quicklime and
    releases 44 kg of carbon dioxide
  (impure limestone or incomplete burning gives less in practice)
```

## 4. Important reactions that changed human society

### 4.1 Combustion

```
Complete combustion (plenty of oxygen):
  C + O₂ → CO₂
  CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (biogas, natural gas)

Incomplete combustion (too little oxygen):
  2C + O₂ → 2CO
  ⚠️ Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and highly toxic → burning
     charcoal indoors or a badly ventilated stove can kill

The three things fire needs: fuel + oxygen + reaching the ignition point
  Putting out a fire means removing one of them: cut off the air
  (smother it), cool it (pour water on it), take away the fuel
```

For carbon monoxide poisoning see [Home and traffic accidents – Carbon monoxide poisoning](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/07-居家与交通意外.md#一氧化碳中毒).

### 4.2 Smelting: from ore to metal

```
In ore, metals are present as oxides and other compounds; smelting uses
carbon (charcoal) to "snatch" the oxygen away:
  Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (iron smelting: haematite + carbon monoxide)
  2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂ (copper smelting)

The charcoal first burns incompletely to carbon monoxide, which then
reduces the ore
Limestone is added as a flux; it combines with impurities in the ore
(mainly silica) to form slag
```

For details see [Iron smelting](../../../docs/05-冶金技术/01-铁的冶炼.md) and [Copper smelting](../../../docs/05-冶金技术/02-铜的冶炼.md).

### 4.3 Rusting

```
Iron + oxygen + water → rust (mainly hydrated iron oxide)
  With only oxygen or only water, iron does not rust easily;
  salt speeds rusting up

Preventing rust: keep out air and water
  - Oil or paint
  - Galvanise (zinc) or tin-plate
  - Keep dry
```

### 4.4 The lime cycle: the basis of building

```
1. Burning lime (above about 900°C):
   CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂↑
   limestone → quicklime

2. Slaking (adding water):
   CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + a lot of heat
   quicklime → slaked lime
   ⚠️ Gives off enough heat to boil water; burns skin and eyes

3. Setting (slowly absorbing carbon dioxide from the air):
   Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O
   slaked lime → hard calcium carbonate again

Uses: lime mortar for laying walls, limewash for walls (limewater has
  some germicidal effect), improving acid soil, disinfection (sprinkled
  on the carcasses of animals that died of disease), making alkali
  (see below)
```

For how to fire a kiln, slake lime and make mortar in practice see [Lime and building materials](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/16-石灰与建筑材料.md).

### 4.5 Alkali and soap

```
Wood ash contains potassium carbonate (K₂CO₃); soaking it in water gives
lye water

Adding slaked lime gives a stronger alkali (causticising):
  K₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2KOH + CaCO₃↓

Saponification (making soap):
  fat or oil + alkali → soap + glycerol
  One end of a soap molecule is attracted to water and the other to oil,
  so it can "pull" grease into the water and wash it away
```

For how to do it see [Soap making](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/04-制皂.md).

### 4.6 Fermentation

```
Alcoholic fermentation (yeast, no oxygen):
  C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂↑
  glucose → alcohol + carbon dioxide (carbon dioxide is also what makes
  dough rise)

Acetic fermentation (acetic acid bacteria, needs oxygen):
  C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CH₃COOH + H₂O
  alcohol → acetic acid (why wine left too long turns sour)

Lactic fermentation (lactic acid bacteria, no oxygen):
  glucose → lactic acid
  Sauerkraut, pickles, yoghurt; the acidity stops other bacteria
  growing, so the food keeps
```

For fermenting food see [Food processing and preservation](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/01-食物加工与保存.md); for distilling alcohol and using it as a disinfectant see [Disinfectants](../../../docs/06-制药技术/01-消毒剂制备.md).

### 4.7 Photosynthesis and respiration

```
Photosynthesis (plants, needs light):
  6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
  carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  → the source of almost all food and oxygen on Earth

Respiration (all living things):
  C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy
  → the reverse of photosynthesis; releases energy for life
  → In a closed root cellar or grain store, the respiration of the grain
    and vegetables uses up oxygen and builds up carbon dioxide;
    ventilate before going down into a cellar
```

### 4.8 Chlorine disinfection

```
Sodium hypochlorite (the active ingredient of "84" bleach): NaClO
Active ingredient of bleaching powder: calcium hypochlorite Ca(ClO)₂

In water they produce hypochlorous acid (HClO), which kills bacteria and
viruses
  → disinfecting drinking water and surfaces

⚠️ Mixed with acidic products (toilet cleaner) they give off toxic
   chlorine gas
```

For making up disinfectant solutions see [Infectious disease at home – Making household disinfectant](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/15-传染病家庭防控.md#家用消毒液的配制).

### 4.9 Cement and glass

```
Cement: limestone + clay, ground fine, mixed and burnt at about 1450°C
  into clinker, then ground into powder
  With water it hydrates and slowly hardens, even under water
  → much stronger and more water-resistant than lime mortar; needs a very
    high temperature and is hard to make well by traditional methods

Glass: quartz sand (SiO₂) + soda ash (Na₂CO₃) + limestone, melted at
  about 1400-1500°C
  Na₂CO₃ + SiO₂ → Na₂SiO₃ + CO₂↑
  → uses: containers, windows, lenses (spectacles, magnifying glasses,
    microscopes)
```

### 4.10 Nitrogen fertiliser and ammonia synthesis

```
Nitrogen is the element plants need most. Air is 78% nitrogen, but
plants cannot use it directly

Natural ways of fixing nitrogen:
- Root-nodule bacteria on legumes (soya beans, peanuts, lucerne, Chinese
  milk vetch) fix nitrogen from the air
- Lightning
- Animal dung and human urine contain a lot of nitrogen

Ammonia synthesis (the Haber process, early 20th century):
  N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
  Needs high temperature (about 400-500°C), high pressure (several
  hundred atm) and an iron catalyst
  → Chemical fertiliser has greatly raised grain yields; a large share
    of the world's population today depends on it for food
  → It needs large industrial plant and cannot be restored soon after
    civilisation collapses;
    soil fertility will have to be kept up by rotating with legumes,
    manure and urine
```

For using fertilisers in practice see [Grain crops](../../../docs/04-动植物培育/02-粮食作物.md) and [Sanitation – Using faeces and urine](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/03-卫生设施.md#粪便与尿液的利用).

### 4.11 Other important basic chemical industries

```
Sulphuric acid: called "the mother of the chemical industry"; used for
  fertilisers, treating ores, storage batteries and more
  burn sulphur → sulphur dioxide → oxidise further → sulphur trioxide
  → absorbed to form sulphuric acid

Soda ash (sodium carbonate): for glass, soap and paper
  Historically from wood ash, seaweed ash and natural soda lakes; later
  produced industrially from salt and limestone

Electrolysis: splitting substances with an electric current
  Electrolysing brine → caustic soda (NaOH) + chlorine + hydrogen
  ⚠️ The chlorine produced is toxic and the hydrogen explosive; do this
     only under professional conditions
```

## 5. Solutions and concentration

### 5.1 Expressing concentration

```
Mass fraction = mass of solute ÷ total mass of solution × 100%
  Example: 9 g of salt dissolved in 991 g of water → 0.9% salt water
  (the concentration of normal saline)

Volume fraction = volume of solute ÷ total volume of solution × 100%
  Example: 75% alcohol = 75 mL of alcohol in every 100 mL

Molar concentration = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution (L),
  unit mol/L

Parts per million (ppm, mg/L): for very dilute solutions
  Example: 500 mg/L available chlorine = 0.05%
```

### 5.2 Dilution

```
The amount of solute is the same before and after diluting:
  concentration₁ × volume₁ = concentration₂ × volume₂

Example: making 100 mL of 75% alcohol from 95% alcohol
  95% × V₁ = 75% × 100 → V₁ ≈ 79 mL, add water to 100 mL
```

### 5.3 Solubility

```
The greatest mass of solute that 100 g of water can dissolve at a given
temperature
  - Most solids: the hotter, the more dissolves (sugar dissolves faster
    and more in hot water)
  - Salt: solubility hardly changes with temperature (about 36 g)
  - Gases: the hotter, the less dissolves (bubbles appear as water
    heats; fish ponds run short of oxygen in hot weather)

Saturated solution: cannot dissolve any more solute
Purifying by recrystallisation: dissolve in hot water to saturation →
  filter off the insoluble impurities → on cooling, purer crystals
  come out
```

## 6. Acids, bases and salts

### 6.1 pH

```
pH 0 ─────── 7 ─────── 14
   strong    neutral    strong
   acid                 alkali

  Stomach acid about 1-2, vinegar about 3, pure water 7, soapy water
  about 9-10, limewater about 12, caustic soda solution about 14

Home-made acid-base indicator: chop red cabbage (purple cabbage) and
  steep it in hot water to get a purple juice
  It turns red or pink with acid, stays purple when neutral, turns blue
  or green with alkali, and yellow with strong alkali
```

### 6.2 Common acids and bases

| Name | Formula | Common name / source | Caution |
|------|--------|----------|------|
| Hydrochloric acid | HCl | Part of stomach acid; toilet cleaner | Corrosive |
| Sulphuric acid | H₂SO₄ | Battery acid | Concentrated acid is extremely corrosive and heats violently with water |
| Acetic acid | CH₃COOH | Vinegar | Weak acid |
| Carbonic acid | H₂CO₃ | Carbon dioxide dissolved in water (fizzy drinks) | Weak acid |
| Sodium hydroxide | NaOH | Caustic soda, lye | Strongly corrosive |
| Potassium hydroxide | KOH | Wood-ash lye with lime added | Strongly corrosive |
| Calcium hydroxide | Ca(OH)₂ | Slaked lime | Corrosive |
| Sodium carbonate | Na₂CO₃ | Soda ash, washing soda | Alkaline in solution |
| Sodium bicarbonate | NaHCO₃ | Baking soda | Mildly alkaline, edible |

### 6.3 Neutralisation

```
Acid + base → salt + water
  HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

Baking soda gives off carbon dioxide with acid:
  NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑
  → raising dough (baking soda + something acidic), fire extinguishers

⚠️ Acid or alkali splashed on skin or eyes: rinse at once with plenty of
   clean water for at least 15 minutes (alkali does more harm; rinse for
   30 minutes or more). Do not try to "neutralise" it with another acid or
   alkali (this gives off heat and makes the burn worse)
```

For treating chemical burns see [Wound and injury care – Chemical burns](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/02-外伤处理.md#化学烧伤).

### 6.4 Diluting concentrated acid: acid into water

```
⚠️ To dilute concentrated sulphuric acid, pour the acid slowly into the
   water down the side of the container, stirring as you pour
❌ Never pour water into concentrated sulphuric acid: the water boils
   instantly and spatters the acid
```

## 7. Basics of organic chemistry

### 7.1 Carbon compounds

```
A carbon atom can bond to 4 other atoms and can form long chains and
rings → it makes an enormous variety of compounds
  All living things are made mainly of carbon compounds

Common organic substances:
- Methane CH₄: the main component of biogas and natural gas
- Ethanol C₂H₅OH: alcohol
- Methanol CH₃OH: ⚠️ highly toxic; a small amount can blind or kill;
  the cause of poisoning from counterfeit liquor
- Ethanoic acid CH₃COOH: acetic acid
```

For methanol poisoning see the section on counterfeit liquor (methanol) poisoning in [Poisoning](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/12-中毒.md).

### 7.2 Esterification

```
Acid + alcohol → ester + water
  acetic acid + ethanol → ethyl acetate (fragrant; one source of the
  aroma of aged liquor)
Fats and oils are esters of glycerol and fatty acids
```

### 7.3 Nutrients

```
Carbohydrates: glucose, sucrose, starch (grain), cellulose (plant fibre,
  wood, cotton)
  People can digest starch but not cellulose (cattle and sheep can,
  thanks to the microbes in their stomachs)
Fats and oils: store the most energy (about 9 kcal/g; carbohydrates and
  protein about 4 kcal/g)
Proteins: made of amino acids; contain nitrogen
  Heat, alcohol, strong acids and alkalis, and heavy metals "denature"
  proteins so that they stop working
  → why cooking food and disinfecting with alcohol kill germs
  → why heavy metals poison
```

For nutritional needs see [Nutrition and deficiency diseases](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/02-营养与营养缺乏病.md).

## 8. Separation and purification

| Method | Principle | Applications |
|------|------|------|
| Filtering | Insoluble particles are held back by cloth or a layer of sand | Purifying water, filtering lye |
| Settling | Heavy particles sink to the bottom | Letting muddy water stand; clearing water with alum |
| Evaporation and crystallisation | Heat or sun-dry; the solvent goes, the solute stays | Sun-drying salt, boiling down brine |
| Distillation | Different substances have different boiling points; on heating they vaporise one after another and are condensed | Purifying alcohol, making fresh water from seawater |
| Extraction (leaching) | The active substance dissolves better in a particular liquid | Steeping medicinal wine, extracting herbs with hot water |
| Recrystallisation | Different substances' solubility changes differently with temperature | Purifying salts |

```
Clearing water with alum: in water, alum forms fluffy aluminium hydroxide,
which traps suspended particles and carries them down as it settles
  → this only makes water clear; it does not kill germs, so you still
    need to boil or disinfect it
```

## 9. Chemical safety

### 9.1 Dangerous combinations

```
❌ Chlorine disinfectant ("84", bleaching powder) + acidic cleaner (toilet
   cleaner) → chlorine gas
❌ Chlorine disinfectant + ammonia, or places with a lot of urine →
   chloramines and other toxic gases
❌ Do not mix different chemicals, and never mix anything whose contents
   you do not know
```

### 9.2 Toxic gases

```
Carbon monoxide (CO): from incomplete combustion; colourless, odourless
Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S): smells of rotten eggs; collects in cesspits,
  biogas digesters and sewers; at high concentration you can no longer
  smell it, and it kills quickly
Carbon dioxide (CO₂): heavier than air; collects at the bottom of cellars,
  dry wells and biogas digesters and suffocates people
Chlorine (Cl₂): yellow-green, choking smell

→ Ventilate cellars, dry wells, biogas digesters and cesspits before going
  in; do not rush down to rescue someone
```

For the dangers of biogas digesters see [Lighting and energy – Biogas](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/06-照明与能源.md#沼气).

### 9.3 Heavy metals

```
Lead: do not keep wine, vinegar, fruit juice or other acidic food and drink
  in lead or lead-containing containers or pewter pots (old pewter often
  contains lead)
  → acid dissolves the lead, causing chronic poisoning over time (anaemia,
    nerve damage, and in children harm to mental development)
Mercury: if a mercury thermometer breaks, open the windows, gather the
  droplets with a piece of stiff card, and put them in a sealed jar;
  do not use a vacuum cleaner, and do not sweep it about
```

For treating poisoning see [Poisoning](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/12-中毒.md).
