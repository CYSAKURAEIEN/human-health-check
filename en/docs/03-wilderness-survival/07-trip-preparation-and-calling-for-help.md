## How most wilderness accidents happen

Surviving on a desert island or in a desert is rare. Far more common in real life: a weekend hike on an "unofficial route", not getting off the mountain before dark, a flat phone battery, a sudden change in the weather, getting separated.

```
Common causes:
- Entering undeveloped or closed areas without permission, walking unmaintained
  "backpacker routes"
- Not telling anyone where you have gone
- Underestimating the distance, setting off late, still in the mountains after dark
- Bringing only a T-shirt, with no warm or waterproof clothing
- Relying only on phone navigation, then the phone dies or has no signal
- Not checking the weather forecast, or pressing on when you can see it turning
- The group gets separated, someone falls behind
```

⚠️ Huangshan and several other scenic areas now charge for rescue: if you get into trouble after entering an undeveloped or closed area without permission, the organisers and the rescued people pay the rescue costs. **More importantly, rescue in such areas often takes a long time, and sometimes comes too late.**

## Before you set out

### Leave a trip plan

**Before you set out, tell someone who is not coming with you about your trip.** This is the simplest step and the one most often skipped.

```
The trip plan says clearly:
- Where: start, route, finish (ideally send a screenshot of the route)
- When you leave and when you expect to be back
- Who is going with you and their phone numbers
- Where the car is parked and its number plate
- The arrangement: "If you cannot reach me after a certain time, call the police"

✓ If you change route on the way, tell them as soon as you have signal
✓ When you get back, tell them you are safe
```

### Check before you go

```
✓ Weather forecast: if there are warnings of heavy rain, thunderstorms, strong wind or a
  temperature drop, postpone
  (flash floods can strike within hours of heavy rain in the mountains; see River
  Crossing, Flash Floods and Hypothermia)
✓ Route: is it an open, official route; its length, height gain, and how many hours it takes
✓ Sunset time: plan to be off the mountain 1-2 hours before sunset
✓ Whether there are water points, phone signal and escape routes along the way
✓ Your own and your companions' fitness; shorten the route when bringing elderly people
  or children
```

Related chapters: [River Crossing, Flash Floods and Hypothermia](../../../docs/03-野外生存/08-涉水山洪与野外失温.md).

### Turn-around time

```
Before you set out, set a "turn-around time" (e.g. 2 pm):
- When that time comes, head back whether or not you have reached your goal
- Most people who get stranded got there by thinking "it's just a bit further"
```

## Equipment

Even for a day hike of a few hours, prepare as if "you may have to spend a night outdoors".

```
Must carry:
- [ ] Phone + fully charged power bank
- [ ] Head torch or torch, plus spare batteries (darkness is one of the commonest
      reasons people get stranded)
- [ ] Warm layer: fleece or light down jacket (carry one even on summer mountains)
- [ ] Rain protection: raincoat or waterproof shell jacket (also needed in every season)
- [ ] Enough water (generally at least 1.5-2 L per person, more in heat or on long routes)
- [ ] High-energy food, a bit more than planned
- [ ] Whistle (shouting soon makes you hoarse; a whistle carries further)
- [ ] First-aid kit: plasters, gauze, bandages, elastic bandage, personal medicines
- [ ] Emergency survival blanket (foil blanket, very light)
- [ ] Lighter

Recommended:
- [ ] Trekking poles (very useful for river crossings and descents)
- [ ] Offline maps (downloaded before you set out), compass
- [ ] Hat, sunglasses, sunscreen
- [ ] Insect repellent (against ticks, mosquitoes)

Clothing:
✓ Well-fitting hiking boots or trainers, not new shoes
✓ Quick-drying clothes, no cotton (wet cotton does not keep you warm, a major cause
  of hypothermia)
✓ Long trousers, tucked into your socks (against ticks and scratches)
```

## On the way

```
✓ Follow the waymarks and obvious paths; do not take "shortcuts"
✓ Look back at the way you came every so often (so you recognise it on the way back)
✓ Remember the features of forks and changes in terrain, or take photos
✓ Put your phone on power-saving mode and look at the map only when needed; turn on
  location sharing when you have signal
✓ Group: the slowest person walks at the front, an experienced person at the back;
  nobody gets left behind
✓ Weather turning bad, someone running out of strength, not enough time → turn back decisively
❌ Do not leave the group alone to take photos or go to the toilet; if you leave,
   tell your companions
```

## Realising you are lost

```
1. Stop. Do not panic, do not keep wandering
   (going further and getting more tired is the commonest mistake once lost)
2. Think: when were you last sure where you were, and roughly how long and in which
   direction have you walked since?
3. Check your phone: is there signal? Can you see your position on the map?
4. If you can find the way you came and are sure it is safe, go back along it
5. If you are not sure:
   - Wait where you are and phone the police
   - If it is getting dark, first prepare to spend the night where you are
     (keep warm, find shelter from the wind)
6. Do not follow streams or gorges downhill
   - Many people think "follow the water down and you'll get out"
   - But mountain stream valleys often have waterfalls and cliffs, and you may meet a
     flash flood
   - They usually get steeper and steeper, until you are stuck halfway, unable to go up
     or down
```

For more on what to do once lost (the STOP principle), see [Basic Survival Skills – If you get lost](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#如果迷路).

## Calling for help: say clearly where you are

### Which number to call

```
✓ 110 or 119 (either will do; they coordinate the rescue)
✓ Call as soon as you have signal; do not wait until it is dark or your phone is
  nearly flat
✓ If calls do not get through, send a text; texts get out more easily
  - You can text the police at 12110 (followed by the province code)
✓ Some phones support satellite messaging, so you can call for help even without
  ground signal
```

Related chapters: [Emergency Communication and Finding Family – During a disaster: how to get a message out](../../../docs/02-灾难应对/06-应急通信与寻亲.md#灾中-怎样让消息发出去).

### How to describe your location

**The hardest part of a rescue is finding you.** Just saying "I'm on such-and-such mountain" is almost useless.

```
1. Latitude and longitude (most accurate):
   - Open the map app on your phone and long-press the blue dot where you are; it
     usually shows the coordinates
   - You can also see them in apps such as the compass
   - Read the numbers out one by one to the call handler, or send them by text

2. Send your location:
   - Use a chat app to send your live location to family or friends, who pass it on
     to the rescuers

3. Describe:
   - Where you started, how long you have walked, roughly in which direction
   - What you can see around you: summits, rivers, pylons, houses, roads
   - Altitude (your phone or watch may show it)

4. Also say:
   - How many people, whether anyone is injured and how badly
   - What equipment you have: head torch, warm clothing, food and water
   - How much battery your phone has left

✓ After the call, keep your phone on to take calls from the rescuers; otherwise save battery
✓ Do not move: once you have given your location, stay there
```

## Waiting for rescue

```
✓ Stay where you are (or at the nearest open, sheltered, safe spot); do not wander
✓ Keeping warm comes first: put on all your clothes, sit on your rucksack or branches,
  not directly on the ground
  - Wrap yourself in the emergency survival blanket
  - Huddle together
✓ Make yourself easy to see:
  - Spread bright clothes and items out in the open
  - At night use your head torch or phone torch
  - When you hear voices or see lights, blow your whistle (three blasts at a time)
✓ Save your strength and phone battery
✓ If you have water, sip it; share out the food
```

For distress signals, see [Basic Survival Skills – Distress signals](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#求救信号); for building a shelter, see [Basic Survival Skills – Building a shelter](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#庇护所建造).

## Reading maps and contour lines

When your phone is flat, a paper map and compass are the most reliable navigation tools. Print or buy a map of the area of your route before you set out.

### Contour lines

```
A contour line joins points at the same height:
- Lines close together → steep slope; lines far apart → gentle slope
- Lines squeezed together, almost overlapping → cliff or steep wall, impassable
- Small closed rings one inside another → summit
- Contours bending out towards higher ground (point of the V aiming uphill)
  → valley or gully, may have water
- Contours bending out towards lower ground → ridge
- The low point between two summits → saddle (pass), often the route over the mountain
```

### Using a map and compass

```
1. Lay the map flat and turn it until north on the map matches north on the compass
2. Find obvious features around you on the map: summits, rivers, lakes, roads
3. Work out where you are on the map
4. Find the direction you want to go on the map and walk on that compass bearing

⚠️ Keep the compass away from phones, knives, cars and other iron or magnetic objects,
   or it will point the wrong way
```

For basic compass use, see [Basic Survival Skills – Navigation and orientation](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#导航与定向).

### Phone navigation

```
✓ Download offline maps and route tracks before you set out
✓ Turn on track recording: if you get lost you can follow the track back
✓ GPS works even in flight mode (positioning does not need phone signal), and it saves battery
✓ The phone is one tool, not the only tool: carry a power bank, or a paper map
```

## 📝 Trip key points

```
1. Before you set out, leave your trip plan with someone not coming with you, and agree
   that they call the police if they cannot reach you
2. Walk only open, official routes; do not enter undeveloped areas
3. Check the weather, set a turn-around time, be off the mountain before dark
4. Even on a day hike, carry a head torch, warm layer, rainwear, power bank and whistle
5. If lost, stop first; do not follow stream valleys downhill
6. Call the police as soon as you have signal, give your coordinates or send your
   location, then stay where you are
7. While waiting, keeping warm comes first; make yourself easy to see
```
