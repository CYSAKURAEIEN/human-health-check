# Forest Survival Guide

The commonest dangers in a forest are not wild animals but **getting lost, nightfall, hypothermia from getting soaked, and falls**. In a forest you can see only a few tens of metres, the trees all look alike, the sun is often hidden by the canopy, and it is easy to walk in circles without noticing. This chapter covers how to keep yourself safe when lost or stranded in a forest until you are rescued or walk out.

⚠️ Principles: call for help and wait for rescue first; do not take risks to press on; do not eat anything you do not recognise. For preparation before setting out and how to call for help see [Outdoor Trip Preparation and Calling for Help](07-trip-preparation-and-calling-for-help.md).

## Finding your direction

### Don't get lost

```
✓ Keep to existing paths/rivers
  - In a forest, do not leave the path to "take a short cut"; paths fade where leaves are
    thick, so when you cannot see the path, stop and find the next waymark
    (trail ribbons, paint marks, trampled ground) before going on
✓ Leave marks (cloth strips, blazes, stone piles)
  - Do this even when leaving the main path to fetch water, go to the toilet or gather wood;
    a few tens of metres into the forest you may not find your way back
✓ Remember landmarks
  - Unusual big trees, rocks, stream junctions, gaps in a ridge;
    note the time as you pass each one
✓ Look back regularly
  - The view on the way back is completely different from the way out; look back every
    few minutes and remember "what it looks like coming back"
✓ Rivers generally lead to people (but following a stream valley is dangerous;
  see "Following a river downstream" below)

More:
✓ Before setting out, download offline maps, keep your phone's location track recording
  on, and carry a power bank
✓ Check your position on the map at regular intervals (for example every 30 minutes, or
  at every fork); do not wait until nothing looks familiar before you look
✓ After three or four in the afternoon the light in a forest fades fast; start thinking
  about turning back or spending the night 1–2 hours before dark
```

For trip plans, turn-around times and equipment see [Outdoor Trip Preparation and Calling for Help – Before you set out](07-trip-preparation-and-calling-for-help.md#before-you-set-out).

### Already lost

```
STOP:
S - Stop
T - Think
O - Observe
P - Plan

Principles:
- If someone knows your route and will come searching: stay where you are and send
  signals (top priority)
- If you are sure nobody will search and you must get yourself out:
  * First go to high ground to look at the land and confirm the direction of roads
    and villages
  * Prefer to follow ridges and existing paths
  * ⚠️ Do not blindly follow a stream valley downhill: stream valleys often lead into
    gorges, waterfalls and cliffs, and flash floods come down them after rain
```

**What to do, step by step**:
```
1. Stop, sit down, have a drink and something to eat, and calm yourself
   (in a panic people walk faster and wander, usually getting farther away and more tired)
2. Check your phone first:
   - Signal: call 110 or 119 at once and send your location; then stay where you are
   - If a call will not go through, send a text; texts get through more easily
   - No signal: open the offline map to see your position (GPS does not need phone signal);
     put the phone in flight mode to save battery and switch flight mode off now and then
     to try for signal; walking a few tens of metres to higher, open ground may find signal
3. Think back: where were you last sure of your position, how long have you walked since,
   and roughly in which direction?
4. Make a clear mark at your current position (see "Leaving marks") as your "origin"
5. With the origin as centre, make short trial searches for the way you came
   (no more than a few tens of metres each time, always keeping the origin mark in sight);
   if you do not find it, return to the origin
6. If you have not found the way 2 hours before dark: stop moving and get ready for the night
   (shelter, warmth, fire)
   ❌ Do not push on through the forest in the dark: the risk of falls, going over a cliff
      or into water at night is far greater than spending a night where you are
```

For how to call the police and describe your location see [Outdoor Trip Preparation and Calling for Help – Calling for help](07-trip-preparation-and-calling-for-help.md#calling-for-help-say-clearly-where-you-are); for distress signals see [Basic Survival Skills – Distress signals](00-basic-survival-skills.md#distress-signals).

### Stay put or move?

| Situation | Advice |
|------|------|
| Someone knows your route and when to raise the alarm | **Stay where you are**; the search starts along your planned route |
| You have already called for help and rescuers know where you are | **Stay where you are**; do not change places (unless there is a direct danger such as a flash flood, forest fire or rockfall) |
| Someone is injured, it is nearly dark, the weather is bad, or you are exhausted | **Stay where you are**; get warm first |
| Where you are is dense forest or a valley floor, invisible from the air and from a distance | You may move to open ground, a clearing or the forest edge **nearby** (within a few hundred metres), and leave a mark at the old spot pointing to the new one |
| You are sure nobody knows you went into the hills, you have waited 1–2 days with no sign of a search, and you can still walk | You may consider getting yourself out: first climb to look for a way, follow ridges or paths; see "Navigation" below |

```
Why "staying put" is usually better:
- Searchers work along your planned route and from your last known position;
  a moving target is hard to find
- Staying still saves energy and body heat; the more a lost person walks,
  the greater the risk of hypothermia and falls
- Search helicopters and drones find it hard to see a person moving under the canopy;
  spreading out brightly coloured things and making smoke in the open gets you found
  more easily

While you wait:
✓ Blow your whistle at intervals, three blasts at a time
  (less effort than shouting, and it carries farther)
✓ When you hear voices, dogs barking, engines or a helicopter, blow the whistle at once,
  wave brightly coloured clothing and light signal smoke
✓ By day make smoke in the open (green branches and leaves on the fire make white smoke);
  at night keep the fire visible
```

**Taking children into the hills**:
```
Before setting out, tell the child: "If you can't find the grown-ups, hug a tree
and don't walk any further."
✓ Give the child a whistle to carry, and teach them to blow it when they hear their name called
✓ Tell the child: the search-and-rescue people are looking for you; answer when someone
  calls you, don't hide
  (lost children often hide because they are afraid of "strangers" or of being told off)
✓ Dress the child in bright colours
```

### Reliable ways to find direction

When you are lost, what you most need is usually not "which way is north" but "which way are the road and the village". Knowing direction only helps if you roughly know where you are and in which direction the place you want to reach lies.

| Method | Reliability | Notes |
|------|---------|------|
| Phone map location | High (while the battery lasts) | Does not need phone signal; download offline maps before going into the hills |
| Compass | High | Keep away from iron objects and electronics; the red needle points to magnetic north, which differs from true north by a few degrees (magnetic declination); this can generally be ignored when hiking |
| Sun | Medium–high | Mid-latitudes of the northern hemisphere: roughly east in the morning, south at noon, west in the evening; the shadow-stick method is more accurate |
| Pole Star | High (clear nights) | Points due north, within 1° |
| Tree crowns, growth rings, moss, ant nests | **Low** | See the next section; do not rely on them |

```
Using a direction in the forest:
1. Fix the direction to walk with a compass or the sun
2. Look out along that direction and pick an easily recognised target in the distance
   (an unusual tree, a big rock)
3. Walk to the target, then pick the next one
   (walking by feel without targets, you will almost certainly go in circles in a forest)
4. When you have to detour round an obstacle, get back onto your original line afterwards
5. The sun moves (about 15° an hour); when using the sun for direction,
   recheck every half hour
```

For the compass, watch method, shadow stick and Pole Star in detail see [Basic Survival Skills – Navigation and orientation](00-basic-survival-skills.md#navigation-and-orientation); for using a map and compass together see [Outdoor Trip Preparation and Calling for Help – Using a map and compass](07-trip-preparation-and-calling-for-help.md#using-a-map-and-compass); for more precise astronomical direction-finding and magnetic declination see [Advanced Celestial Navigation](../../../docs/09-进阶科学与工程/09-天文导航进阶.md#二精确定方向).

### Why tree crowns, growth rings and moss are unreliable

Folk wisdom says "tree crowns are thicker on the south side, growth rings are wider to the south and narrower to the north, and moss grows on the north side". There is a little truth in this (in the northern hemisphere the south side gets more light), but **in a forest it is almost useless**:

```
❌ Tree crowns: in a forest trees shade each other, and crowns grow towards gaps,
   downslope and towards wherever there is more light, with little to do with north
   and south; trees on slopes generally lean out from the slope
❌ Growth rings: ring width depends mainly on slope, wind direction, lean of the trunk
   and competition from neighbouring trees; in many trees the rings are almost equally
   wide on the north and south sides, and in some they are wider on the north
❌ Moss: moss grows in damp, shady places. In a forest everywhere is shady and damp,
   so it may cover all sides; there is more moss on the side facing a stream, the side
   facing the rain, and the underside of a leaning trunk
❌ Ant nests on the south side of trees, snow melting more slowly on the north side:
   sometimes true in the open, unreliable in a forest

Conclusion:
- Only when you cannot find the sun or stars and have no compass, look at many trees
  together and use them as a very rough guide
- Do not choose your direction from a single tree or a single patch of moss
```

## Water

### Finding it

```
Clues:
✓ Lush vegetation
  - A patch of especially green, dense plants in the forest (reeds, ferns, willows,
    bamboo thickets) often has water beneath it
✓ Animal trails converging
  - Several game trails joining and heading downhill often lead to water
✓ Lots of insects
  - Many mosquitoes and dragonflies mean still water nearby; bees often fly back and forth
    between water and the hive
✓ Where birds fly (in the evening)
  - Seed-eating birds such as turtle doves, pigeons and sparrows fly to drink in the early
    morning and evening; the flight of hawks and other birds of prey tells you nothing
✓ Low ground in valleys
  - Follow the land downhill; keep still and listen for running water
  - Dry stream beds: dig a few tens of cm down on the outside of a bend or below a big rock;
    water may seep in
✓ Follow animal tracks

Other ways to get water:
✓ Bamboo: old green bamboo sometimes holds water inside the nodes; you can hear it when
  you shake it; cut a hole just below the node to get the water
✓ Climbing plants: in rainforest some thick vines release clear water when cut;
  only drink it if it is colourless, tasteless and not sticky; do not drink milky,
  coloured, bitter or sticky liquid
✓ Rainwater: catch it with a tarpaulin or large leaves (see the next section)
```

### Water sources

**Order of preference**:
```
1. Flowing streams (upstream)
2. Springs
3. Rainwater
4. Dew
5. Transpiration water from plants
6. Ponds/lakes (must be purified)

Note: the ones near the top are only "relatively clean"; that does not mean you can
drink them as they are
```

```
When collecting water:
✓ Take water where it runs fast and clear, not from shallow water at the edge,
  water with foam on it or water with dead animals in it
✓ Look upstream first: are there villages, pastures, cattle or sheep droppings,
  mines or farmland (pesticides)?
✓ Let muddy water settle first, then filter it through cloth or clothing,
  then disinfect it
❌ Do not go to a stream for water when there are signs of a flash flood
```

For signs of a flash flood see [Wading, Flash Floods and Hypothermia in the Wild – Warning signs of a flash flood](08-river-crossing-flash-floods-and-hypothermia.md#signs-that-a-flash-flood-is-coming).

**Purification**:
```
⚠️ Purify all water from the wild!
- Boiling (best)
  * Bring to a rolling boil and keep it boiling for at least 1 minute;
    3 minutes above 2000 m altitude
- Filtering + boiling
  * Filter muddy water before boiling it; a home-made sand, gravel and charcoal filter
    only removes sediment and does not kill germs
- Chemical disinfection
  * Water purification tablets: use the dose on the leaflet and wait 30 minutes
    (longer if the water is cold or muddy)
  * Household bleach (containing sodium hypochlorite, unscented): 2 drops per litre of
    clear water, 4 drops for muddy water; shake well and wait 30 minutes

Why even clear forest streams must be purified:
- Wild animals and grazing cattle and sheep bring Giardia, Cryptosporidium and harmful
  bacteria into streams; "it looks clear" and "it's mountain spring water" are no guarantee
  of safety
- Diarrhoea in the wild causes dehydration and weakness, and can stop someone who could
  have walked out from walking out
```

For details of each purification method see [Basic Survival Skills – Water purification](00-basic-survival-skills.md#water-purification). For rehydration if diarrhoea starts see [Treating and Evacuating Casualties in the Wild – Diarrhoea and vomiting](11-wilderness-casualty-care-and-evacuation.md#diarrhoea-and-vomiting).

## Shelter

There is plenty of material in a forest, but the main problems are **damp ground, falling temperatures at night, and cold and wet after rain**. Even in summer a forest night can drop to a dozen or so °C; wet clothes plus wind are enough to cause hypothermia.

### Choosing a campsite in the forest

```
✓ Slightly raised, flat, well-drained ground: a gentle terrace on a slope or a small mound
✓ Look up: no dead trees overhead, and no broken branches hanging in mid-air
  ("widow-makers")
  - Dead trees, half-fallen trees and trees with roots washed out may fall at any time
    in strong wind or after rain
  - Camp farther from a dead tree than the tree is tall
✓ Out of the wind: use an earth bank, a big rock or dense scrub as a windbreak
✓ Close to a clearing or the forest edge where you can see the sky (easier to be found
  and to signal), but not in the middle of the clearing

❌ Avoid:
- Stream banks, river beaches, dry gullies, the mouths of ravines (flash floods)
- Low ground where water collects, wet ground with especially thick moss
  (damp, and cold air settles there at night)
- Game trails, near ant nests and wasp nests, under fruit trees (they attract animals)
- Below steep slopes (rockfall, landslides), under lone big trees (lightning)
```

**In thunderstorms**:
```
⚠️ There is no truly safe shelter from lightning in the open; start getting ready as
   soon as you hear thunder, and if you can get to a building or a vehicle, do so
✓ In a forest: move away from the tallest trees and lone big trees, into a stand of
  lower trees of about the same height, at least a few metres from any trunk
✓ Leave ridges, summits, the middle of clearings and the water's edge
✓ Tents and simple shelters give no protection from lightning; when the storm is
  overhead, move away from metal objects and crouch with your feet together on a
  sleeping mat or a dry backpack
✓ In a group, spread out, at least 15 m apart
❌ Do not shelter under big trees, under rock overhangs or in shallow cave mouths
```

For lightning in detail see [Weather Disasters – Lightning safety outdoors](../02-disaster-response/02-weather-disasters.md#lightning-safety-outdoors) and [Mountain Survival – Lightning](05-mountain-survival.md#lightning); for general principles of choosing a campsite see [Wading, Flash Floods and Hypothermia in the Wild – Choosing a campsite](08-river-crossing-flash-floods-and-hypothermia.md#choosing-a-campsite).

### Natural shelters

```
Use:
✓ Caves (check there are no animals)
  - Look at the entrance first: if there are droppings, bones, hair or a strong animal
    smell, do not go in
  - Do not go deep into narrow, wet caves or caves with running water
    (you may be trapped or drowned)
  - If a cave has a lot of rat droppings: sprinkle water to wet them before clearing them;
    do not sweep them dry
    (dry rat droppings stirred up and breathed in can spread haemorrhagic fever)
✓ Under a big tree (temporary)
  - Only for sheltering briefly from rain; do not shelter under big trees in a thunderstorm
✓ Beside a fallen trunk
  - Choose a log lying firmly on the ground, not on a slope, that cannot roll
  - Beat and poke around it with a stick first; snakes, wasps, ants, scorpions and
    centipedes often live under fallen logs
  - Do not shelter under a fallen trunk leaning on another tree or hanging in mid-air
    (it can suddenly slip)
✓ Rock overhangs
  - First look above for loose stones and signs of fresh rockfall
```

### Building a shelter

**A-frame shelter**:
```
1. Find a ridge pole (a long, thick stick)
   - About 1 m longer than your height, at least wrist-thick, strong and not rotten
2. Raise one end (forked branch)
   - Rest it in a tree fork or on two sticks lashed together in a cross, about 1 m off
     the ground (enough that your head does not touch when you sit up)
3. The other end on the ground
4. Lean sticks against both sides
   - About a fist's width apart, with the lower ends firmly planted
5. Lay branches and leaves thickly (like roof tiles)
   - From the bottom up, each layer overlapping the one below
6. The thicker the better (30 cm+)
   - The colder it is, the thicker; the test is that you cannot see daylight from inside
7. Line the inside with dry leaves
   - At least 10–15 cm thick after packing down
```

**Debris hut**:
```
- Frame: A-frame
- Thick covering of leaves
- Small entrance
- Keeps you warm well
- Inside, only a little bigger than your body (the more space, the harder it is to keep warm)
- Entrance away from the wind; it can be plugged with a pile of leaves or your backpack
```

**Keeping the rain out**:
```
✓ Lay like tiles (lower layer outside)
✓ Bark as the top layer
✓ Steep enough (so water runs off)
  - The roof should be at least about 45° to the ground; if it is too flat, rain seeps in
✓ Dig drainage ditches around it
  - Dig a shallow ditch on the uphill side to lead rainwater away to the sides
✓ If you have a tarpaulin, raincoat or large plastic bag, lay it over the layer of
  branches; it works far better than leaves alone
```

For other kinds of shelter see [Basic Survival Skills – Building shelters](00-basic-survival-skills.md#building-shelters).

### Keeping dry and warm

```
⚠️ The most overlooked thing when spending a night in a forest is "the ground":
   damp ground draws heat from the body much faster than air does

✓ Do not lie or sit directly on the ground: spread dry leaves, pine needles, ferns or
  branches at least 10 cm thick after packing down, or sit on your backpack or a sleeping mat
✓ Wring out wet clothes as well as you can and change them if you can; dry socks
  separately (wet feet blister and become waterlogged)
✓ Eat something and drink something hot before sleeping; if you are cold, move about
  (a few dozen squats) before lying down
✓ If there are several of you, huddle together; wrap yourself in an emergency foil blanket
✓ Shivering with cold at night, clumsy hands and feet and slurred speech are signs of
  hypothermia; deal with it at once
```

For recognising and treating hypothermia see [Wading, Flash Floods and Hypothermia in the Wild – Not only in winter: hypothermia in the wild](08-river-crossing-flash-floods-and-hypothermia.md#not-only-in-winter-hypothermia-outdoors).

## Fire

There is plenty of wood in a forest, but **forest fire is the biggest man-made disaster in a forest**. Before lighting a fire, think it through: do you really need it (warmth against hypothermia, boiling water, signalling), and is the area in its forest fire-prevention season?

### Choosing a site

```
✓ Flat ground
✓ Sheltered from the wind but with airflow
✓ Close to the shelter (but at a safe distance)
  - At least a few metres from the shelter, so sparks cannot drift onto a leaf roof
✓ Close to a wood supply
✓ Clear away anything that can burn around it
  - Clear a ring of bare earth about 3 m across; sweep away leaves, dry grass and pine needles
✓ Dig a pit or ring it with stones
  - Do not ring a fire with wet stones from a river (they may burst when heated)

❌ Do not light a fire:
- Under trees (the leaves and branches above will catch), on tree roots, beside fallen logs
- On thick leaf litter or peat (it can smoulder underground for a long time and break out
  as flames somewhere else hours later)
- On windy days or among dry grass
```

### Lighting a fire with wet wood

**Finding dry material**:
```
✓ Small twigs on dead trees (not yet fallen)
  - Twigs that snap cleanly with a "crack" are dry; ones that bend without breaking are
    wet or alive
✓ The dry layer under bark
✓ Pine resin (burns easily)
  - Resin that has run out of wounds in pine trees and hardened, or resin-rich pine
    heartwood ("fatwood"), will light even in rain
✓ Birch bark (waterproof and burns easily)
  - Strip it from fallen logs and dead trees; do not strip bark from living trees
    (it kills the tree)
✓ Split wet wood (dry inside)
  - Choose dead branches about wrist-thick; once split, the core is often dry
```

**Method**:
```
1. Prepare plenty of tinder
   - Prepare 2–3 times as much as you think you need; on wet days fires most often fail
     at this step
2. Split wet wood to expose the dry core
3. Carve a feather stick
   - On a stick of dry core wood, carve a ring of thin curls that stay attached;
     the thinner, the easier they catch
4. Start a small fire
   - Put a layer of bark or sticks underneath; do not build the fire straight on wet ground
5. Dry the wet wood
   - Stand the wood you will use around the fire to dry
6. Add fuel gradually
   - From thin to thick: matchstick → pencil → thumb → wrist thickness;
     only add the next size once the previous one is burning well
```

For fire-lighting methods and fire lays see [Basic Survival Skills – Fire-making techniques](00-basic-survival-skills.md#making-fire).

### Preventing forest fires

```
✓ Before going into the hills, check the local forest fire danger rating and
  fire-season rules
  - In China the main fire seasons are spring and autumn in the north, and winter and
    spring in the south; during the fire season all outdoor fires are generally banned in
    forest areas, offenders are punished, and causing a forest fire carries criminal liability
  - Even when your life is genuinely at risk (lost overnight, hypothermia) and you use
    fire for warmth and signalling, take extra care
✓ Someone watches the fire at all times, with water or sand and soil to hand
✓ Keep the fire small: just enough for warmth and boiling water
✓ Put it out completely before leaving or sleeping:
  1. Pour water on it
  2. Stir the ashes with a stick to turn up embers underneath
  3. Pour on more water until there is no smoke and no hissing
  4. Hold the back of your hand close to the ashes; it is only fully out when you
     feel no heat
  With no water, cover with damp soil and stir, again and again
```

### If you meet a forest fire

```
✓ If you see a forest fire: call the forest fire line 12119 or 119; say where you are,
  which way the fire is moving and which way the wind is blowing
✓ Leave at once: go upwind, downhill, towards rivers, open ground or ground that has
  already burnt
❌ Do not run uphill, do not run downwind, do not go into narrow valleys or ravine mouths
❌ Do not stop to take photos or watch, or go back for belongings
```

For escaping and saving yourself when surrounded by fire see [Fire – Forest fires](../02-disaster-response/04-fire.md#forest-fires).

## Food

A person can live for weeks without food; **during the few days you wait for rescue, food matters far less than water and warmth**. Searching the forest for food often costs more energy, and carries more risk (poisoning, injury, getting more lost), than the energy you get from it. If stranded for a short time, ration your emergency food and do not risk foraging.

Only if you cannot be rescued for a long time do you need to get food from the forest, and the rule is: **do not eat what you do not recognise; do not eat what you are not sure of.**

### Edible plants

**Common edible plants** (once identified):
```
✓ Acorns (leach out the bitterness, cook them)
  - Full of tannin; raw they are bitter and upset the stomach
  - Shell and crush them, then soak in clean water or boil repeatedly, changing the water
    each time, until they are no longer bitter (it may take several days)
✓ Dandelion (whole plant)
  - The older the leaves, the more bitter; blanch young leaves before eating;
    the roots can be boiled
✓ Plantain (Plantago)
  - Blanch young leaves before eating
✓ Wild garlic and wild onion
  - Always crush and smell: it is only the real thing if it smells clearly of onion or garlic;
    an "onion" or "garlic" without that smell may be a poisonous plant such as spider lily
    (Lycoris) or daffodil
✓ Certain wild fruits (once identified)
  - Only eat ones you have known since childhood and that are commonly eaten locally
    (such as wild strawberries, wild kiwifruit, chestnuts, pine nuts)
✓ Bracken fiddleheads
  - Only eat the young, still-curled shoots; they must be blanched and soaked, then cooked
    thoroughly; do not eat a lot over a long time (they contain a carcinogen)
✓ Bamboo shoots
  - Raw shoots contain substances that can produce cyanide; they must be peeled, cut open
    and cooked right through, and the cooking water thrown away
✓ Wood ear fungus, mushrooms (⚠️ expert identification!)
  - Cook fresh wood ear thoroughly before eating; soak dried wood ear only just before
    using it, not for a long time or overnight (it may produce bongkrekic acid, which
    cooking does not destroy and which has no specific antidote); never eat any wild
    mushroom you do not recognise
```

**Avoid**:
```
❌ White berries (mostly poisonous)
❌ White and yellow kinds of aggregate berries
❌ Milky sap (mostly poisonous, except identifiable species such as dandelion)
❌ A bitter-almond smell
  - A bitter-almond smell means cyanide
❌ Umbrella-shaped flower heads (many poisonous)
  - The carrot family includes deadly poisonous plants such as water hemlock and poison
    hemlock, which look like wild carrot, water dropwort (Chinese water celery) and wild celery
❌ Leaves in threes (many poisonous)
❌ Anything that makes your mouth or tongue go numb or burn, or is extremely bitter when
   chewed: spit it out at once and rinse your mouth

⚠️ Mushrooms: if not certain, never eat them! (they can kill; see Poisonous Mushrooms
   and Wild Plant Poisoning)
```

Related chapter: [Poisonous Mushrooms and Wild Plant Poisoning](09-poisonous-mushrooms-and-wild-plants.md).

```
⚠️ About the "edibility test":
- Some sources describe a step-by-step "universal edibility test" of tasting
- It cannot detect toxins that act hours or days later, it is completely useless for
  mushrooms, and it is not safe for deadly poisonous plants
- Use it only as a last resort when you cannot be rescued for a long time and are truly
  about to collapse from hunger; do not use it when stranded for a short time
```

For why folk methods of telling mushrooms apart are unreliable, and poisonous plants that are easily mistaken for edible ones, see [Poisonous Mushrooms and Wild Plant Poisoning](09-poisonous-mushrooms-and-wild-plants.md#poisonous-mushrooms-there-is-no-simple-test); for the limits of the edibility test see [Poisonous Mushrooms and Wild Plant Poisoning – About the "edibility test"](09-poisonous-mushrooms-and-wild-plants.md#about-the-edibility-test).

### Animal protein

**Insects**:
```
Edible (cooked):
✓ Grasshoppers, crickets
  - Remove the wings and hind legs (the barbs on the legs can stick in the throat)
✓ Termites
✓ Beetle larvae (in rotting wood)
  - Split rotting wood or peel back bark and you will often find white grubs
✓ Earthworms (squeeze out the contents, rinse)
  - You can first leave them in clean water for a while to purge the soil;
    they must be cooked
✓ Ants (remove the heads)

Avoid:
❌ Bright colours
❌ Hairy ones
❌ Strong smells
❌ Spiders, ticks, centipedes, scorpions, flies, mosquitoes
❌ Insects that have been dead for a while, or that you picked off dung or carrion

Note:
- Always boil or roast before eating (insects and earthworms may carry parasites)
- People allergic to prawns and crabs may react to insects; try a small amount first
```

**Small animals**:
```
Trapping:
- Simple snare
- Pit trap
- Deadfall

⚠️ Needs skill and time
- You may use more energy than you gain
- Insects and plants come first
- Traps do not choose their victims: they may catch protected animals, someone's dog,
  or even a passer-by (including children)
- Wild animals you catch may carry parasites and pathogens; wear gloves or wrap your hands
  when handling them, and cook the meat thoroughly
- Do not eat animals that died of illness or of unknown causes
```

**Fishing**:
```
In streams:
✓ Make hooks (bent needles, bone)
  - Bending a safety pin or sewing needle is simplest
✓ Fishing line (inner strands of paracord)
✓ Bait (worms)
  - Earthworms, beetle larvae; fish mostly hide in pools, under tree roots along the bank,
    and on the downstream side of big rocks
✓ Or by hand (under stones)
  - Beware of slippery stones; do not go into fast or deep water to catch fish
✓ Fish spear

⚠️ Freshwater fish must be cooked thoroughly (eating it raw or half-raw can give you
   liver flukes and other parasites)
```

### The law on hunting and fishing

```
⚠️ In normal times in China:
- The Wildlife Protection Law forbids hunting or killing wild animals under national or
  local key protection; other wild animals may only be hunted with a hunting licence,
  at set times and places
- Snares, leg-hold traps, ground guns, poison, explosives, electric shock, bird nets and
  similar tools are banned
- Eating terrestrial wild animals is banned (except farmed poultry, livestock and the like)
- The Fisheries Law bans electrofishing, poisoning and dynamiting fish; fishing is not
  allowed in closed seasons and closed areas (including the long-term fishing ban in key
  waters of the Yangtze basin)
- Offenders are fined, and serious cases are crimes

This section is only for understanding the basics of wilderness survival, or for extreme
situations such as civilisation collapse or being unable to be rescued for a long time.
Being lost for a few days waiting for rescue does not call for hunting.
```

For general methods of collecting insects and fishing see [Basic Survival Skills – Getting food](00-basic-survival-skills.md#getting-food); for raising animals and fisheries in long-term survival see [Aquaculture](../04-farming-and-animal-husbandry/07-aquaculture.md).

## Dangers

### Animals

**Large animals**:
```
If you meet a bear:
- Stay calm
- Back away slowly
- Do not run
- Do not stare at it
- Make noise to warn it
- Make yourself look big (raise your arms)
- If it attacks:
  * Defensive attack by a brown bear (protecting cubs, startled at close range):
    lie flat and play dead, hands protecting the back of your neck
  * Attack by a black bear, or any bear that keeps attacking or treats you as prey
    (following you, breaking into a tent at night): fight back with everything you have
  * If you have bear spray, use it first

If you meet a wild boar:
- Do not approach
- Climb a tree
- Keep your back to a big tree

If you meet a snake:
- Keep away
- Do not disturb it
- Check sleeping bags and shoes
- Probe the way with a stick
```

**Bears: prevention matters more than response**:
```
The bears in China are mainly the Asiatic black bear (commonly called "dog bear" or
"black blind one", in forests of the north-east, south-west, Qinling and elsewhere)
and the brown bear (north-east, north-west, Qinghai–Tibet Plateau). Most bears avoid
people when they meet them; injuries mostly happen in sudden close encounters,
when a mother is protecting cubs, or when a bear is defending food.

✓ When walking in forests where there are bears, talk, clap or hang a bell so bears
  know in good time that someone is coming
✓ Walk in a group, not alone; do not push into dense scrub at dusk or dawn
✓ If you see fresh bear droppings or footprints, stumps and ant nests torn open,
  or bees' nests clawed open, change route and leave
✓ Keep food, rubbish, toothpaste and other things with a smell out of the tent:
  - Cook and store food about 100 m from where you sleep, preferably downwind
  - If you can, hang the food bag from a branch about 4 m off the ground and 1–2 m
    from the trunk
❌ Do not feed, approach or photograph bear cubs (the mother is usually close by)

More on meeting a bear:
- A bear standing up is usually looking and smelling, not necessarily about to attack
- A bear charging at you is often a "bluff charge" and stops a few metres away;
  do not run, stand your ground
- Spray bear spray at the bear's face once it comes within about 10 m
- Climbing a tree will not get you away from a black bear (black bears climb trees)
- After being clawed or bitten by a bear you must get medical care quickly: the wounds are
  deep and heavily contaminated and need cleaning out, a tetanus jab and rabies vaccine
```

For preventing and dealing with wild boar, dogs, monkeys and snakes see [Wasps, Ticks and Dangerous Animals](10-wasps-ticks-and-dangerous-animals.md); for treating snakebite see [Wound and Injury Care – Snakebites](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/02-wounds-and-injuries.md#snakebites); for animal bites and preventing rabies see [Wound and Injury Care – Bites](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/02-wounds-and-injuries.md#bites).

**Insects**:
```
✓ Check your body (ticks)
  - Check your whole body at least once a day, especially the scalp, behind the ears,
    armpits, waist, groin and backs of the knees
  - If you find an attached tick, grip it close to the skin with fine tweezers and pull
    it straight out slowly; do not burn it or smear oil on it
✓ Smoke to keep off mosquitoes
✓ Smear mud on the skin (against insect bites)
  - Only when you have no repellent; do not put it on wounds
✓ Check clothes and sleeping bags
✓ Wasps: one of the animals that most often injure people in forests. If you see a nest
  or hear the buzzing get louder, go round; if a swarm attacks, protect your head and neck
  and run fast away from the nest
  ⚠️ If stung more than 10 times, or if there is difficulty breathing, a rash all over or
     a swollen face, call for help and get medical care at once
✓ If you have insect repellent (containing DEET), apply it to bare skin as the leaflet says;
  tuck your trouser legs into your socks
```

For preventing and treating wasp stings, ticks and land leeches see [Wasps, Ticks and Dangerous Animals](10-wasps-ticks-and-dangerous-animals.md).

### Plants

**Poisonous plants**:
```
❌ Poison ivy, poison oak (leaves in threes, allergic reaction on contact)
  - Found mainly in North America; in Chinese forests the lacquer tree and wild lacquer
    tree (Toxicodendron) are more common: touching or even going near them causes red,
    swollen skin, blisters and intense itching ("lacquer rash")
❌ Water hemlock (deadly)
  - Grows in wet ground by water and looks like water dropwort or wild celery;
    the cut root oozes yellow juice
❌ Oleander (the whole plant is poisonous)
  - Do not use oleander twigs as skewers or chopsticks, and do not burn it to cook food
❌ Gelsemium (Gelsemium elegans, "heartbreak grass"): a climber common in southern
   mountain forests, with small yellow flowers; a small amount can kill
❌ Nettles: the stinging hairs on leaves and stems make the skin sting and come up in
   a rash, which usually fades after a few hours

Principles:
- If you do not know it, do not eat it
- If you are not sure, do not touch it
```

```
After touching a poisonous plant:
✓ Wash the skin with soap and plenty of water as soon as possible
  (ideally within a few tens of minutes of contact)
✓ Also wash clothes, gloves and tools that touched it (the sap can stay on them and
  keep causing reactions)
✓ Do not scratch; if it itches badly, use a cold compress
⚠️ Blisters on the face or eyes or over a large area, or coughing and wheezing after
   breathing smoke from burning poisonous plants: see a doctor
⚠️ If vomiting, a numb mouth, seizures or palpitations follow eating an unknown plant,
   call for help at once and keep the leftover plant to show the doctor
```

For poisonous plants that are easily mistaken for edible ones and what to do after eating one see [Poisonous Mushrooms and Wild Plant Poisoning – Poisonous plants easily mistaken for edible ones](09-poisonous-mushrooms-and-wild-plants.md#poisonous-plants-that-are-easily-mistaken); for a point-by-point comparison of Gelsemium, water hemlock and others with useful plants see [Comparison of easily confused poisonous plants](../../../docs/10-中药材识别与采集/09-有毒中药与易混淆有毒植物.md#四易混淆的有毒植物对照); for general treatment of poisoning see [Poisoning](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/12-poisoning.md).

### Illness

**Prevention**:
```
✓ Purify water
  - Prevents diarrhoeal illnesses (bacillary dysentery, Giardia and others)
✓ Cook food thoroughly
✓ Personal hygiene
  - Wash hands before eating and after using the toilet (hand sanitiser if there is no water);
    bury faeces in a dug hole at least 30 m from water
✓ Avoid standing water (malaria)
  - Do not camp beside standing water; use a mosquito net and repellent;
    wear long sleeves at dusk and at night
✓ Keep wounds clean
  - Forests are warm and humid, so even small wounds get infected easily;
    rinse them with clean water and dress them promptly
```

```
Some illnesses to watch for in forests:
- Tick-borne diseases: tick-borne encephalitis (forests of the north-east and north-west),
  severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), Lyme disease;
  if you get a fever within about a month of a tick bite, see a doctor and tell them
- Malaria: mainly in tropical regions abroad and some border areas of China; before going
  to these places, ask a doctor whether you need preventive medicine
- Scrub typhus: caused by bites of chigger mites in grass and scrub in the south;
  a black scab (eschar) at the bite site, with fever
- Leptospirosis: from contact with water and mud contaminated by the urine of rats or
  livestock (in the rainy season, after floods); if you have cuts on your skin, do not
  wade or bathe in such water
- Haemorrhagic fever: from breathing dust from rodent droppings; do not sweep rat
  droppings dry, and do not sleep beside rat holes

⚠️ If fever, headache, a rash or weakness appear within a few weeks of returning from
   a forest area, tell the doctor where you have been
```

For family prevention and control of infectious diseases see [Infectious Disease Control at Home](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/15-infectious-disease-at-home.md); for ticks in detail see [Wasps, Ticks and Dangerous Animals – Ticks](10-wasps-ticks-and-dangerous-animals.md#ticks).

## Navigation

### Following a river downstream (with caution)

```
Advantages:
✓ Rivers eventually lead to people
✓ A guaranteed water source
✓ Food (fish)

⚠️ Risks:
- A stream valley may turn into a gorge, waterfall or cliff, leaving you unable to go
  forward or back
- Riverbanks are slippery; it is easy to fall in
- Flash floods after rain

What to do:
- Only follow a river where the valley is open and both banks can be walked
- If you meet a gorge or waterfall, do not force your way through; go back and
  take the ridge instead
- Do not camp by the river
```

| | Suitable for following the river | Do not follow the river |
|---|---|---|
| Terrain | Gentle hills, lowland forest; wide valley with a path or walkable banks | Mountain stream valleys with steep slopes or cliffs on both sides |
| River | Larger rivers, often with villages, roads and fishermen along the banks | Small streams, which often turn into gorges and drops further down |
| Weather | Fine weather, steady water level | Rain, rain upstream, water turning muddy |
| Body | Able to walk steadily | Tired, injured, nearly dark |

```
When following a river:
✓ Walk on the bank above the riverbed, not in the channel or on the rocks
✓ If you see the valley ahead narrowing, the sides getting steeper, or hear the roar of
  a waterfall, get out early; do not wait until you are in it before turning back
✓ If you would have to cross the river, avoid it if you can
✓ At signs of a flash flood (water turning muddy, rising, a roar upstream),
  go at once to high ground on either side
```

For wading and flash floods see [Wading, Flash Floods and Hypothermia in the Wild](08-river-crossing-flash-floods-and-hypothermia.md).

### Climb high to look around

```
✓ Climb to high ground/up a tree
  - When climbing, choose places with a path and a gentle slope; do not climb wet,
    slippery rock faces
  - Only climb a tree as high as you are confident of, and do not step on dead branches;
    the cost of falling and getting hurt is much greater than being lost
✓ Look for:
  - Smoke (people)
  - Lights (at night)
  - Roads, buildings
  - Open ground
  - Which way rivers flow
  - Power lines, pylons, terraced fields, tea gardens and plantations (trees in neat rows)
    all mean people are nearby
✓ Use your ears too: when it is quiet, listen for cars, dogs barking, cocks crowing,
  logging and machinery to judge direction
✓ Once you see a target, note its direction with a compass or the sun and pick
  intermediate targets along the way before you set off
  (once you are down in the forest you will not see the target)
```

### Leaving marks

```
✓ Cloth strips (brightly coloured)
  - Hang them at eye level where they can be seen from both directions
✓ Blazes cut on tree trunks
  - Only in emergencies; normally it harms trees, and it is banned in nature reserves
    and scenic areas
✓ Stone piles
  - Stack three or more stones; such piles rarely occur in nature, so anyone can tell
    they are man-made
✓ Broken branches pointing the way
  - Break the branch but leave it hanging, with the break facing the direction of travel;
    a fresh break is pale and easy to spot

Purpose:
- So search teams can find you
- So you do not retrace your steps

How:
✓ Make one every so often; from each mark you should be able to see the last one
✓ When leaving your original spot, lay a large arrow of stones or branches pointing
  the way you went; if you can write, leave the date, time, number of people and
  where you are going
✓ Always leave a mark where you change direction
✓ Use one kind of mark; do not switch between cloth strips and stone piles,
  or search teams may get confused
✓ After you are rescued or walk out, collect any cloth strips you can find
```

## Tropical rainforest and temperate forest

China's tropical rainforests are mainly in southern Yunnan (Xishuangbanna and elsewhere), Hainan and south-east Tibet; most people go to the subtropical evergreen forests of the south, and the temperate deciduous and coniferous forests of the north and north-east. The main dangers differ between these forests.

| | Tropical rainforest, hot humid southern mountain forest | Temperate forest (north, north-east) |
|---|---|---|
| Climate | Hot and humid all year, rain almost every day; clothes are hard to dry | Distinct seasons, bitterly cold winters; big day–night temperature differences in spring and autumn |
| Undergrowth | Dim, with dense vines and shrubs; you can see only a few metres and may cover only a few km a day | More open under the trees, better visibility; terrain is easier to read when the leaves are down |
| Water | Plentiful, but almost all contaminated; must be purified | Many streams; frozen in winter, so ice must be melted |
| Main dangers | Land leeches, mosquitoes and insect-borne diseases such as malaria, venomous snakes, wasps, heatstroke in humid heat, skin ulcers and fungal infections, flash floods in the rainy season | Hypothermia, frostbite, ticks (tick-borne encephalitis), bears, forest fires |
| Overnight | Avoid sleeping on the ground if you can: use a hammock or build a raised bed of branches; always have a rain layer above | Insulation from the ground matters most; lay thick leaf litter and pine needles |
| Fire | The wood is soaked and fire is hard to light; look for bamboo, resin, and dead branches not yet fallen | High forest-fire risk in dry spring and autumn seasons; fires banned during the fire season |

```
In hot, humid mountain forest:
✓ You can get hypothermia in a rainforest too: after a night of rain in wet clothes you
  may feel cold and shiver even at twenty-something °C
✓ Dry your feet every evening, let them air, and change into dry socks;
  feet left in wet shoes for long become waterlogged and ulcerated
✓ Do not grab vines or trunks for support without looking (they may have thorns, ants,
  venomous insects or snakes)
✓ Wear long sleeves and long trousers, with the trouser legs tied tight against land
  leeches and ticks
✓ You sweat a lot: replace both water and salt

In temperate and cold-temperate forest:
✓ In autumn and winter it gets dark early and nights are cold; when lost, the biggest
  threat is hypothermia
```

For moving and keeping warm in snow see [Snow and Polar Survival](04-snow-and-polar-survival.md); for treating immersion foot see [Wound and Injury Care – Chilblains and immersion foot (not frostbite)](../01-first-aid-and-family-health/02-wounds-and-injuries.md#chilblains-and-immersion-foot-not-frostbite); for heatstroke see [Treating and Evacuating Casualties in the Wild – Altitude sickness, heatstroke, hypothermia](11-wilderness-casualty-care-and-evacuation.md#altitude-sickness-heatstroke-hypothermia).

## 📝 Forest survival: key points

```
Priorities:
1. Water (streams, rain, plants)
2. Shelter (from rain, for warmth)
3. Fire (keeping animals away, warmth, signalling)
4. Food (insects, plants, fish)
5. Navigation/signals

Techniques:
✓ Stay put when people are searching for you
✓ Leave marks
✓ When getting yourself out, climb high to look for a way first, and follow rivers with caution
✓ Use natural shelters
✓ Plenty of wood (fire)
✓ Stop and prepare for the night before dark; do not push on in the dark
✓ Find direction by compass, phone, sun and stars, not by moss and growth rings
✓ However clear the stream, boil the water

Dangers:
⚠️ Getting lost
⚠️ Wild animals
⚠️ Poisonous animals and plants
⚠️ Hypothermia (at night)
⚠️ Falling dead trees, lightning, flash floods, forest fires
```

```
Note: the priorities above apply in general. When the night is cold, it is raining and
your clothes are wet, keeping warm (shelter and fire) is more urgent than finding water;
when stranded for a short time, food can come last.
```
