# Snow and Polar Survival Guide

## Core challenges

```
- Extreme cold: the body constantly loses heat to the air, the ground and the snow, and only food eaten can replace it
- Risk of hypothermia: fastest when soaked by sweat or meltwater, in strong wind, or when tired and hungry
- Frostbite: fingers, toes, ears, the tip of the nose and the cheeks are hit first
- Blizzards: visibility drops to a few m; you may be unable to move for hours to days
- Easy to get lost in the white: snow covers paths and landmarks; in a "whiteout" you cannot even see the rise and fall of the ground
- Hard to get food and water: snow is everywhere but water is scarce (melting snow takes fuel), and the cold multiplies the energy you burn
- Avalanches, breaking ice: hidden terrain hazards where the first few minutes decide life or death
```

```
The most important principles:
1. Do not go into the mountains when there is a blizzard warning or high avalanche risk; in winter it is even more important than in summer to leave a trip plan
2. If you are stranded, call for help and wait where you are for rescue first, rather than risk walking out yourself
3. Stop and make shelter at least 1-2 hours before dark — winter days are short, and after dark everything is hard
```

For preparation before setting off, trip plans, getting lost and calling for help see [Trip Preparation and Calling for Help](../../../docs/03-野外生存/07-户外出行准备与求救.md); for blizzards and cold waves in towns and the countryside see [Blizzards](../../../docs/02-灾难应对/02-气象灾害.md#暴风雪).

## Keeping warm (the most critical!)

The body loses heat in four ways: **conduction** (sitting on snow, wearing wet clothes), **convection** (wind), **radiation** (bare head, neck and hands) and **evaporation** (sweating, breathing). Keeping warm means blocking these four routes one by one:

| Route of heat loss | Typical situation | How to block it |
|------|------|------|
| Conduction | Sitting or lying directly on snow or cold rock; wet clothes against the skin | Put a rucksack, sleeping mat or branches underneath you; keep clothes dry |
| Convection | Strong wind blowing through clothes | Windproof outer layer; get to the sheltered side; snow wall to block the wind |
| Radiation | No hat or gloves, neck uncovered | Wear a hat, neck gaiter and gloves |
| Evaporation | Sweating while walking, water vapour lost in breath | Sweat less; cover mouth and nose with a scarf to warm the air you breathe in |

### Clothing system

**Layering**:
```
1. Base layer (wicking):
   - Synthetic fibre or wool
   - No cotton! (causes hypothermia when wet)
   - Close-fitting but not tight; its job is to move sweat away from the skin and keep the skin dry

2. Mid layer (insulation):
   - Wool, fleece
   - Several thin layers beat one thick one
   - Down is the warmest and lightest, but gives almost no warmth when wet; in damp weather or when you sweat easily, use fleece or synthetic insulation

3. Outer layer (windproof and waterproof):
   - Waterproof jacket
   - Windproof
   - Ideally breathable, with underarm zips to let heat out

Principles:
✓ Adjustable (take off when hot, put on when cold)
✓ Stay dry! (wet = deadly)
```

Why no cotton: when cotton gets wet its fibres collapse and the air in it is replaced by water. Water conducts heat about 20 times faster than air, so wet cotton against the body is like wearing a layer of cold water, and it is very slow to dry. Wet wool still keeps some warmth, and synthetics dry fastest.

**The COLD principle** (a mnemonic used for winter outdoor activities abroad, easy to remember):
```
C - Clean:              clothes soiled with grease and sweat insulate less
O - avoid Overheating:  sweating is soaking yourself; take a layer off as soon as you are warm
L - Loose, Layered:     tight clothing squashes insulation and restricts blood flow
D - Dry:                guard against wet from inside (sweat) and outside (snowmelt, rain)
```

**Substitutes when you have no proper gear**:
```
- Stuff newspaper, dry grass or dry leaves between two layers of clothing (to trap air); replace when wet
- Plastic bags and bin bags can be a windproof layer: put them over the insulating layer;
  they can also be "vapour-barrier socks": thin sock → plastic bag → thick sock, so the thick sock does not get soaked with foot sweat
  (the plastic bag does not breathe, so the thin sock inside gets wet; dry it every day)
- Cut a head hole in a blanket or quilt and wear it as a poncho
- Several layers of old clothes are easier to adjust than one thick padded coat
```

**Key areas**:
```
✓ Head (noticeable heat loss when bare):
  - Hat, head cloth
  - Cover the ears; wear a hat to sleep as well

✓ Hands and feet:
  - Gloves (mittens are warmer)
  - Thick socks
  - Boots (waterproof and warm)
  - Leave room in shoes to move your toes; with two pairs of thick socks, shoes need to be half to one size larger
    (feet squeezed into shoes get frostbite most easily)
  - Carry a spare pair of dry socks and gloves in a waterproof bag, close to your body
  - Tie gloves together with cord or hang them from your wrists; in strong wind, take them off for a moment and they can blow away

✓ Face:
  - Scarf, face mask
  - Prevents frostbite
  - Companions check each other's faces every so often; if you see white patches on the nose or cheeks, deal with them at once

✓ Eyes:
  - Protection from snow blindness
  - Sunglasses/goggles
```

**Children, older people and the frail**:
```
- Children have a relatively large body surface, lose heat fast, and do not tell you they are cold;
  a child carried on someone's back or sitting on a sledge is not moving and gets much colder than an adult who is walking; give them one more layer and check their hands, feet and the back of the neck often
- Carry a baby close against you in a sling inside the adult's coat; this is warmest; keep the mouth and nose uncovered
- Older people and people with diabetes have reduced sensation and may not feel pain from frostbite; check their hands and feet yourself
- People who are tired, hungry or have been drinking lose heat fast and cannot produce much heat by shivering; they show hypothermia first
```

### Wind chill: wind doubles the cold

Wind keeps blowing away the layer of warm air at the skin surface, so you feel far colder than the thermometer reads:

| Air temperature | Wind about 10 km/h | Wind about 30 km/h | Wind about 50 km/h |
|------|------|------|------|
| 0°C | Feels like about -3°C | Feels like about -6°C | Feels like about -8°C |
| -10°C | Feels like about -15°C | Feels like about -20°C | Feels like about -22°C |
| -20°C | Feels like about -27°C | Feels like about -33°C | Feels like about -35°C |
| -30°C | Feels like about -39°C | Feels like about -46°C | Feels like about -49°C |

```
- Feels like -28°C to -39°C: exposed skin may get frostbite within 10-30 minutes
- Feels like -40°C to -47°C: frostbite possible within 5-10 minutes; below -48°C: 2-5 minutes, faster in strong wind
- What to do: keep skin covered as much as possible; get to shelter from the wind; in strong wind it is better to stop and wait
- Estimating wind speed: branches swaying and snow being lifted about 20-30 km/h; struggling to walk into the wind about 50 km/h or more
```

### Staying dry

```
⚠️ Wet + cold = a deadly combination!

✓ Take off wet clothes
✓ Wring them out
✓ Dry them with body heat
✓ Do not sweat heavily (take layers off in time)
✓ Brush off snow (do not let it melt)
✓ Waterproof layer
```

**Managing sweat** (the most overlooked point in winter):
```
- Take one layer off before setting off: feeling "a bit cold" at the start is just right; you will be warm after 10 minutes of walking
- Before going uphill, breaking trail or digging a snow cave, take layers off and open zips in advance, and slow down;
  "better a bit slower than drenched in sweat"; when your back starts to feel damp, it is time to take a layer off
- As soon as you stop (to rest, read the map, wait for someone), put the insulating layer straight back on; do not wait until you are cold
- For heavy work such as digging a snow cave or chopping wood, wear only the base layer + windproof layer, and keep the insulating layer dry in your rucksack; put it on when you finish
```

**Drying clothes**:
```
- Small items (socks, glove liners) can go in your sleeping bag or an inside pocket while you sleep, dried by body heat
- If you have a fire, hang clothes beside it, but not too close, and keep watch: synthetics melt in flame, and scorched down and shoes are common
- In hard frost, wet clothes can first be hung outside to freeze stiff, then the ice knocked off, removing some of the water
- Brush all the snow off yourself before going into a tent or snow cave (snow melts once inside and makes everything wet)
```

**How to tell it is becoming dangerous**: constant shivering, clumsy fingers (unable to work a zip or strike a match), slurred speech, stumbling — this is early hypothermia. Stop at once, add clothes, eat something and find shelter from the wind; see [Hypothermia](#hypothermia) below.

## Shelter

```
Principles of winter shelter:
✓ The smaller the warmer — make only just enough room to lie down, so your body heat can warm it
✓ Block the wind first — wind is the biggest thief of heat
✓ Insulate from the ground — see "Sleeping" below
✓ Ventilate — snow-cave walls do not let air through; see "Air in the shelter" below
✓ Finish before dark — digging a snow cave takes 2-3 hours; always start early
✓ Look up before digging: do not build below a slope that could avalanche, under a cornice, or under trees heavy with snow
✓ Take a shovel (snow shovel, pot, flat board) into the shelter, so you can dig yourself out if it collapses
```

If you are stranded in a vehicle, the vehicle itself is the best shelter; see [Blizzards – Trapped in a vehicle](../../../docs/02-灾难应对/02-气象灾害.md#暴风雪中).

### Snow cave

**Best option** (warm, sheltered from wind):

```
Steps:
1. Find a deep snowdrift or a slope with deep snow
   - The snow should be at least about 2 m deep; a drift on the downwind slope is best
   - Push a stick or probe in to test the depth, and make sure there is no rock or hollow underneath
2. Dig the entrance (small, to keep heat in)
   - Just big enough to crawl through, about 60 cm wide
3. Dig upwards:
   - Entrance lower than the sleeping platform
   - Cold air sinks, warm air rises
4. Inside:
   - Dome-shaped (structurally stable)
   - Sleeping platform higher than the entrance
   - Enough room (but the smaller the warmer)
   - Roof at least 30 cm thick; smooth the inside of the roof so meltwater runs down the curve instead of dripping on you
   - Make a "cold trench" in the cave lower than the platform, where cold air can collect
5. Ventilation hole! (poke a hole, against CO2)
   - Push a hole about the thickness of a fist at an angle through the roof above the platform
   - Leave a stick in the hole and twist it every hour or two so it does not get blocked by snow or ice
6. Mark the entrance (against burial)
   - Stick branches, ski poles or something brightly coloured in the snow outside the entrance, so it can be found even in heavy snow
7. Lay insulation between you and the snow

Note:
✓ It must be ventilated!
✓ The entrance can be partly blocked with snow blocks or a rucksack
✓ The inside can reach 0°C (below freezing outside, so relatively warm!)
✓ When digging, wear only the base layer + windproof layer; do not get your clothes wet; dig slowly
✓ Two people take turns: one digs inside, one clears the snow outside and keeps watch
```

### Air in the shelter: suffocation and carbon monoxide

Snow caves, quinzhees and tents hold two invisible dangers:

```
1. Lack of oxygen, build-up of carbon dioxide
   - The inside wall of a snow cave is melted by body heat and stoves and refreezes into an ice crust, and then the snow no longer lets air through
   - In heavy snowfall outside, the ventilation hole and entrance can be buried
   → Keep the ventilation hole clear at all times; never seal the entrance completely; if it snows at night, get up and check once

2. Carbon monoxide (colourless, odourless, deadly)
   - Produced by stoves, candles or charcoal burning where ventilation is poor
   - Snow caves and tents are small; dangerous levels can build up within minutes to tens of minutes
   → Cook outside or at the entrance where possible; if you must use a stove inside, open the entrance and ventilation hole, and put it out as soon as you finish
   → Never burn charcoal for warmth in a closed snow cave or tent, and never sleep with a stove lit

Danger signs: headache, dizziness, nausea, drowsiness, several people affected at once, or a candle flame getting smaller and dimmer
→ Get everyone out into fresh air at once; treat anyone who is not fully conscious as carbon monoxide poisoning
```

For recognising and treating carbon monoxide poisoning see [Carbon monoxide poisoning](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/07-居家与交通意外.md#一氧化碳中毒).

### Snow wall

```
Windbreak:
- Pile up a snow wall
- In a semicircle or U shape
- Opening on the downwind side
- Combine with a fire

How to build it:
- Use a shovel, pot or board to cut snow blocks from packed snow (about 50 cm long, 30 cm wide, 20 cm thick)
- Wall about 1-1.5 m high; enough to shelter a person sitting or lying down
- Lay the blocks with staggered joints and pack the gaps with snow
- Rig a tarp, branches or a tent inside the wall for much more warmth
- Put the fire at the opening; the wall reflects heat back; a fire too close to the wall melts it
```

### Snow trench

```
Emergency:
- Dig a snow trench
- Line it with pine branches, grass
- Cover with branches, snow blocks
- Simple and quick

How to build it:
- Trench a little longer than your body, about 1 m wide and about 1 m deep, at right angles to the wind
- Line the bottom with at least 20-30 cm of pine branches, dry grass or rucksacks
- Lay branches, snow slabs or skis across the top, then cover with a tarp or snow blocks, leaving just a small entrance
- It can be finished in about 1 hour; suitable when it is nearly dark and you have no strength left to dig a snow cave
- Where the snow is shallow, use the hollow under a fallen tree or beside a big rock, or pile the snow up first and then dig
```

### Quinzhee snow shelter

```
Method:
1. Pile a large snow mound
   - About 3 m across and about 2 m high (for two people)
   - Mix snow from different layers as you pile it; it sets harder faster
2. Wait 1-2 hours (for the snow to set)
   - Powdery dry snow needs longer; in very low temperatures wait about 3 hours
3. Dig it out from one side
4. Dig upwards to make a sleeping platform
5. Walls 20-30 cm thick
   - Before digging, push a ring of sticks about 30 cm long into the mound from outside;
     when digging from inside, stop as soon as you reach the ends of the sticks, so the walls are even
6. Ventilation hole!
   - Same requirements as a snow cave: keep it clear, never burn charcoal inside

Suitable for: places where the snow is not deep enough for a snow cave but there is still enough to pile up
```

### Sleeping: insulation underneath matters more than covers on top

When you lie on snow, the insulation of your sleeping bag is squashed flat on the side your body presses down, and most heat is lost downwards. Many people under thick covers still wake up cold, and the reason is underneath them.

```
Insulating from the ground:
✓ Sleeping mats (foam mat, inflatable mat); in winter ideally two stacked together
✓ With no mat: pine branches, dry grass or leaves at least 20-30 cm thick (still about 10 cm once compressed)
✓ Rucksacks, rope or an empty rucksack can also go under your feet
✓ Put something underneath when sitting to rest too; never sit directly on snow

Before sleeping:
✓ Eat something fatty and drink hot water — the body needs "fuel" to make heat through the night
✓ Go to the toilet first (with a full bladder the body uses extra heat keeping the urine warm)
✓ Do a few exercises on the spot to warm up before getting into the sleeping bag, but do not sweat
✓ Change into dry socks and base layer; do not sleep in wet clothes
✓ Wrap a bottle of hot water in a sock and put it in the sleeping bag (check the lid is screwed tight)

In the sleeping bag:
✓ Keep your hat on; cover everything except your mouth and nose
✓ Do not bury your head inside the sleeping bag to breathe — the water vapour you breathe out makes the bag damper and damper
✓ Put the glove liners and socks you will need tomorrow, water bottle (upside down, so the neck does not freeze first), batteries and phone in the sleeping bag
✓ With several people, sleep huddled together for warmth

If you wake up cold at night: eat something, do a few small exercises such as sit-ups, then sleep again
```

## Water

You become dehydrated in the cold too: cold air is very dry and breathing carries away a lot of water vapour; the cold makes you not want to drink, and makes you pass more urine. Dehydration thickens the blood, making hands and feet more prone to frostbite and you more prone to hypothermia.

```
- Drink at least about 3-4 L a day, more for heavy work
- How to tell: pale yellow urine means enough; dark yellow and little means you are short of water
- Keep the water bottle in your rucksack or close to your body, or wrap it in socks or clothing; store it upside down so the neck does not freeze
- In the evening, boil the next day's water and put it in a vacuum flask
```

### Melting snow

**⚠️ Do not eat snow directly!**
```
Why:
- It lowers body temperature (dangerous)
- It uses energy
  Melting 1 kg of snow at 0°C and warming it to body temperature uses about 490 kJ (about 120 kcal),
  as if all the energy of a small biscuit were used just to "warm the snow"; the colder the snow, the more it uses
- Snow is very fluffy; a big handful of snow gives only a small mouthful of water, but chills your mouth and makes you thirstier
- Acceptable only when your body temperature is normal, you are active and warm, and you take just a little to wet your mouth

The right way:
✓ Melt it and then drink
✓ Boil it (warmer)
✓ Or melt it with body heat (bottle against your body, slow)
  - Leave a little water in the bottle, add snow, shake, and keep it inside your jacket close to your body (not directly against skin)
  - Good only for small top-ups; it cannot cover a day's needs
```

**Snow-melting tips**:
```
✓ Put in a little water first, then add snow (stops the pot burning)
  - Heating snow in a dry pot damages the pot and gives the water a burnt taste; with no water, start with a small handful of snow and slowly melt a base of water
✓ Choose clean white snow
✓ Packed snow (more water than fluffy snow)
  - Freshly fallen powder snow: about 10 L of snow for 1 L of water; packed old snow or granular snow: about 2-3 L for 1 L
✓ Do not use yellow, pink or dirty snow
  - Yellow snow may contain urine, pink snow is algae (can cause diarrhoea), grey or black snow has dust and soot
  - Do not use snow from campsites, toilets, places animals use, or roadsides
✓ Put the lid on, and keep adding snow as it melts; you do not need to wait for it all to melt each time
✓ You can also put snow in a dark cloth bag hung beside the fire or in the sun, with a container underneath to catch the water
```

**Is there enough fuel**:
```
Melting snow uses a lot of fuel: first the snow has to be brought up to 0°C, then melted (this step takes the most heat), then heated
→ To get the same 1 L of hot water, melting snow uses two to three times more fuel than heating water you already have

Rough estimates:
- Gas stove: when snow is your only water source, about 100-200 g of gas per person per day
- Firewood: even one person's drinking water for a day takes a big armful of firewood
- Gas canisters burn weakly in the cold: warm the canister inside your jacket before use;
  liquefied gas vaporises poorly below about 0°C, so in winter choose low-temperature canisters

Saving fuel:
✓ Keep the lid on, shield the stove from wind, and keep the pot bottom close to the flame
✓ If there is running stream water or water from a hole in lake ice, take liquid water first (it only needs disinfecting)
✓ On sunny days, melt snow on a dark plastic sheet or in a black pot in the sun
✓ Boil enough in one go and put it in a vacuum flask; do not boil again and again
```

Snowmelt is mostly clean, but once melted it may carry germs like any other water in the wild: if the snow is not clean or was taken where people and animals are active, boil it for 1 minute after melting (3 minutes above 2000 m). Lake and stream water needs disinfecting even more; for methods see [Basic Survival Skills – Water purification](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#水质净化).

### Ice

```
✓ Ice is better than snow (more water)
  - For the same potful, ice gives several times as much water as snow, and uses less fuel
✓ Choose clean ice
  - Water under the ice of rivers and lakes can also carry germs; disinfect after melting
✓ Melt and drink

Sea ice (polar coasts):
- New sea ice is salty (greyish-white, opaque, sharp-edged); do not drink it
- Old sea ice from a previous year has lost its salt (pale blue, clear, rounded edges); once melted it is basically drinkable
- Taste it: if it is salty, do not use it
```

## Fire

### Fire-lighting challenges

```
Difficulties:
- Wet wood
- Covered by deep snow
- Strong wind
- Tinder hard to find

Solutions:
✓ Find branches on dead trees (dry)
  - Dead branches still hanging on trees, not touching the ground, are driest; ones that snap cleanly are dry, ones that bend without breaking are wet
✓ Split wet wood and use the inner core
✓ Pine resin (excellent fire starter)
✓ Birch bark (waterproof, burns easily)
✓ Shield from the wind
✓ On snow: first lay a platform of wood (stops melting snow putting the fire out)
  - Lay a row of wrist-thick wet or green logs as a base, and build the fire on top; where the snow is deep a platform is essential
✓ Keep matches and lighters in an inside pocket (a cold lighter will not light; warm it in your hand first)
✓ Bring fire starters: cotton balls soaked in petroleum jelly, candle stubs, solid fuel
```

For basic fire-lighting methods and fire structures see [Basic Survival Skills – Fire-lighting](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#生火技术).

```
Note:
- Do not light a fire under trees heavy with snow: rising heat shakes the snow off the branches, and it falls and puts the fire out
- Do not light a fire inside a snow cave or quinzhee (it melts and collapses; carbon monoxide)
- Stand a row of logs or a rock behind the fire as a reflector to throw heat back towards you
- Gather enough wood before lighting the fire: a handful of tinder, a big armful of twigs (from matchstick to finger thickness), and several times that amount of larger wood
```

### Keeping the fire going

```
✓ Have enough fuel ready
  - Winter nights are long; you need far more wood for the night than you think; gather it before dark
✓ Shelter it from wind and snow
✓ Tend it constantly
✓ At night: cover with ash to keep embers alive
✓ Take turns tending the fire with several people, and check on each other (is anyone asleep getting too cold?)
```

## Food

```
In the cold the body needs more energy:
- Normally an adult needs about 2000 kcal a day; outdoor activity in winter often needs 3000-4500 kcal
- Fat gives more than twice as much energy per gram as sugar and starch, and digests slowly, giving lasting heat:
  nuts, fatty meat, butter, chocolate and sesame paste are all good choices
- Little and often: eat something every 1-2 hours; do not wait until you are hungry
- Eat something fatty before sleeping, and you are less likely to wake up cold
- If stranded, first take stock of all the food and share it out in fixed rations
```

### Animals

```
⚠️ China's Wildlife Protection Law prohibits hunting wild animals in normal times,
   and setting traps or snares is illegal in normal circumstances.
   What follows applies only to a genuine life-or-death survival situation; if you can call for help, call for help and wait for rescue first.
   People can live for weeks without food; the first problem when stranded in winter is keeping warm, not hunting.
```

**What can be hunted**:
```
- Rabbits/hares, squirrels
- Birds
- Fish (ice fishing)
- Large animals (requires skill)

Methods:
- Traps, snares
- Ice fishing (cut a hole in the ice)
  - Cut holes only in ice that is thick enough (see "Ice" below), and do not make the hole too big
- Follow tracks in the snow
```

**Cook all meat thoroughly!** (against parasites)

```
- Wild animals may carry parasites (such as Trichinella) and infectious diseases; do not eat raw meat or raw blood
- Do not touch the carcasses of animals that died of disease or of unknown cause
- Eating only very lean meat such as rabbit without fat leads to nausea, diarrhoea and weakness ("rabbit starvation");
  eat fat and plant foods as well
- Polar regions: the livers of polar bears, seals and similar animals contain extremely high levels of vitamin A and are poisonous; do not eat them
```

### Note

```
⚠️ Food needs water to digest
- Eating too much causes dehydration
- Balance water and food
- High-protein meat in particular needs a lot of water to digest and get rid of; when water is short, eat less meat and more starch and sugar
```

## Navigation

### Easy to get lost in the white

```
Prevention:
✓ Compass/GPS
✓ Map
✓ Leave markers (flags, snow piles)
✓ Do not wander when visibility is poor
✓ Stop in a blizzard
✓ Stay where you are (if lost)
```

**Whiteout** (everything white):
```
When it is overcast with snow or fog, the sky, the snowfield and distant mountains are all the same white;
you cannot see the horizon, cannot see the rise and fall of the ground, and cannot judge distance —
people walk off cliffs, fall into gullies, or think they are walking straight when they are going in circles.

What to do:
1. The best option: stop, shelter from the wind where you are and wait; whiteouts often clear within a few hours
2. If you must move:
   - Walk on a compass bearing, not by feel
   - Rope the group together a few m apart; the person in front walks, the person behind watches the compass and corrects the direction
   - Throw snowballs or dark objects (such as a glove) ahead and see where they land, to tell whether the ground ahead is flat, downhill or a drop
   - Probe ahead with a trekking pole or long stick
   - Leave a marker every so often, so you can go back if you get lost
3. Keep away from the edges of ridges: cornices on ridges overhang empty space and look like solid ground, but collapse when you step on them
```

For what to do when lost and how to tell rescuers where you are, see [Realising you are lost](../../../docs/03-野外生存/07-户外出行准备与求救.md#发现自己迷路了) and [Calling for help: say clearly where you are](../../../docs/03-野外生存/07-户外出行准备与求救.md#报警求救-说清楚你在哪里).

### Travelling on snow

```
Speed and effort:
- Walking in deep snow without snowshoes, every step sinks to the knee or even the thigh, which is extremely tiring;
  you may cover only a few km a day
- Snowshoes or skis greatly improve speed and reduce effort;
  without them, tie branches or boards to the soles of your shoes as improvised snowshoes (bigger area, so your feet do not sink)
- When several people travel, walk in single file, stepping in the footprints of the person in front;
  the person breaking trail works hardest; swap over every 10-15 minutes

Time:
- Winter days are short; start preparing for the night by three or four in the afternoon
- Set a "turn-around time" before setting off: if you have not reached your destination by then, go back the way you came

Route:
✓ Choose woodland, gentle slopes and wider parts of ridges
✗ Avoid steep slopes, the area below cornices, and gullies (avalanche paths)
✗ Avoid frozen rivers and lakes (unless you are sure the ice is thick enough)
✗ Never go onto a glacier alone: crevasses on glaciers are hidden by snow bridges, and falling in is extremely dangerous;
  it needs a rope team and specialist training

Tree wells: the snow around big trees is loose with a hollow underneath;
a person who falls in head first and gets stuck can suffocate — in deep snow do not walk right next to big trees
```

For how to set a "turn-around time" see [Trip Preparation and Calling for Help – Turn-around time](../../../docs/03-野外生存/07-户外出行准备与求救.md#掉头时间).

### Blizzards

```
If one hits:
1. Find or build shelter at once
   - Do not wait until the wind and snow are so bad you cannot see; start as soon as there are signs of a blizzard (sky darkening, wind suddenly rising,
     clouds coming down low)
2. Snow cave, snow wall
3. Wait for it to pass
   - A blizzard can last a day or two; ration food and fuel
4. Do not keep going (risk of getting lost)
5. Keep warm
6. Clear snow from the entrance and ventilation hole regularly
7. If you have a phone, report your position first, then save battery and switch on at set times
```

For signs of changing weather see [Weather Observation and Forecasting](../../../docs/08-基础科学知识/07-天气观测与预报.md).

## Dangers

### Hypothermia

**The biggest killer!**

See [Acute Symptoms – Hypothermia](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/05-急性症状.md#失温症)

For recognising and treating hypothermia in the wild see [River Crossing, Flash Floods and Hypothermia – Hypothermia in the wild](../../../docs/03-野外生存/08-涉水山洪与野外失温.md#不只是冬天-野外失温); for falling into cold water see [Falling into cold water](../../../docs/03-野外生存/08-涉水山洪与野外失温.md#落入冷水).

```
Key signs:
- Early: constant shivering, clumsy fingers, stumbling, slurred speech, low mood or irritability
- Dangerous: shivering stops but the person is more confused, cannot be woken, or strangely starts taking clothes off
- People with hypothermia often do not realise it themselves; companions must watch each other

Key steps on snow:
1. Stop, and get into a shelter or out of the wind
2. Change wet clothes for dry ones; insulate from the ground; wrap them from head to toe in a sleeping bag, blankets or an emergency blanket
3. If alert and able to swallow, give warm sweet drinks and food
4. Wrap hot-water bottles in clothing and place them on the chest, in the armpits and at the groin
5. If severe (not shivering, confused): call for help at once; lay them flat and handle them gently;
   do not rub their hands and feet, do not make them walk, do not warm them by a fire or in hot water
❌ No alcohol
```

**Prevention**:
```
✓ Layers for warmth
✓ Stay dry
✓ Avoid sweating
✓ Plenty of food (fuel)
✓ Shelter
✓ Keep moving (but not too much)
✓ Drink enough
✓ Watch each other; if someone falls behind or goes quiet, stop and check
```

### Frostbite

When frostbite and hypothermia occur together, **treat the hypothermia first (to save life), then the frostbite**. For degrees of frostbite, rewarming and care after rewarming in detail, see [Wound and Injury Care – Frostbite](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/02-外伤处理.md#冻伤).

**Symptoms**:
```
Superficial:
- Skin white, numb
- Tingling

Deep:
- Skin black
- No sensation
- Tissue death

Further notes:
- When first frozen, all degrees look much the same: pale, waxy, hard to the touch, no sensation
- How deep frostbite really goes only becomes clear 1-2 days after rewarming (clear blisters or blood blisters, turning purple or black)
- The most common is "frostnip": white patches on the tip of the nose, ears, cheeks or fingers that feel cold and numb;
  at this stage nothing is frozen yet, and warming brings full recovery
```

**Prevention**:
```
✓ Cover exposed areas
✓ Keep the circulation going (move fingers and toes)
✓ Nothing too tight (gloves, shoes)
✓ Stay dry
✓ Deal with numbness at once
✓ Do not touch metal or petrol below freezing with bare hands (petrol evaporates fast, carries heat away quickly, and causes instant frostbite)
✓ Eat well, drink enough, no alcohol, no smoking
✓ If cold feet do not warm up, stop, take your shoes off to check, and change into dry socks
```

**Treatment**:
```
Frostnip (white patches, numbness, can still move):
- Put a frozen hand in the opposite armpit or on the belly; put frozen feet in a companion's armpit or on their belly
- Cover cheeks, ears and nose with your hands; do not rub
- If colour returns and there is tingling after a few minutes, you have done it right

Frostbite that has already frozen hard:
✓ Move to a warm place
✓ Rewarm in warm water (37-39°C)
  - The helper tests the water with the elbow or inner wrist: "warm, and you could keep it in there indefinitely" is right;
    the casualty's frostbitten part has no feeling, so they cannot test it themselves
  - Soak until the skin softens and the colour returns, usually about 30 minutes; keep adding hot water to keep the temperature up
    (take the limb out before adding hot water)
  - Rewarming is very painful; ibuprofen or a similar painkiller can be taken as directed on the leaflet
✓ After rewarming, wrap loosely in dry gauze, with gauze between fingers and toes; raise the limb; do not burst blisters
✓ Do not:
  ❌ Rub
  ❌ Use hot water or a fire
  ❌ Let it refreeze after rewarming (more dangerous)
  ❌ Rub with snow (a wrong old method)
✓ See a doctor

⚠️ When to rewarm: only rewarm if you are sure it will not freeze again afterwards
- If you are still out in the snow and must keep walking to get out, it is better not to rewarm frozen feet yet:
  frozen feet can still just about walk, rewarmed feet are swollen, painful and cannot walk, and refreezing causes worse damage
- Rewarm once you reach somewhere you can stay warm (camp, vehicle, building, hospital)
- If you can get to hospital quickly, wrap the part up to keep it warm and let the hospital rewarm it
```

```
Must see a doctor as soon as possible:
- Blisters (especially blood blisters), skin turning purple or black, or still no sensation after rewarming
- Frostbite over a large area, or frostbite of the hands, feet or face
- Hypothermia at the same time
- Frostbite wounds carry a risk of tetanus and need a medical assessment
```

For non-freezing cold injuries (chilblains, immersion foot from wearing wet shoes and socks for a long time) see [Chilblains and immersion foot](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/02-外伤处理.md#冻疮与浸渍足不是冻伤).

### Snow blindness

**Cause**: snow reflects UV

```
Fresh snow reflects more than 80% of ultraviolet light back, and at high altitude UV is already strong,
so the eyes are burnt from above and below at once — in effect the cornea gets "sunburnt".
It does not hurt while it happens; it usually comes on several hours later (often in the evening).
```

**Symptoms**:
```
- Eye pain
- Watering eyes
- Blurred vision
- Sensitivity to light
- A gritty feeling like sand in the eyes, red swollen eyelids, unable to open the eyes
```

**Prevention**:
```
✓ Sunglasses/goggles
  - Must block UV (marked UV400 or UV protection), ideally with side shields
  - Ordinary tinted glasses do not block UV, and make the pupils dilate, which harms the eyes more
✓ Or improvise:
  - A strip of cloth with slits
  - A piece of wood with narrow slits carved in
  - Cardboard or bark also works: wide enough to cover both eyes, with two slits 1-2 mm wide, tied behind the head with string
  - Blackening under the eyes and on the cheekbones (with charcoal ash) reduces glare
✓ Needed on cloudy days too!
  - Clouds do not block most UV; you can get snow blindness on cloudy and foggy days as well
✓ Someone who has lost their glasses should walk in the line looking down at the back of the person in front, not at the snow
```

**Treatment**:
```
- Rest away from light
- Cold compresses
- Usually recovers in 24-48 hours

How to do it:
- Close the eyes and cover them with a clean damp cloth, or stay somewhere dark; keep wearing glasses if possible
- Remove contact lenses
- Do not rub the eyes
- If pain is marked, ibuprofen or paracetamol (acetaminophen) can be taken as directed on the leaflet
- Avoid walking on snow until recovered; if you must walk, wear goggles and have a companion lead the way

⚠️ See a doctor: no improvement after more than 2 days, clearly reduced vision, pus from the eye, or severe pain in only one eye
```

### Avalanches

```
The great majority of avalanches are triggered by the weight of the victims themselves or their companions.
The most effective way to stay alive is not to enter avalanche terrain — surviving burial depends on luck and companions.
```

**Recognising danger**:
```
- Slopes of 30-45°
  - Most often on slopes of about 35-45°; snow does not settle on steeper slopes and often keeps sliding off in small amounts
  - Estimating slope: make a right angle with two trekking poles of equal length, one vertical and one horizontal,
    or look at the slope: a slope where standing on it you feel you "need a hand to steady yourself" is already over 30°
  - People below a slope are in danger too: an avalanche can come down from steep slopes above and run a long way
- After fresh snowfall
  - 20-30 cm or more of snow in one day; the following day or two are most dangerous
- Sudden warming
  - Spring midday, after rain, slopes in strong sun
- Slopes with no vegetation
  - Few trees, snapped-off trees, piles of broken snow and branches at the bottom of the slope → avalanches have happened there before
- "Whumpf" sounds
  - A dull "whumpf" when you step on the snow, or cracks shooting across the snow surface → the snowpack is unstable; leave at once
- Downwind slopes: strong winds pile snow up into thick slabs that break off especially easily
- Recent avalanches nearby
```

**Avoiding them**:
```
✓ Avoid dangerous slopes
✓ Know your route
  - Before setting off, check the local avalanche forecast and ask local people and the authorities
✓ Mornings (snow frozen) are safer
  - Especially in spring; after midday the risk of wet-snow avalanches rises sharply
✓ Cross one at a time (in a group)
  - While one person crosses, the others watch from a safe place, so they can remember the spot at once if the person is buried
✓ Keep to ridges, gentle slopes and dense woodland; when following a valley floor, keep away from the steep slopes on either side
✓ Do not rest or camp in gullies or below steep slopes
✓ Undo your rucksack hip belt and chest strap, and do not use the wrist straps on your poles, so you can throw them off if caught
```

**Equipment** (when entering avalanche-prone areas, each person must carry it, and must have practised with it):
```
- Avalanche transceiver (beacon): switch to transmit when setting off, and check everyone's is working
- Probe: a folding long pole for pushing into the snow to find people
- Snow shovel: buried avalanche snow quickly sets like concrete, and you can hardly dig it with your hands
Carrying only one is the same as carrying none: the transceiver finds the rough position, the probe pinpoints the position and depth, and only the shovel can dig the person out
```

**If you are buried**:
```
1. Swim hard upwards
   - When the avalanche starts, shout to warn your companions, and try to run sideways and get out of its edge
   - If caught, throw off ski poles and rucksack, grab trees or rocks, and make swimming movements with arms and legs to stay as near the surface as possible
2. Before the avalanche stops:
   - One hand protecting your face (to make an air space)
   - One hand reaching upwards (to mark your position)
   - The snow sets as soon as it stops, so do these two things at the very last moment before it stops
3. After it stops:
   - Enlarge the space
   - Dig upwards
     - If you can move, dig; if you cannot tell which way is up, let some spit run out of your mouth and see which way it runs; up is the opposite way
     - If buried deep, the snow will set too hard to dig; do not waste your strength
   - Save oxygen
     - Stay calm, breathe slowly; shout only when you hear people above you
   - Wait for rescue
```

**When a companion is buried (searching)**:
```
Time is life:
- Dug out within about 18 minutes, roughly nine in ten survive; by about 35 minutes the survival rate has fallen to about one in three (most die of suffocation)
- Outside rescue teams often take over an hour to arrive — companions on the spot are the buried person's best hope
→ Search and rescue yourselves first, while sending one person (or using a phone) to raise the alarm; do not let everyone leave to fetch help

Steps:
1. Make sure it is safe first: is there a risk of a second avalanche from above? Everyone switches their transceiver to search mode
2. Remember where the buried person was last seen, and search downwards from there in the direction the avalanche flowed
3. Look first: hands, feet, clothing or equipment showing on the snow surface
4. Search with transceivers; when close, probe in a spiral outwards at about 25 cm intervals;
   when you hit the person, leave the probe in place as a marker
5. Start digging below the probe (on the downhill side) at about 1-1.5 times the burial depth, not straight down from above
   (digging from directly above tramples the buried person's air space); with several people, line up and take turns digging, passing snow back
6. Uncover the head first and clear the mouth and nose; if they are not breathing, start CPR
7. Once out, prevent hypothermia, treat as for hypothermia, and get them to hospital

Without transceivers: stand in a line and advance step by step, pushing probes or long sticks into the snow, with probe points about 50 cm apart front-to-back and side-to-side;
if one pass finds nothing, offset the positions and probe again
```

For CPR see [CPR](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/00-基础急救知识.md#心肺复苏cpr); for moving casualties see [Wilderness Casualty Care and Evacuation – Moving a casualty](../../../docs/03-野外生存/11-野外伤病处置与转运.md#搬运伤员).

### Ice and falling through

Every winter people drown after falling through ice on rivers, lakes and reservoirs. Ice may look solid, but its thickness varies a great deal.

**Ice thickness guide** (for new, clear, blue-green solid ice):

| Ice thickness | What it can bear |
|------|------|
| Under 15 cm | Stay off |
| About 15 cm | One person, or several people walking spread out |
| About 20 cm | A group of people (such as a crowd of skaters) |
| About 25 cm | Snowmobiles, all-terrain vehicles; never drive an ordinary car onto ice |

```
⚠️ The table is only a guide and cannot guarantee safety:
- White, opaque ice with bubbles is only about half as strong as clear ice; double the thickness
- Ice is thinner in these places: rivers (water flows under the ice), inlets and outlets, near bridge piers,
  around reeds and branches, where there are hot springs or drain outfalls, and on snow-covered ice (snow acts like a quilt and stops the ice thickening)
- In the thaw season (early spring), when temperatures stay above 0°C for days, or when the ice is grey or dark with water on it → stay off the ice
- If the ice cracks or makes a "crack-crack" sound → lie down at once and go back the way you came

When going onto ice:
✓ First cut a hole near the bank with an axe or ice chisel to measure the thickness, and measure again every so often as you go
✓ With several people, spread out at least a few m apart; do not bunch together
✓ Carry a long pole held across your chest (if you fall in, it lies across the hole and holds you up), and carry a rope
✓ Hang a pair of ice picks on your chest: two wooden handles with spikes, joined by a cord; if you fall in, drive them into the ice and pull yourself out
  (if you have none, use two sharpened sticks or two screwdrivers)
✓ Undo your rucksack hip belt
✓ Better to take the long way round along the shore
```

**Getting yourself out after falling through**:
```
1. First minute: cold water makes you gasp uncontrollably ("cold shock")
   → Do not panic and thrash; keep your head above water, control your breathing and wait for this to pass
2. Turn to face the way you came — the ice you just walked over held you, so it is the strongest
3. Put both arms up on the ice and drive the ice picks into it (or press down with your elbows or palms)
4. Kick hard with your legs behind you to bring your body as horizontal as possible, like a seal climbing out;
   kick and pull forwards with your hands at the same time, slowly pushing your body up onto the ice
5. Once on the ice, do not stand up: roll away from the hole, or crawl back on your front,
   spreading your weight over a large area, until you are back on thick ice or the shore
6. You have about 10 minutes when your arms and legs still have strength; use it; if you cannot climb out,
   rest your arms on the ice and keep still, letting your clothes freeze to the ice so you can hold on longer, and wait for rescue
7. Once out, get out of the wind at once, take off wet clothes, change into dry ones, and treat as for hypothermia
```

**Rescuing someone who has fallen through**:
```
❌ Do not walk straight out to pull them — the ice around the hole is already cracked, and the rescuer will fall in too
1. Shout to them: "Turn to face me, kick your legs, crawl out slowly"
2. Call for help first (110, 119)
3. Reach: lie on the ice (or stay on the bank) and hold out a long pole, branch, scarf or belt
4. Throw: a rope (with a loop tied in one end), a life ring, or anything that floats
5. If you must get close: lie flat to spread your weight, push a ladder, plank or long pole ahead of you, with someone behind holding your feet or a rope
6. Once out, drag them away from the hole lying flat; once on shore, prevent hypothermia; anyone who inhaled water or is not fully conscious goes to hospital
```

For the "1-10-1" rule for falling into cold water see [Falling into cold water](../../../docs/03-野外生存/08-涉水山洪与野外失温.md#落入冷水); for drowning first aid see [Drowning](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/05-急性症状.md#溺水).

## Signals

```
On a white background:
✓ Dark markers (SOS, X)
✓ Smoke (day)
✓ Firelight (night)
✓ Mirror flashes
✓ Brightly coloured clothing
✓ Trample out large letters

How to do it:
- On open snow, trample or dig out large letters, each at least 3-5 m long; tread the trenches deep
  so the shadows show; filling the trenches with branches or dark clothing makes them stand out more
- Wind and snow will fill the letters in; tread them out again regularly
- Daytime smoke: add pine branches and green leaves to the fire for thick white smoke;
  white smoke does not stand out against snow, so add rubber, plastic or oil to make black smoke
- Prepare three piles of firewood in a triangle, and light them when you hear an aircraft
- Phone: in the cold the battery drains very fast; keep it warm against your body, switch it off to save power, and switch on at set times to check for signal
```

For the meaning of ground-to-air signals see [Basic Survival Skills – Ground-to-air signals](../../../docs/03-野外生存/00-基础生存技能.md#地面对空信号); for waiting for helicopter rescue see [Wilderness Casualty Care and Evacuation – Helicopter rescue](../../../docs/03-野外生存/11-野外伤病处置与转运.md#直升机救援).

## 📝 Snow survival key points

```
Priorities:
1. Keep warm (hypothermia kills)
2. Shelter (snow cave)
3. Water (melting snow)
4. Fire (warmth, melting snow, signals)
5. Food

Key points:
✓ Stay dry!
✓ Dress in layers
✓ Sweat less: take a layer off before you get hot, put it straight back on when you stop
✓ Snow-cave shelter
✓ Keep the ventilation hole clear; never burn charcoal in a snow cave or tent
✓ Insulate from the ground when sleeping
✓ Do not eat snow (melt it, then drink)
✓ Frostbite: rewarming water 37-39°C; do not rewarm if it may refreeze
✓ Snow blindness: wear goggles even on cloudy days

Dangers:
⚠️ Hypothermia (#1 killer)
⚠️ Frostbite
⚠️ Avalanches: avoid 30-45° slopes; carry transceiver, probe and shovel
⚠️ Snow blindness
⚠️ Getting lost: in a whiteout, stop and wait
⚠️ Ice: stay off if under 15 cm; if you fall in, turn round, kick, crawl out, roll away
⚠️ Carbon monoxide and suffocation
```
