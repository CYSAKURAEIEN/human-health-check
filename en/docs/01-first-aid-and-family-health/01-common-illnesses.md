# A Guide to Common Illnesses

> ⚠️ This chapter covers home care for common minor illnesses and "when you must go to hospital". If you can reach a doctor, ask the doctor first or call 120; this chapter cannot replace a diagnosis. Medicine doses give only the usual principles for common over-the-counter medicines - **always follow the package leaflet and your doctor's instructions**. Infants, older people, pregnant women and people with chronic illnesses can get worse quickly, so set a lower threshold for seeing a doctor.

## Colds

### Recognising the Symptoms

**Common cold**:
- Blocked or runny nose
- Sneezing
- Mild sore throat
- Mild cough
- Usually no fever, or a low fever (<38°C)

**Flu (influenza)**:
- Sudden high fever (>38.5°C)
- Aching muscles all over
- Severe exhaustion
- Headache
- Dry cough

**How to tell them apart**:

| | Common cold | Flu |
|------|----------|------|
| Onset | Slow, getting worse over a day or two | Sudden, feeling very ill within hours |
| Main symptoms | Nose and throat | Whole body: high fever, aching muscles, exhaustion |
| Course | Usually gets better by itself in 7-10 days | Fever for 3-5 days; tiredness can last 1-2 weeks |
| Complications | Few | Pneumonia, myocarditis and others; can be fatal in high-risk groups |
| Infectious period | The first few days, when symptoms are worst | From 1 day before falling ill to about 1 week after |

```
Key points:
- Colds and flu are both viral infections; most people get better within 1-2 weeks through their own immunity
- The aim of treatment is to "get through it comfortably" and "spot complications early", not to "cure" the illness
- Nasal mucus turning from clear to yellow-green is a normal part of the illness and does not mean a bacterial infection
```

### Home Care

#### 1. Rest and Fluids
```
✓ Plenty of sleep (8 hours or more a day)
✓ Drink plenty of warm water (2-3 L a day)
✓ Warm drinks (honey and lemon, ginger tea)
✓ Avoid dehydration

How to tell you are drinking enough:
- Pale yellow urine, passing urine every 3-4 hours during the day → enough
- Little urine, dark yellow → not drinking enough
- People with heart failure or kidney disease who must limit fluids: drink the amount your doctor set; do not just drink more

Rest:
- While you have a fever, stay in bed or rest at home; do not go to work or school (both to recover and to avoid infecting others)
- Return to normal activities only after the fever has been gone for 24 hours or more and you feel better
- With a fever, do not push yourself to go running or play ball games (strenuous exercise during a viral infection can trigger myocarditis)
```

#### 2. Relieving Symptoms
- **Blocked nose**:
  - Steam inhalation (10-15 minutes)
    - How: put a bowl of water just off the boil on a table, keep your face about 30 cm above the water, cover your head with a towel, close your eyes and breathe in the steam
    - ⚠️ Do not use a bowl of hot water for children (knocking it over and getting scalded is common); instead, sit with the child for a while in the bathroom while a hot shower is running
  - Saline nasal spray
    - You can also use 0.9% salt water (1 L of cooled boiled water + 9 g of salt) as nose drops or a rinse. Water for rinsing must be boiled and then cooled - never use unboiled water. For how to make it, see [Disinfectants - Saline](../../../docs/06-制药技术/01-消毒剂制备.md#七生理盐水)
    - When a blocked nose stops an infant feeding: put 1-2 drops of salt water in each nostril, wait a minute or two, then gently suck it out with a nasal aspirator
  - Sleep with your head raised on extra pillows

- **Sore throat**:
  - Gargle with warm salt water (half a teaspoon of salt + warm water)
    - Dissolve half a level teaspoon of salt (about 3 g) in a glass (about 250 mL) of warm water, gargle and spit it out, several times a day
    - Suitable for adults and older children who can learn to gargle without swallowing
  - Honey (over 1 year old)
  - Throat lozenges (do not give hard sweets to children under 3 - they can choke)

- **Cough**:
  - Honey (a spoonful before bed)
  - Keep the air moist
  - Avoid irritating smells
  - For details, see [Cough](#cough) in this chapter

#### 3. Medicines (Not Essential)
- For fever and pain: paracetamol (acetaminophen), ibuprofen (for doses, see [Fever](#fever) in this chapter)
- Decongestants: relieve a blocked nose
  - Pseudoephedrine by mouth (contained in many combination cold remedies): use with caution if you have high blood pressure, heart disease, an overactive thyroid, glaucoma or an enlarged prostate
  - Nasal sprays (oxymetazoline, xylometazoline and others): generally use for no more than 3-5 days in a row (follow the leaflet; the shorter the better), otherwise the blockage gets worse when you stop
- **Note**: antibiotics do not work against viral colds

```
Traps with combination cold remedies:
⚠️ Many "cold medicines" and "cold granules" already contain paracetamol (look for "paracetamol" or "acetaminophen" on the ingredients list)
   → taking a separate fever medicine on top leads to a paracetamol overdose, and in severe cases liver failure
⚠️ Use only one combination cold remedy at a time; never take two cold medicines together
⚠️ Cold medicines containing chlorphenamine (chlorpheniramine) or diphenhydramine make you drowsy: after taking them do not drive, operate machinery or work at heights
⚠️ Do not give combination cold medicines or cough medicines to children under 6 on your own initiative (benefit uncertain, high risk of overdose);
   manage their symptoms mainly with salt-water nose drops, plenty of fluids and fever medicine
```

### Colds in Children, Older People and Pregnant Women

```
Infants and young children:
- A blocked nose interferes with feeding and sleep: clear the nose before feeds, and feed little and often
- Watch not so much the temperature as: how much they feed, how much urine they pass, how alert they are, their breathing
- Infant under 3 months with a fever → see a doctor straight away (see "Fever" in this chapter)
- Fast breathing, chest sucking in when breathing in, blue lips → see a doctor immediately (see the breathing-rate check under "Cough" in this chapter)

Older people:
- After a cold they easily develop pneumonia, and the symptoms are often atypical: they may have no fever and little cough,
  and only seem listless, lose their appetite, become suddenly confused, unsteady on their feet, or fall
- In people with existing heart disease, COPD or diabetes, a cold often makes that illness worse → watch for breathlessness, swollen legs, swings in blood sugar

Pregnant women:
- With a fever (≥38°C), contact a doctor; paracetamol is the first choice for fever - do not take ibuprofen or aspirin on your own initiative
- Do not take combination cold remedies or Chinese patent medicines on your own initiative; ask a doctor or pharmacist first
- Pregnant women are at high risk of severe flu; see a doctor early if they have a high fever or shortness of breath

High-risk groups for flu (children under 5, especially under 2; adults over 65; pregnant women; people with chronic illnesses; people with obesity):
- With confirmed or suspected flu, see a doctor within 48 hours of falling ill: the doctor may prescribe an antiviral (prescription medicine), which works better the earlier it is started
```

### Prevention

```
✓ Wash your hands often (20 seconds or more)
✓ Avoid touching your face
✓ Keep your distance from others
✓ Wear a mask (in crowded places)
✓ Strengthen your immunity (balanced diet, moderate exercise)
✓ Get a flu vaccine
```

For how to wash your hands, see [Sanitation - How to Wash](../../../docs/07-日常生活技能/03-卫生设施.md#怎么洗); for isolation and disinfection when someone at home has a respiratory infection, see [Infectious Disease at Home](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/15-传染病家庭防控.md#呼吸道传染病).

### ⚠️ When to See a Doctor

See a doctor immediately if there is:
- High fever lasting more than 3 days
- Difficulty breathing
- Chest pain
- Severe headache or stiff neck
- Persistent vomiting
- Confusion
- Sudden worsening of symptoms

Also see a doctor soon if:
- Fever comes back or the cough gets worse after symptoms had improved (possibly a secondary bacterial infection such as pneumonia, middle-ear infection or sinusitis)
- A cold has not started to improve after more than 10 days
- A child has earache, pulls at their ears, or has pus coming from an ear
- Someone in one of the high-risk groups for flu above has flu-like symptoms

## Fever

### Temperature Ranges

**Normal temperature at different sites**:

| Where measured | Normal range | Notes |
|----------|----------|------|
| Armpit | 36.0-37.0°C | Most commonly used in China |
| Mouth | 36.3-37.2°C | About 0.2-0.5°C higher than the armpit |
| Rectum | 36.5-37.7°C | About 0.5°C higher than the armpit; closest to core temperature |
| Ear | Close to rectal temperature | Strongly affected by technique; readings vary a lot |

**Fever grades** (armpit or mouth temperature):

| Low-grade fever | Moderate fever | High fever | Very high fever |
|------|----------|------|--------|
| 37.3-38.0°C | 38.1-39.0°C | 39.1-41.0°C | >41°C |

```
Key points:
- Body temperature varies through the day: afternoon to evening is about 0.5°C higher than early morning, and it is also higher after meals, exercise or crying
- Fever itself is the body's response to fighting infection; an ordinary fever will not "burn out the brain"
- What is really dangerous is the illness causing the fever (meningitis, pneumonia, sepsis and so on),
  so how alert the person is matters more than the number on the thermometer
- The high temperature of heatstroke works differently from fever; fever medicines do not work, and the person must be cooled physically at once (see Acute Symptoms - Heatstroke)
```

For treating heatstroke, see [Acute Symptoms - Heatstroke](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/05-急性症状.md#中暑).

### How to Take a Temperature Correctly

```
Digital thermometer (recommended; first choice at home):
1. Wipe the sweat from the armpit
2. Put the tip in the middle of the armpit and hold the arm tight against the chest
3. After the "beep", keep it in a little longer for a steadier reading
4. After a hot drink, a bath, exercise or crying, rest for 30 minutes before measuring

Mercury thermometer:
1. Shake it down below 35°C before use
2. Hold it in the armpit for 5-10 minutes
3. To read it, hold it horizontally and turn it until you can see the mercury column
4. ⚠️ If it breaks: open the windows; people and pets leave the room. Push the mercury beads together with a piece of stiff card,
   pick them up with a damp wipe or sticky tape and put them in a sealed jar. Do not use a broom or vacuum cleaner (they spread the mercury);
   if a child bites through a thermometer, or swallows mercury or glass → see a doctor

Infants (under 3 months, rectal temperature is best):
1. Put a little petroleum jelly on the tip of a digital thermometer
2. Lay the infant on their back with legs raised (as for a nappy change), or face down across an adult's lap
3. Gently insert the tip about 1-2 cm into the anus; do not force it; hold the thermometer until the reading is done
4. A rectal temperature of ≥38.0°C is a fever

Forehead and ear thermometers:
- Quick to read and good for screening, but strongly affected by room temperature and technique
- If a forehead reading is high or doubtful, check it with an armpit or rectal temperature

Without a thermometer:
- Feeling the forehead with your hand gives only a rough idea and is unreliable
- Signs to go by: shivering, hot and flushed skin, clearly faster breathing and heartbeat, less alert
- As soon as you can borrow a thermometer, measure again and write it down
```

### When to Bring a Fever Down

**Adults**:
- Temperature ≥38.5°C and feeling unwell
- People with underlying illnesses: temperature ≥38°C

**Children**:
- Under 3 months: any fever needs a doctor
- 3-6 months: armpit temperature ≥38.2°C, or clearly uncomfortable because of the fever (paracetamol only; ideally ask a doctor first)
- Over 6 months: armpit temperature ≥38.2°C, or clearly uncomfortable, crying or sleeping badly because of the fever

```
Understanding "when to bring a fever down":
- The point of fever medicine is to make the person more comfortable, able to drink and able to sleep, not to push the temperature back to normal
- If the temperature falls by 1-1.5°C after the medicine and the person feels better, it has worked; it does not have to get down to 37°C
- Fever medicines do not prevent febrile seizures; if the child is sleeping peacefully, there is no need to wake them for a dose
- Infants of 3-6 months can only have paracetamol (ibuprofen is for over 6 months), and preferably only after asking a doctor
```

### Ways to Bring a Fever Down

#### 1. Physical Cooling

**Sponging with lukewarm water**:
```
1. Water temperature: 32-34°C (slightly below body temperature)
2. Where to sponge: neck, armpits, groin, arms and legs
3. Time: 15-20 minutes
4. Never: sponge with alcohol (can cause poisoning)

Note:
- Lukewarm sponging only helps a little; the cooling effect is short-lived and it cannot replace fever medicine
- If the child is shivering or trembling, or cries and is uncomfortable while being sponged → stop; sponging during shivering actually makes the temperature keep rising
- ❌ Do not sponge with cold water or iced water; do not put ice directly on the skin
- Cooling gel patches do very little; do not use them on young infants (they may slip and cover the mouth and nose)
```

**The room**:
- Keep the room at 22-24°C
- Fewer clothes and covers
  - While shivering they can have a light blanket; once the shivering stops and they start to feel hot and sweat, take it off
  - ❌ Do not "sweat it out" under heavy covers - infants and young children wrapped up too warmly can have a sudden rise in temperature, become dehydrated, and their life can even be in danger
- Keep the room ventilated
  - Do not point fans or air conditioning straight at the sick person

**Fluids**:
- Drink 200-300 mL of water an hour
- Good to drink: water, fruit juice, clear soup
- Avoid: drinks containing caffeine
- Infants: keep breastfeeding or giving formula, and feed more often; exclusively breastfed infants under 6 months generally do not need extra water
- With heavy sweating plus diarrhoea or vomiting, switch to oral rehydration salts (ORS) (see [Diarrhoea](#diarrhoea) in this chapter)

#### 2. Fever Medicines

**Paracetamol (acetaminophen)**
- Adult dose: 500-1000 mg, at least 4 hours between doses
- Child dose: 10-15 mg/kg body weight, at least 4 hours between doses
- Maximum: adults 4000 mg a day
- Children: no more than 4 doses in 24 hours; can be used from 3 months
- Chinese over-the-counter leaflets usually say 500 mg per dose and no more than 4 doses in 24 hours (2000 mg a day); follow the leaflet when treating yourself; higher amounts only on a doctor's instructions
- Adults who drink heavily over long periods, have liver disease, are malnourished or weigh <50 kg need a lower daily total; ask a doctor first
- Overdose causes serious liver damage, possibly with no symptoms at all at first (see [Poisoning - Drug Overdose and Accidental Ingestion](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/12-中毒.md#药物过量与误服))

**Ibuprofen**
- Adult dose: 200-400 mg, every 4-6 hours
- Child dose: 5-10 mg/kg body weight, every 6-8 hours
- Maximum: adults 1200 mg a day (over the counter)
- Children: no more than 4 doses in 24 hours; can be used from 6 months
- Do not use on your own initiative if there is: a history of stomach ulcer or stomach bleeding, kidney disease, severe dehydration (with diarrhoea and vomiting and passing almost no urine it damages the kidneys), allergy to aspirin or ibuprofen, use of anticoagulants, pregnancy (especially after 20 weeks)
- In some people with asthma it triggers an asthma attack
- For fever in a child with chickenpox, use paracetamol, not ibuprofen

**Cautions with medicines**:
- ❌ Do not use two fever medicines at the same time
- ❌ No aspirin under 18
- ✓ Take after food to reduce stomach upset

```
Other cautions:
- ❌ "Alternating" two fever medicines is not recommended either: it is easy to get the number of doses and the total wrong
- ❌ Aspirin is forbidden under 18: aspirin given to a child with a viral infection (flu, chickenpox)
  can cause Reye's syndrome (acute brain disease and liver damage, high death rate);
  some combination medicines and "cold granules" contain aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) - read the ingredients list
- ✓ Write down the time and dose every time, especially when several people look after the child, to avoid giving double doses
- ✓ The medicine takes effect after about 30-60 minutes; if the temperature has not come down to normal after one dose,
  do not give more early - wait until the full interval has passed
- ✓ Medicines such as metamizole (dipyrone) have serious side effects; do not give them to children on your own initiative
- ✓ Lock all medicines away out of children's reach (sweet-tasting children's medicines are easily taken secretly)
```

### Fever Medicine Dose Chart

Work out children's doses by **weight**, not age. The table gives the range for each dose (converted from the mg/kg rules above); always follow the leaflet of the product you are using:

| Weight | Paracetamol per dose (10-15 mg/kg) | Ibuprofen per dose (5-10 mg/kg) |
|------|------|------|
| 5 kg | 50-75 mg | Not used (under 6 months) |
| 8 kg | 80-120 mg | 40-80 mg |
| 10 kg | 100-150 mg | 50-100 mg |
| 15 kg | 150-225 mg | 75-150 mg |
| 20 kg | 200-300 mg | 100-200 mg |
| 30 kg | 300-450 mg | 150-300 mg |
| Interval | ≥4 hours, ≤4 doses in 24 hours | ≥6 hours, ≤4 doses in 24 hours |

```
Converting "milligrams (mg)" into "millilitres (mL)" - the step where mistakes are most often made:
1. Read the strength on the bottle, e.g. "100 mg ibuprofen per 5 mL" → 20 mg per 1 mL
2. mL per dose = mg per dose ÷ mg per mL
   Example: a 10 kg child, ibuprofen 100 mg per dose → 100 ÷ 20 = 5 mL
3. ⚠️ For the same medicine, "drops" and "suspension" can differ in strength by a factor of two or more;
   whenever the packaging changes, work it out again - do not carry over the millilitres from the last bottle
4. Use the measuring cup, syringe or dropper that comes with the medicine, not a household spoon (sizes vary)
5. Weight ≥40 kg or over 12 years: a low adult dose can generally be used; follow the leaflet
```

### Caring for Someone with a Fever

#### Keeping a Record
```
Write down:
- Time measured
- Temperature
- Other symptoms
- Medicines given
- How much they drank

Also record:
- Urine (how often, colour) - to tell whether they are dehydrated
- How alert they are (playing, can communicate → good; drowsy, cannot be woken, restless and irritable → bad)
- How much they fed or ate

How often: during a fever, about every 4 hours; check again 30-60 minutes after a fever medicine
If a child is sleeping peacefully at night, there is no need to wake them to take a temperature
Take this record with you to the doctor - it makes the doctor's assessment much more accurate
```

#### Food
- **Good**: light, easily digested food
  - Thin rice porridge, noodles
  - Fresh fruit
  - Vegetable soup
- **Avoid**:
  - Greasy food
  - Spicy, irritating food
  - Raw or cold food
- Poor appetite during a fever is normal, and eating little for a day or two does not matter much. **Drinking enough matters more than eating enough**; do not force food on them

### Common Mistakes in Fever Care

```
❌ "Sweat it out under the covers" → infants and young children can overheat suddenly, become dehydrated and have seizures
❌ Rubbing down with alcohol or iced water → alcohol is absorbed through the skin and poisons; iced water causes shivering, which raises the temperature
❌ Demanding an injection, a drip or antibiotics for every fever → most fevers are viral and antibiotics do not work;
   overusing antibiotics breeds resistance, and then they fail when they are really needed
❌ Giving another or a bigger dose early because the temperature is not yet normal → overdose
❌ Alternating or combining two fever medicines → easy to get the total wrong
❌ Watching only the number → a child at 39°C who is lively and drinking is less worrying than a listless child at 38°C
```

### When to See a Doctor for Fever, by Group

| Group | Go now (emergency) if | See a doctor the same day or soon if |
|------|------|------|
| Infants under 3 months | Any fever (rectal ≥38.0°C); or temperature too low (<36°C), feeding poorly, drowsy | — |
| 3-6 months | Listless, drowsy, fast breathing, not feeding, seizures | Temperature ≥39°C; any fever lasting more than 24 hours |
| 6 months to 5 years | Drowsy and cannot be woken, laboured breathing, a rash that does not fade, seizures, bulging soft spot on the head, no urine for 6 hours or more | Fever for more than 3 days; fever with earache, pain passing urine, or frequent vomiting |
| Older children and adults | Temperature ≥40°C; with stiff neck, severe headache, change in consciousness, seizures, difficulty breathing, chest pain | Fever for more than 3 days; fever coming back after improving |
| Older people (over 65) | Change in consciousness, fast breathing, low blood pressure, passing little urine | Temperature more than 1°C above their usual, or not high but suddenly listless, not eating, falling |
| Pregnant women | Fever with abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding or leaking fluid, fewer baby movements | Any fever ≥38°C: contact the obstetrician |
| People with weak immunity (on chemotherapy, long-term steroids, after an organ transplant) | Any fever ≥38°C | — |

```
More notes:
- Older people react weakly with temperature; with a serious infection they may only reach about 37.5°C or have no fever at all,
  so an older person who "isn't right" is a reason to see a doctor in itself
- A high fever in a pregnant woman also affects the baby; do not just put up with it - paracetamol is the first choice
- A child having a seizure with fever (febrile seizure): the first one must always be seen by a doctor
```

For on-the-spot care of febrile seizures, see [Heart, Stroke and Sudden Illness - Febrile Seizures in Children](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/06-心脑血管与突发病症.md#儿童高热惊厥); for other danger signs in pregnancy, see [Emergency Childbirth and Newborn Care - Danger Signs in Pregnancy](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/11-紧急分娩与新生儿护理.md#孕期危险信号); for danger signs in newborns, see [Emergency Childbirth and Newborn Care - The Newborn's First Few Days](../../../docs/01-日常疾病和伤害/11-紧急分娩与新生儿护理.md#新生儿最初几天).

### ⚠️ Emergency Care

See a doctor immediately in these cases:
- Any fever in an infant (<3 months)
- Temperature ≥40°C
- Fever with:
  - Severe headache
  - Stiff neck
  - Change in consciousness
  - Seizures
  - Difficulty breathing
  - Chest pain
  - Severe abdominal pain
  - Rash (especially one that does not fade)
  - Persistent vomiting
- Fever for more than 3 days without improvement

```
The "glass test" - does the rash fade?
1. Take a clear drinking glass and press its side firmly against the rash
2. Look through the glass: the rash disappears and goes pale under pressure → it fades
3. You can still clearly see red dots or purple patches while pressing → it does not fade → possibly meningococcal meningitis or sepsis
   → call 120 immediately; do not wait for other symptoms (these illnesses can threaten life within hours)
```
